The part of a connected toy that fails first is almost never the toy. It is the tablet that no longer runs the operating system, the account nobody remembers the password for, or the parent who has run out of patience for a pairing process that has to be repeated every single time the battery goes flat.

That does not make app-connected toys a bad category. Some of them do something a purely physical toy cannot do at all, and a few of them survive the death of their app without any trouble. The trick is knowing which kind you are buying before it arrives, because the box will not tell you.

## What "connected" actually means

There are three quite different products sharing one label.

The first is a toy that treats a screen as an optional extra. A drawing tablet that also works on paper, a robot with a remote as well as an app. Pull the app and the toy still does most of its job.

The second is a toy that requires a device to do anything at all. No phone, no play. These are the ones that become plastic waste the moment the app stops being updated.

The third is not really a toy in the usual sense. It is a device your child uses with a toy-shaped body around it, whether that is a camera, a watch or a talking assistant. Judge it as a device rather than as a toy, and the decision gets considerably easier.

## The device is part of the toy's lifespan

Ask one question in the aisle: what does this do with the app switched off?

If the answer is nothing, then the product's real lifespan is the shorter of two things, the manufacturer's support for the app or your household's tolerance for maintaining it. Phones get replaced, operating systems move on, and a toy that needs a specific app version is on a clock from the day it is unboxed.

If the answer is most of it, you have bought a toy with a feature rather than a subscription with a plastic handle. That is usually the better purchase, and over the years it tends to be the calmer one as well.

## What a screen genuinely adds

It is worth being fair to the category, because a few things here genuinely work better than the physical version.

Immediate feedback is the big one. A child learning to put a sequence of movements together can see what their instruction actually did, undo it, and try again without an adult resetting anything. A drawing toy can correct a shaky line instead of leaving it on the page. A camera can make a photo visible without a trip to a shop.

Feedback loops like that keep a child at a task longer than they would stay without them. That is a real advantage, and it is the reason a small number of these toys earn a permanent place on the shelf.

## Where it goes wrong

The failure modes repeat across the whole category.

The toy does the interesting part. If the app animates, sounds and narrates while the child taps, the child is operating a screen through a plastic intermediary. The physical object is a controller wearing a toy costume.

The play requires a charged device. A toy that only works when a tablet is above a certain battery level will be used less than one that works on the floor.

The setup is long. Toys that need pairing, an account, a firmware update and an adult every time are toys nobody reaches for on a rainy Tuesday.

The play is single-player by design. The connected toys that last tend to be the ones with a physical, social mode that does not need the device at all. Those are the ones that come out when a friend arrives.

The content dries up. Some apps are supported for years and some are not, and you cannot tell which from the packaging. That is an argument for buying the version of a product that still works without one.

## What the AAP guidance actually says

The American Academy of Pediatrics publishes age-specific screen recommendations, and they are useful even if you never buy a connected toy.

- Under 18 months: avoid screens other than video chat.
- 18 to 24 months: only high-quality content, and co-viewed with an adult.
- 2 to 5 years: no more than one hour a day of high-quality programming.
- 6 years and older: no fixed hour limit, but consistent limits on time, type and place, plus a family media plan made with the child.

A connected toy sits inside that guidance. It is screen time, whatever the packaging calls it, and the same household rules apply to it.

## Rules about balance beat rules about hours

This is the part most families get backwards. The evidence suggests that household rules about balance, content, co-viewing and communication are associated with better outcomes than rules that only limit hours.

So the useful questions are not how many minutes, but what the screen is for, where it is used and who is in the room. The AAP also recommends screen-free zones, such as bedrooms and the dining table, and screen-free times, such as the hour before bed and mealtimes.

On the risk side, the evidence is uneven and worth stating honestly. The association between more screen time and obesity or depressive symptoms is moderately strong. The evidence for behaviour problems, anxiety, attention and sleep is weaker. Some research links four or more hours a day to higher risk of anxiety, depression, behaviour problems and ADHD, particularly when screens displace sleep and physical activity. None of that is a reason to ban a toy. It is a reason to notice when a toy has quietly started eating the hours that used to hold sleep and running around.

## Three questions before you buy

Run the product against these and most of the decision makes itself.

What happens when the device is unavailable? If the answer is that the toy becomes inert, you are buying hardware with an expiry date you do not control.

What does the child do with their hands? If the hands only tap, the toy is doing the interesting work.

Does it have a mode that works for two children in the same room? If yes, it will outlive both the app and the novelty.

## What we would not buy

- Anything that requires an account before a child can play.
- Toys whose only mode is the app, unless you are happy to treat them as disposable.
- Anything that asks for a microphone or a camera with no offline mode at all.
- Toys aimed at children under three that involve a screen, because that age group has no screen allowance to spend.
- Tablets sold as toys with a fixed set of pre-installed games and no way to add or remove anything.

## The bottom line

Buy connected toys for the feedback loop, not for the word "smart" on the box. Prefer the version that still does something with the app switched off, assume the app has a shorter life than the plastic, and treat the whole purchase as screen time under the rules you already have.
