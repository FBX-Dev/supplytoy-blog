The photograph is always the same. A baby in a bath or a shallow pool, chin resting on an inflatable collar, arms out, apparently swimming. An adult two steps away with a phone in one hand taking the picture. It is one of the most shared images in parenting, and in December 2025 it became the subject of a mandatory federal standard in the United States written for that product and nothing else.

The fact that a category needed its own rule is itself the information. When the Consumer Product Safety Commission writes performance requirements for one kind of object, it is usually because the object's basic design is the problem rather than the manufacturing.

## A rule written for one product

The standard is 16 CFR 1250.5, published on 15 December 2025 at 90 FR 58134. Its scope is narrow and precise: it covers neck floats, whether or not they are inflatable, where the product is worn around the neck, holds its position by tightening or fastening, carries the child's weight from the neck, is used as a water play device in a sink, a bathtub, a paddling pool or a swimming pool, and is intended for children from birth to four years old.

The rule also carries an anti-stockpiling clause, which tells you something about how the industry reacted to it. Between 15 December 2025 and 15 June 2026, manufacturers and importers may not make or import non-compliant neck floats at a rate above 105 per cent of the average monthly volume they manufactured or imported in the thirteen months before publication. That provision exists because a new standard creates an obvious commercial temptation: fill the warehouse with the old design before the deadline.

## The problem is where the weight is carried

Everything else about a neck float follows from one design decision. The child hangs from the neck. The device's buoyancy is applied through a ring around the throat, so the load is taken by the neck, the jaw and the airway rather than distributed across the torso.

A life jacket works in the opposite direction. It spreads flotation across the chest and back, keeps the head above the surface as a consequence of how the body sits in the water, and can be lifted by a shoulder strap when an adult reaches in to pull the child out. A ring around the neck does none of those things. If it deflates, shifts or tips, the head is the part that goes under first, and there is nothing else holding the child up.

That is why the new standard is less about perfecting the category and more about fencing it in. Buoyancy reserve, a release mechanism that demands deliberate force or two separate actions, and a probe test for the size of the opening are each aimed at the neck-bearing design rather than at any particular brand.

## What the standard actually requires

The performance requirements are worth reading as a list, because each one maps onto a way the product can fail.

- A conditioning procedure, so that the device is tested after realistic use and storage rather than straight out of the packet.
- Minimum buoyancy of at least 30 per cent of the stated load capacity, and no more than 5 per cent loss of initial buoyancy once the inherent buoyancy elements are inflated.
- A restraint system that either requires two simultaneous actions to release, or a single action needing at least 50 N of force to open.
- A neck opening test in which a specified headform probe must not pass through the opening.
- Additional labelling on inflatable versions, which must read: "Check for leaks before use. Never use with leaks."

## The fit problem the weight table exposes

The rule includes a table of nominal load capacities, which is the closest thing to an official answer to the question of how much a baby of a given age weighs.

- 0 to 3 months: 17.0 lb
- 4 to 6 months: 21.0 lb
- 7 to 9 months: 23.4 lb
- 10 to 12 months: 25.4 lb
- 1 to 2 years: 38.8 lb
- 2 to 3 years: 51.2 lb
- 3 to 4 years: 52.3 lb

Read down that column and the central problem of the category becomes obvious. One device has to fit a body that roughly triples in mass across the range it is sold for, and the neck is the part that cannot be sized generously, because the opening must be small enough not to slip off and large enough not to press on the airway. There is no version of that trade-off that is comfortable for a baby, and the standard's answer is to police the opening rather than to claim the product is safe.

## The pediatricians name the category too

The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2026 policy statement on drowning prevention removes the ambiguity for anyone deciding what to buy. It says to avoid inflatable armbands, neck rings and "floaties" outright, on the grounds that they were not designed to protect a child.

The same statement sets out the rest of the water plan: a four-sided isolation fence at least four feet high with a self-closing, self-latching gate; a US Coast Guard approved life jacket, sized by weight and properly fastened, on any boat; and CPR training for the adults and teenagers in the household. The academy also extends the suction-entrapment requirements that already apply to public pools to private pools, on the grounds that many home pool owners do not know the risk exists.

## The rule that actually works

For a baby, the only supervision standard that survives contact with real life is contact itself. The guidance is touch supervision, which means an adult within arm's length of infants, toddlers and weak swimmers whenever they are in or around water.

Arm's length means close enough to grab a wrist without moving your feet. It is not the same as being in the room, and it is not the same as watching from the edge with a towel in your hands. The academy's own description is that drowning is quiet and fast: it does not shout, and it takes about a minute. The CDC counts roughly 4,000 fatal drownings a year in the United States and about 8,000 non-fatal ones, and the highest-risk group is 12 to 36 months, children who are mobile and curious and have no judgement about water at all.

For a baby, the places where this matters most are not pools. Infants most often drown in bathtubs and in buckets. A bucket left full in a garden, a bath run a few inches deep, a paddling pool with an inch of water in the bottom: these are the settings to think about, and none of them needs a special product, only an adult within reach who is not also holding a phone.

## Water safety before the first birthday

Before one, water safety is almost entirely about the adult rather than the equipment.

- Never leave a baby or toddler alone in the bath, not even to answer the door, and not with an older child supervising instead.
- Empty the bath, the bucket and the paddling pool the moment the session ends.
- Keep bathroom doors closed, and use a latch wherever a toddler can reach the handle.
- Fence or drain any standing water in the garden, including the inflatable pool.
- Take a CPR course, and make sure everyone who regularly looks after the child has done the same.

## Swimming lessons, after one

The academy's position on lessons is that a child can start working on water competency after their first birthday, and that the timing depends on emotional maturity, physical and cognitive ability and health, decided with a pediatrician rather than by the calendar.

That is a deliberately unsatisfying answer for anyone who wants a date, and it is the correct one. A one-year-old who is comfortable in water, follows a simple instruction and can be handled by an instructor is ready for a class. A one-year-old who is not is not, and waiting another season costs nothing. Whenever lessons start, they are not a substitute for supervision or for a life jacket, and neither is optional afterwards.

## What to buy instead

The honest answer to "what should I put a baby in for bath time" is nothing, and the honest answer to "what should a child wear near open water" is a life jacket.

- For the garden: [a four-sided pool fence with a self-closing, self-latching gate](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=pool+fence+self+closing+gate&tag=cnxixi-20).
- For the bath itself: [a non-slip mat on the floor of the tub](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=non+slip+bath+mat+for+baby&tag=cnxixi-20), which deals with the slip that happens in the second before a fall, and nothing at all that claims to hold a baby up.

## The bottom line

The United States published a mandatory standard for baby neck floats on 15 December 2025, and the reason one was needed is the design: the device carries a child's weight from the neck, which is the least forgiving place to put it. The American Academy of Pediatrics tells parents to avoid inflatable armbands, neck rings and floaties, and to keep an adult within arm's length of any child who cannot swim. Before the first birthday that is the entire water plan, plus a fence with a gate that closes itself. After the first birthday, discuss lessons with a pediatrician, and put a properly fitted life jacket on the child the moment open water is involved.
