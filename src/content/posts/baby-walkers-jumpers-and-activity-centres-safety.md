The walker arrives as a hand-me-down, still in the box, with the original stickers on the tray. The pitch is always the same and it always sounds reasonable: it keeps the baby entertained, it gives them a taste of moving around before they can walk, and you will be right there the whole time anyway.

It is that last part that the professional guidance is aimed at.

## A device that moves more than three feet in one second

The American Academy of Pediatrics has formally called for a stop to the manufacture and sale of wheeled baby walkers. The figure behind that position is the one worth keeping: a child in a walker can move more than 3 feet in 1 second.

Three feet per second is faster than an adult walks across a kitchen. It is faster than a hand travels from a lap to a hot pan. It is also faster than the reaction time that makes supervising feel like an adequate plan.

## Why "I was watching" is not a safety plan

Most walker injuries happen while an adult is watching. That single sentence is the most useful thing in the whole policy position, because it removes the assumption most families are running on.

Supervision is a good answer to a hazard that develops slowly and announces itself. It is a poor answer to a hazard that develops in a second, at knee height, behind a tray that blocks the view of the floor directly in front of the child. Nobody is claiming parents are careless. The claim is that the device outruns attention, and it does so by design rather than by accident.

It is also worth separating two different jobs people expect from the same object. The first is keeping a baby occupied and upright, which a stationary centre does perfectly well. The second is buying the adult twenty minutes of hands-free time, which a play yard does. Neither of those jobs requires the baby to be able to travel across a room faster than you can cross it, and that is the only thing the wheels add.

## Canada stopped selling them in 2004

Canada has banned wheeled baby walkers outright since April 2004. The ban covers manufacture, import, advertising and sale — including secondhand sales and garage sales, which is the detail that catches people out when the walker is being offered rather than bought.

When one product category is prohibited rather than regulated next door, and has stayed prohibited for more than twenty years, that is worth weighing against the fact that it is sitting on a shelf near you.

## What the injury research found

Between 1990 and 2014, more than 230,000 children under 15 months of age were treated in emergency departments for injuries related to baby walkers. Ninety per cent of those injuries were to the head or neck, and the category includes skull fractures.

The age range is the point. This is not a toy for older children that occasionally catches a toddler. It is a device used by children who are, by definition, the least able to protect their own heads.

## The voluntary redesign did not remove the risk

Since 1997, American walkers have been built to a voluntary design intended to reduce exactly these injuries: a base too wide to fit through a standard doorway, or a braking mechanism that catches at the edge of a step. The product standard behind that design is ASTM F977.

Injuries did not stop. A walker engineered so it cannot reach the stairs is still a device that moves a child at adult knee height, at speed, with no judgement about what is in front of them. The typical incident is not a staircase. It is a hot drink, a heater, a closing door, a pet, or another child.

## They do not teach walking

The other half of the sales pitch does not survive contact with the evidence either. Walkers do not help a child walk earlier, and there is evidence that they delay milestones such as independent walking.

The mechanism is not mysterious. In a walker, a child propels themselves on their toes, and they skip the sequence that actually builds the strength and balance walking requires: crawling, moving from lying down to sitting, and pulling up to standing. Those stages look like nothing is happening. They are the training.

## Doorway jumpers and suspended seats

The doorway jumper belongs in the same conversation, and it is worth being careful about what is known here. It is a device that suspends a baby who cannot yet stand, so that their weight hangs from a harness while they bounce. Falls and loading through the neck are the concerns that come up, and the developmental advice points the same way as it does for walkers: floor time and a stationary activity centre rather than a suspended one.

I would not quote a statistic for doorway jumpers, and I would be wary of anyone who does.

## What to use instead, in order

Start with the thing that costs nothing and works best, then work up. The order matters, and most families buy these in exactly the wrong sequence.

- Floor time. A blanket, a few safe objects within reach, and a baby free to roll, reach and push themselves up. This is the developmental work, and it is the thing a walker replaces.
- [A play yard or playpen](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=play+yard+for+babies&tag=cnxixi-20), which gives you a contained, safe area for the twenty minutes you need to cook. It solves the walker's real problem — a mobile baby and an unavailable adult — without adding wheels.
- [A stationary activity centre](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=stationary+activity+center+for+babies&tag=cnxixi-20), which offers the upright posture and the busy tray with no floor travel at all. This is the closest thing to a like-for-like swap.
- [A push and pull toy](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=push+and+pull+toys+for+1+year+old&tag=cnxixi-20), used once the child can already pull themselves up to standing, so they carry their own weight and set their own speed.

The push toy is the one people buy first and the one that should come last. A child who is not yet standing on their own will lean on it rather than push it, which teaches the same toe-first habit a walker does.

## What we would not buy

- A wheeled baby walker, new or secondhand, at any age.
- A walker described as tested without naming the standard it was tested to.
- A doorway jumper used as the substitute, which removes the wheels and keeps the suspension.
- A walker bought for a child under 15 months, which is precisely the group the injury data describes.
- A free hand-me-down walker, which is how almost all of them arrive in a house in the first place.

## The bottom line

Wheeled baby walkers are the rare piece of baby equipment where the professional advice is a ban rather than a caution. A child in one can move more than 3 feet in 1 second, most walker injuries happen while an adult is watching, and more than 230,000 children under 15 months were treated in emergency departments for walker injuries between 1990 and 2014, with ninety per cent of those injuries to the head or neck. Canada has prohibited them since April 2004. They do not teach walking, and there is evidence they delay it.

The swap is straightforward. Floor time first, then a play yard, then a stationary activity centre, and a push toy only once the child can stand up on their own.
