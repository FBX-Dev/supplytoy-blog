Every backyard looks bigger from the kitchen window than it does with a swing set in it. That is the most common mistake in this purchase, and it is usually made about four minutes into scrolling through listings, before anyone has walked the garden with a tape measure.

The official guidance for home play equipment is unusually specific about the ground, and almost none of it is about which frame you choose.

## Draw the use zone on the grass first

Before comparing anything, walk the space and mark out where the equipment will stand and how far the protective surface has to reach. The Consumer Product Safety Commission's Outdoor Home Playground Safety Handbook — publication 324, written as guidance for homes and residential child care facilities — gives the dimensions directly, and they are larger than most people expect.

Do this first because the answer frequently rules out the set you were looking at. A frame with a long swing arm needs a corridor of soft surface roughly twice the height of the top beam, front and back. That is a real distance in a normal garden, and it is the number that turns a promising layout into a non-starter.

## The use zone, in plain numbers

- Protective surfacing at least six feet in every direction from the equipment.
- For a to-fro swing, the surfacing in front of and behind the swing must extend a distance equal to twice the height of the top beam.
- For a tire swing, extend outward from the suspension point to the height of the suspending chain plus six feet.
- Nothing hard inside the use zone: tree trunks, fence posts, shed corners, raised planters and the edge of a patio all count.

The swing rule is the one people underestimate. A beam at a typical garden height means a soft corridor that is longer than the swing itself, in both directions, because a child comes off a moving swing forward and backward.

## Anchoring is not a finishing touch

Home equipment moves. The handbook is direct about it: play equipment may need to be restrained with anchors to keep it from tipping over while in use. The tipping direction is not always the obvious one, either — a swing under full travel pulls the frame sideways and forward as well as down.

Anchors are cheap, and they belong in the same order as the set itself. Buying them later means a first weekend of use with an unsecured frame, which is exactly when the novelty is at its peak and the children are least careful.

## The hardware details worth checking

Nearly all of the hardware risk compresses into two things you can check with your hands in the garden.

- Close S hooks and all hooks so that the gaps are less than the thickness of a dime.
- Cap or cut any exposed bolt end, because a threaded stud at head height is a scalp injury waiting to happen.
- Check swing hangers, chains and beam fittings before the season starts and again each month.
- Replace anything cracked, rusted through or stretched rather than tightening it and moving on.

A gap in a hook is not a cosmetic issue. It is the point where a chain works its way free under repeated loading, and it is the reason the dime test is written into the guidance with such precision.

## Nothing on the frame may form a loop

This is the rule that gets broken by well-meaning additions rather than by the manufacturer. The handbook is explicit: never attach, or allow children to attach, any kind of loop-forming material to the play set — ropes, twine, a jump rope, a pet leash, a clothesline, a belt.

That covers the rope swing improvised from the climbing frame, the skipping rope knotted around the beam, and the dog leash hung up out of the way on a post. A loop at head height on a structure children climb is the one modification that turns a play set into a strangulation risk, and it is worth checking the frame for it at the same time you check the bolts.

## The number that gets quoted wrong

You will see the claim that there are more than 200,000 playground injuries a year, usually presented as a public playground figure. It is not one. The wording in the CPSC public playground handbook says "on public playgrounds," but the underlying total covers public and home equipment combined.

The clean split comes from a CPSC special study published in April 2001. In 1999 the total was 205,850 injuries. Public equipment accounted for 156,040 of those, or 75.8 per cent. Home equipment accounted for 46,930, or 22.8 per cent. Homemade equipment accounted for 2,880, or 1.4 per cent.

That split is the useful part for a buyer. Roughly 47,000 injuries were on home equipment, and nobody inspects your garden but you. The public playground number is somebody else's maintenance schedule; the home number is yours.

## Hot slides and the back-of-hand test

A metal slide in full sun is the least obvious hazard in the garden, because children do not test surfaces before they use them. CPSC publication 3200, on thermal burns from playground equipment, notes that uncoated metal slides can cause serious burns, and that the agency learned of nearly 30 thermal burn incidents between 2001 and 2008. Plastic slides can also become hot enough to hurt, particularly in dark colours.

The habit takes ten seconds. Before the children go out, put the back of your hand on the slide surface and hold it there. The back of the hand is more sensitive than the palm, and it is the same test people use on a baby's bath water. If you would not hold your hand there, they should not sit on it.

## What the standard covers, and what it does not

Home playground equipment is covered by ASTM F1148, in full the Standard Consumer Safety Performance Specification for Home Playground Equipment, current version F1148-24, written for children from 18 months to 10 years old.

It is a voluntary standard. The CPSC lists it under voluntary standards, and unlike the toy standard ASTM F963 it is not a federal mandatory rule. Practically, that means it is a floor you can look for in a specification sheet rather than a guarantee that arrives in the box, and it is the reason assembly and maintenance matter as much as the purchase.

One useful exception: ground-level equipment — a sandbox, an activity wall, a playhouse — does not need protective surfacing at all. The use-zone arithmetic applies to the parts that lift a child off the ground.

## The bottom line

The swing set is the easy decision. The ground is the one that decides whether the purchase was a good idea: six feet of protective surfacing in every direction, twice the top beam height in front of and behind a swing, and the chain height plus six feet around a tire swing. Anchor the frame, close every hook until the gap is thinner than a dime, and keep every rope, leash and skipping rope off the structure permanently.

If your garden cannot give the set its use zone, the honest answer is a smaller set or a ground-level one — a sandbox, a playhouse, an activity wall — rather than a larger frame squeezed in against a fence. And on the day, ten seconds with the back of your hand on the slide saves an afternoon.
