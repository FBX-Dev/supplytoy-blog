## The night "just go to bed earlier" stopped working

Somewhere around ten or eleven, the old instruction stops landing. You say go to bed earlier, and your child lies there wide awake — not defiant, just awake. That is not a discipline problem, and a firmer tone does not fix it.

The AAP describes what is happening inside the body: "changes in the teenage brain shift their natural sleep-wake cycle later, making it harder for them to fall asleep early and wake up early." The shift starts before the teenage years officially do, which is why the second half of elementary school is where bedtime arguments get harder. From here on, bedtime is less about the clock and more about the two things you control — the routine and the room.

## How much sleep a 9-to-12-year-old actually needs

Start with the range, because it is wider than most parents assume. The CDC's sleep table lists school age, 6–12 years, at 9–12 hours, and teens, 13–17 years, at 8–10 hours. Those two bands overlap at the 12-to-13 boundary, and that overlap is the useful part: no switch flips on a thirteenth birthday and removes an hour of need.

So treat the number as a band to land inside, not a target to hit exactly. If your child is dragging through the afternoon anyway, sleep quality is the more useful question than sleep length.

## The room does more work than the argument

The AAP advises dimming the lights prior to bedtime and controlling the temperature in the home, and notes that falling asleep is easier when the bedroom is dark, cool and quiet. The CDC's version is a bedroom that is quiet, relaxing, and at a cool temperature.

The practical translation is unglamorous. Swap the overhead light for a real [bedside reading lamp for kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=bedside+reading+lamp+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) so the last hour of the evening can be dim instead of bright. If your child reads in bed, a [book light for reading in bed](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=book+light+for+reading+in+bed&tag=cnxixi-20) keeps the light on the page instead of bouncing off the ceiling. And the AAP is blunt about one thing parents rarely move: do not keep toys in children's bed.

## All the screens, out of the bedroom

This is the recommendation worth holding without negotiation. The AAP recommends keeping all screens — TVs, computers, laptops, tablets and phones — out of children's bedrooms, especially at night, and turning off all screens at least 60 minutes/1 hour before bedtime. The CDC gives a shorter version: turn off electronic devices at least 30 minutes before bedtime. Those are two different numbers from two different bodies, so pick the one you can actually hold — and know which one you picked rather than blending them into a middle figure.

There is a mechanical reason the screen rule matters. The AAP notes that the light coming from a screen may delay the brain's ability to release melatonin, a hormone that helps with sleep onset. An e-reader in bed is not the same object as a paper book, even when the words are identical. If you want something to replace the screen hour, the AAP points to a printed book or magazine.

## The routine that actually carries the load

Bedtime routines work because they are boring. The AAP's framing is to keep to a regular daily routine, with the same waking time on weekdays and weekends. The CDC's first sleep habit says the same thing: going to bed and getting up at the same time every day. The point is not the specific steps, but that the sequence is predictable enough that the body anticipates the end of the day.

Two more items from the CDC's list belong in this age band. Avoid caffeine in the afternoon or evening — for a ten-year-old that usually means soda, iced tea and chocolate milk after dinner, not coffee. And exercise regularly and maintain a healthy diet, because a child who has been active all day falls asleep more easily than one who has been sitting since three o'clock.

Then there is the last ten minutes, which is where the morning is won. The AAP suggests setting out clothes and packing lunches and bags before bed, because it helps mentally prepare for sleep and makes the morning routine easier.

## Weekends are where the routine quietly breaks

Saturday morning is where most families undo the week. Sleeping until ten on Saturday resets the body clock to a later schedule, and Monday's early alarm then feels like jet lag. The AAP's wording is explicit that the same waking time applies on weekdays and weekends.

You do not have to be rigid about it. A slightly later weekend wake-up still holds the shape of the routine; a four-hour swing does not. If your child needs something to keep the wake time steady, a [simple kids alarm clock](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+alarm+clock&tag=cnxixi-20) that they set themselves is more likely to survive than one you set for them. And keep an eye on evening commitments: the AAP warns about overscheduling, noting that sports games, lessons and appointments in the evening pose challenges to a good night's sleep. One evening activity is fine. Four is a sleep decision.

## Why this is worth the argument

Because the cost is real and it shows up in the daytime. The AAP writes that regular sleep deprivation often leads to some pretty difficult behaviors and health problems — irritability, difficulty concentrating, hypertension, obesity, headaches and depression.

That list is a reason to act, not a reason to panic. The action is small: pick one variable this week and hold it. Move the phone charging station to the kitchen, fix the weekend wake time, or dim the lights an hour earlier. One change you actually keep beats four you announce and abandon.

## How to tell whether they are short on sleep

You cannot always see it at bedtime, because a tired child at this age often does not look tired. They look wired, or annoyed, or unable to settle.

The CDC lists the signs worth watching for: trouble falling asleep, repeatedly waking up during the night, and feeling sleepy or tired even after getting enough sleep. Any of those, several nights running, is data rather than mood.

The other half of the picture is the daytime, which you will not see because you are not at school. The AAP suggests asking your child's teacher about alertness during the day, and notes that a child with not enough, or poor quality, sleep may have difficulty paying attention or "zoning out" in school. Zoning out is a signal, not laziness.

## What we would not put in a bedroom

A tablet, a smartwatch, or a projector night light that turns the ceiling into a screen. Each of those is a screen in the room where the AAP says screens should not be, and each tends to be marketed as helpful for sleep. The projector surprises parents most, because it looks like a night light. It is still a light source in the room, and still a screen.

## When to ask a doctor

Neither the CDC nor the AAP frames any of this as something a parent should diagnose. The CDC's line is to talk to your healthcare provider if you regularly have problems sleeping or notice signs or symptoms of common sleep disorders. The AAP lists the common sleep problems in children — difficulty falling asleep, nighttime awakenings, snoring, stalling and resisting going to bed, sleep apnea, and loud or heavy breathing while sleeping — and treats those as reasons to get a professional opinion.

Persistent snoring, or a child who seems to stop breathing briefly at night, is not a normal bedtime problem. Everything here is about the routine and the environment you control. It is not a diagnostic standard, and not a substitute for a pediatrician.

## The bottom line

The biological shift is not something you can argue away, and it is not evidence that your child is being difficult. What you can hold is the shape of the day: the same wake time on Saturday as on Tuesday, screens out of the room, lights down before bed, and the bag packed the night before. The AAP's advice to make sufficient sleep a family priority is the honest summary of all of it. And its reminder that you are a role model applies here more than anywhere else — a house where the adults also put their phones down at nine is a house where nine o'clock is believable.
