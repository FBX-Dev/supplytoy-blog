There is a specific and unpleasant experience familiar to a large number of parents: squeezing a rubber bath duck and watching a stream of dark water come out of it. The inside of a standard squeaky toy is warm, wet, dark and nutrient-rich, which is a fairly precise description of a mould incubator.

The good news is that this is entirely a design problem, and it is avoidable. This review covers which bath toys stay hygienic, which cannot be salvaged, and what to do with the ones you already have.

## Why squeaky toys are the problem

A classic rubber duck has three properties that combine badly:

1. **A hole.** The squeaker needs air to move, so there is an opening into a hollow interior.
2. **No drainage.** Water goes in and cannot get out, because the hole is small and the interior is sealed.
3. **No access.** You cannot open it, scrub it, or dry it properly.

Water enters during play, sits inside at body temperature, and stays there. Even if you squeeze the water out, the interior remains damp for days. Mould follows.

This is not a quality issue. An expensive rubber duck has the same problem as a cheap one, because the problem is the hole.

## The rule that solves it

**A bath toy should either have no interior, or be fully openable.**

That single filter eliminates most of the problematic products on the shelf and leaves you with a small, reliable set.

## The bath toys that pass the test

### Solid moulded pieces with no holes

Cups, boats, pouring tools, and shapes made from a single solid piece of plastic or silicone. No cavity means nowhere for water to sit.

Look for moulded bath toys with a solid body. They are often less charming than an animal shape, and considerably easier to live with.

### Open-bottomed cups and strainers

A cup is a bath toy. So is a colander, a small sieve, a funnel, and a jug. These pour, fill, sink, and float, and they are washable in a dishwasher.

A set of four open cups with holes in the base is one of the best-value bath purchases available, and it does not degrade.

### Stacking and nesting cups

They already work as bath toys, they have no enclosed cavities, and they drain. If you own nesting cups for dry play, they can simply be used in the bath as well. See [nesting bath cups](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=bath+toys+cups+toddler&tag=cnxixi-20).

### Boats and vehicles with a fully open hull

An open hull that drains is fine. A sealed hull with a small hole is not.

Test by looking at the underside: if there is a hole into a sealed interior, it will eventually hold water.

### Bath books

Waterproof books are excellent bath toys — flat, washable, no cavities, and they support language in a context where a child is a captive audience. Look for ones without a squeaker built into the spine, which reintroduces the problem.

### Silicone pieces, checked carefully

Silicone is more hygienic than rubber, but silicone toys with a hole still hold water. Check whether a silicone toy has an open structure or a sealed cavity before buying.

### Foam letters and shapes

These float, stick to wet tiles, and are genuinely useful for early literacy. Their weakness is that the foam can break down into small pieces, and they need to be dried flat rather than left in a wet heap.

Replace them when they start to crumble, and squeeze them out after every bath.

## The bath toys to avoid

**Any squeaky toy with a hole.** The classic duck, the squeaking frog, the squeaking anything. If it squeaks by moving air through a hole, it will take on water eventually.

**Toys with a battery compartment.** Water and batteries belong nowhere near each other, and the compartment seal is the first thing to fail. If a child is going to sit in water with something, that something should have no electronics at all.

**Toys with stickers or glued-on parts.** They lift, and the raw edge underneath traps water.

**Toys with narrow internal channels.** Pipes, sprays, and pumps are almost impossible to dry. If water cannot drain out by gravity, it will not.

**Anything too large for your bath.** A large ride-on bath toy needs space that a typical bathroom does not have, and it becomes an obstacle.

## What to do with the toys you already own

If you have a collection of bath toys that has been in use for months, this is worth an evening.

1. **Squeeze each one.** If dark or cloudy water comes out, it is colonised. Nothing you do will fully fix the interior.
2. **Seal the hole or retire it.** The standard fix is to seal the squeaker hole with hot glue or silicone sealant, which stops the problem worsening but also removes the squeak and permanently traps whatever is inside. For a toy with visible discharge, retirement is the better answer.
3. **Clean the ones that pass.** Wash in hot soapy water, then a dilute vinegar or bleach solution, then rinse thoroughly. Bath toys go in a child's mouth, so rinse properly.
4. **Dry everything completely.** Standing them upright to drain is not enough; they need airflow. A mesh bag hung up allows toys to drain and dry between baths.
5. **Set a rotation.** Keep two sets and alternate weekly so each set dries fully. This one habit eliminates most mould growth.

## Reducing the problem structurally

A few habits make more difference than any purchase.

**Empty and dry the toy container after every bath.** A bucket of standing water with toys in it is the worst possible storage.

**Use a mesh draining bag** rather than a sealed bin. Air movement is what prevents mould.

**Do not leave toys in the bath between uses.** Toys that stay in the water never dry.

**Limit the number of toys in the bath.** Fewer toys means each one drains and dries properly, and bath play does not suffer — a child mostly wants cups, water and a container.

**Rinse after every bath.** Soap residue and skin cells are what feeds the growth, and a five-second rinse prevents most of it.

## Keeping bath time interesting without more toys

The most engaging bath activities cost nothing. Pouring between cups, watching a cup sink and float, squeezing a sponge, painting the tiles with water and a brush, and blowing bubbles through a straw.

For children over three, a small set of open cups, a jug, a sponge, a brush and a couple of open-hulled boats will hold attention for far longer than a bucket of squeaking animals, and none of it will ever need to be thrown away.

## The bottom line

Choose bath toys with no interior or a fully open one. Solid moulded cups and shapes, open-hulled boats, waterproof books and pouring tools are the whole list you need. Avoid anything that squeaks, anything with batteries, and anything with a sealed cavity and a small hole.

Store them in a mesh bag, dry them after each bath, and rotate two sets. That is the entire solution, and it costs less than the mouldy toys it replaces.
