Four and five is when the toy industry starts losing the plot. The children's aisle fills with licensed plastic tied to whatever is popular on screen this year, while the things four-year-olds actually play with for hours — construction sets, real craft materials, board games, and anything that resembles adult equipment — sit in other parts of the shop entirely.

If you are buying for a four or five-year-old, it is worth leaving the toy aisle.

## What four and five-year-olds can do

The jump from three to four is substantial, and the jump from four to five is larger than most people expect.

- **Plan ahead.** They can decide what they are going to build before starting, and hold that intention across a session.
- **Follow multi-step instructions.** Long enough to work through a simple diagram or a sequence of four or five steps.
- **Sustain attention for twenty to forty minutes** on something they chose, which is a completely different proposition from being entertained.
- **Cooperate with rules.** Genuine turn-taking, negotiated rules, and the beginnings of fairness arguments.
- **Write some letters, and their name.** Often imperfectly, and that is fine.
- **Use scissors, tape and glue with reasonable control.** Supervised, but genuinely functional.

## The purchases that pay off most

### Construction kits with real mechanisms

Gears, axles, pulleys, and working joints. Four and five-year-olds are ready for machines rather than towers, and [kits built around gears and pulleys](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=building+set+with+gears+and+pulleys+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) hold attention far longer than a static model, because the child is building a thing that moves rather than a picture of one.

The value here is enormous and it compounds: the same kit stays interesting at seven and eight, because the child's building ambition grows with their ability.

### A proper set of real art materials

Not washable crayons and a colouring book. Actual acrylic or poster paint, decent brushes, real tape, a good pair of scissors that cut properly, and a large supply of cardboard.

Blunt scissors that barely cut are one of the most common frustrations at this age. A child who cannot cut successfully will conclude they are bad at cutting. Buy scissors that work, and supervise.

Cardboard deserves its own line. Boxes, tubes and offcuts have more play value at four than most purchased toys, and they are free.

### Board games that a family can genuinely play

Four and five is the golden age for family games, because the child can now follow rules, take turns, and cope — mostly — with losing.

Start with games that have short rounds and little reading: matching games, simple card games, first strategy games with a board and a few pieces. Avoid games that take longer than fifteen minutes, and avoid anything where a single bad turn eliminates a player early, because that produces tears before the game is over.

This is also the age where a deck of ordinary playing cards becomes genuinely useful. Simple matching and memory games need nothing else.

### Audio books and a player

A significant, underrated purchase. A four or five-year-old can follow a story for twenty minutes with no adult present, which buys the household quiet time and builds narrative comprehension. Look for [an audio player the child can work without help](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=audio+player+for+kids+with+buttons&tag=cnxixi-20), ideally with physical buttons and no screen.

### Building sets with small pieces

Lego-style sets with bricks in the four-to-six-millimetre range arrive properly now. Buy by theme loosely and prioritise sets with wheels, hinges and a variety of special pieces over sets that build one specific model.

The best value is a large mixed tub rather than a sequence of small themed kits. Mixed bricks get rebuilt for years; a kit gets built once.

### Woodworking and real tools

Real tools, properly sized, with an adult. A hand drill, a small saw, sandpaper, and a hammer with real nails. Under supervision, this is one of the most absorbing activities available to a five-year-old, and it teaches consequence better than any conversation about consequence.

Start with sanding, then hammering, then cutting. Buy safety glasses at the same time.

### Money, shops and small economy games

A toy till, play money, and a real-world trip to a shop with a small budget. Four and five-year-olds are intensely interested in buying and paying. This is maths arriving naturally, in a context they care about, rather than as a worksheet.

## What to skip

- **Most licensed character merchandise.** It is bought for recognition rather than for play value, and it rarely survives the year.
- **Electronic learning tablets.** They ask questions and mark answers, which is a remarkably narrow version of learning at an age when real-world cause and effect is available.
- **Large single-purpose plastic playsets.** Floor space is precious and these rarely get reconfigured.
- **Anything with more than a couple of hundred pieces, unless the child already builds.** Volume at this age still usually means adult tidying.
- **Workbooks aimed at age four.** There is no rush, and a resistant four-year-old is harder to work with than a willing five-year-old.

## Screens, honestly

Most four and five-year-olds will use screens, and pretending otherwise is unhelpful. What matters is the pattern rather than the total.

Interactive, creative screen use — drawing, building, coding toys with physical components — behaves more like a toy than like television. Passive viewing behaves differently and is better rationed. Co-viewing and talking about what is happening changes the value of the time considerably.

The clearest practical rule is this: screens should not be the only option available. A child with paints, cardboard and a building kit in reach will often choose them, if choosing is possible.

## Making space for long play

Four and five-year-olds need a project to stay out for days. A half-built machine, a cardboard castle under construction, a game left mid-round. If everything must be tidied away each evening, long projects never happen.

A corner, a table, or a tray that can be left in place is genuinely a toy in itself. It is the most valuable thing you can give a child of this age, and it costs nothing.

## The bottom line

At four and five, buy a mechanical construction kit, real art materials including scissors that cut, a handful of good board games, an audio player, a mixed tub of small bricks, and some real tools to use with an adult.

Then give the child somewhere to leave things half-finished. That is the purchase that makes all the others worth it.
