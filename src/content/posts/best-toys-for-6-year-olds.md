Six is the year a toy box stops being organised by the child and starts being organised by the calendar. School arrives with a start time, a bag that has to be packed the night before, and an afternoon shorter than the one they had last year. Whatever gets played with at six gets played with in the gap between coming home and dinner.

That is a better filter than any age label on a box. A toy has to survive a tired child with forty minutes and no adult willing to set anything up.

## What actually changes at six

- Reading takes off unevenly. Some children read fluently at six and some are still decoding, and both groups need the same thing: books they can finish.
- Rules become interesting. Games with real rules and real losses are playable now in a way they were not a year earlier.
- Fine motor control is good enough for real construction: small bricks, supervised needle and thread, proper scissors, model kits.
- Friends matter more. A toy that supports two children at once beats a toy that only supports one.
- The appetite for competence appears. Children this age would rather do something hard that is real than something easy that is pretend.

If you buy one kind of toy this year, buy the kind that gets better with practice rather than the kind that is finished the moment it is unboxed.

## Games with real rules

Six is when a board game stops being a lesson in turn-taking and starts being an actual game. The games that get played repeatedly are short, involve no reading or almost none, and can be taught in two minutes by a child to another child.

Length is the variable that matters most. A game that takes fifteen minutes will be played after school and again before bed. A game that takes forty-five minutes will be played twice and then live on top of the wardrobe, because the setup cost is higher than the reward. Watch out for elimination mechanics as well: a child who is out of the game in the first five minutes has nothing to do but interfere with everyone else.

Cooperative games are worth one slot in the cupboard, particularly for a child who struggles with losing. Browse [board games for early readers](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=board+games+for+early+readers&tag=cnxixi-20) if you want the versions that do not require an adult to read every card aloud.

## Reading independence changes the toy list

At six, books stop being something an adult performs and become something a child can do alone, which quietly moves them into the toy category. What a new reader needs is volume and ease: short books, series with the same characters, comics, joke books, anything with fewer words per page than the child's patience for decoding.

The mistake is buying the book you want them to read. A child who can finish a thin book in twenty minutes will read four of them and feel like a reader. A child handed something impressive and long will read four pages and feel like a failure. Ask a librarian or a teacher what the child can actually decode, then buy three of those.

If you want a starting point, [chapter books for new readers](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=chapter+books+for+beginning+readers&tag=cnxixi-20) is the section of the shop to browse, and thin books beat thick ones every time.

Two accessories make this work better than any book: a light they control, so reading after lights-out is possible without a negotiation, and a shelf or box that is theirs to organise. A reading nook is not a luxury at this age. It is the delivery system.

## Fine motor work disguised as play

The reason craft supplies beat most toys at six is that the child now has the hands for it. Folding, cutting on a line, threading, tying, pressing hard enough with a pencil, rolling clay into a shape that holds, sewing a running stitch with supervision. None of it looks like schoolwork, and all of it is the same skill set.

Buy real scissors. The blunt plastic kind that cannot cut paper are harder to use and teach nothing except that cutting is a chore. The same principle applies across the category: clay that holds its shape, paper that does not shred under a brush, glue that sticks within a minute.

Cooking belongs here too. A six-year-old can measure, stir, pour and cut soft things with a small knife under supervision, and the result is dinner, which is a much better outcome than anything on a shelf.

## Building sets and the first collections

Small bricks start working properly at six, because the pincer grip and the patience for a sequence of steps both arrive at roughly the same time. Look for sets that build more than one thing, and be sceptical of enormous single-model kits, which produce one proud afternoon and one object that can never be taken apart without grief.

At the same age, the first genuine collections appear. Cards, small figures, stones, shells, stamps, anything the playground is trading that term. A collection is not clutter if the child is the one sorting it, so the useful purchase is usually storage: binders, sleeves, boxes with dividers, a shelf with room to grow. [Building sets for the six to nine range](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=building+sets+for+kids+6+to+9&tag=cnxixi-20) and card sleeves are both unglamorous and both get used for years.

## Wheels and the scooter decision

This is where a six-year-old's toy list stops being harmless. Nonmotorised scooters are the single largest category of toy-related injury in the United States, accounting for about 23% of estimated toy-related injuries in 2023, or roughly 53,000 injuries, and for more than a fifth of all toy-related injuries in children under 15.

Two numbers make that less alarming and more useful. Of toy-related emergency visits, 45% involve the head and face, and 92% of patients are treated and released. In other words, most of these incidents are survivable and unpleasant, and the ones that are not tend to involve the head. That is the argument for a helmet, bought at the same time as the scooter rather than in the spring.

When you buy the scooter itself, the brake is the feature to test in the shop, not the colour. A rear brake that needs a hard stamp from a small foot is a brake that will not be used. Check the wheels are large enough to cross a pavement crack without stopping dead, and that the handlebar folds and locks with an audible click rather than a hopeful wobble. If you would rather shop from a shortlist than from a shelf, [scooters with a rear brake and larger wheels](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+scooter+rear+brake&tag=cnxixi-20) is the direction to look in. A quiet and genuinely useful point: helmets should be removed on playground equipment and when climbing trees, because a helmet can catch in a gap that a bare head would slide through.

The mandatory toy safety standard, ASTM F963-23, covers toys for children under 14, but it does not cover bicycles, kites, playground equipment or non-powder guns. A first bike is regulated on its own terms, so the safety of the thing is down to the buyer.

## The screen boundary, not the screen limit

This is the year the official advice changes shape. Up to five, the guidance is a limit, and at six and older it stops being a number. The American Academy of Pediatrics does not set a universal hourly cap for children six and above; it recommends consistent boundaries about when, what kind and where, agreed with the child in advance, and written into a family media plan.

The evidence behind that shift is worth knowing. Family rules about balance, content, co-viewing and talking about media are associated with better outcomes than rules that only count minutes. Screen time climbing above four hours a day has been linked with higher rates of anxiety, depression, behaviour problems and ADHD symptoms, particularly where the screen is displacing sleep and physical activity.

So the practical version at six is not a stopwatch. It is no screens in the bedroom and none at the table, nothing before school, and a shared agreement about what happens when the time is up. Toys that come with an app are fine as long as the physical toy still works when the app is gone, which is the test most of them fail.

## What to skip

- Workbooks marketed as toys. Six-year-olds get enough of that at school.
- Sets with several hundred pieces and one possible model.
- Battery toys with one function and no volume switch.
- Toys that need an adult to make them fun, unless the adult has agreed to be there.
- Products aimed at the child they were last year. Read the age label critically, in both directions.
- Uninflated or broken balloons. They are a choking hazard, and children under eight should be kept away from them.
- Small parts and loose magnets if there is a younger sibling in the house. The same toy is a different risk to a three-year-old.

## The bottom line

At six, buy short games with no reading, a stack of books the child can finish alone, real scissors and materials worth using, bricks that build more than one thing, storage for a collection, and a scooter with a brake that works plus a helmet that fits. Then protect the forty minutes after school, because that is where all of it actually happens.
