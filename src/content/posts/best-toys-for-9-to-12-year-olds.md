Walk the toy aisle with a ten-year-old and you can watch the shelves run out of ideas. Up to about eight, every metre of it is aimed at them. After that the products thin out into collectibles, electronics and things that are not toys at all, because the industry designs for the youngest buyer in the house, and a ten-year-old is rarely that buyer.

This is the most underserved age in the shop, and the widest spread. A nine-year-old and a twelve-year-old can want completely different things and be handed the same present.

## The gap in the middle of the toy aisle

The reason is not mysterious. A toy that sells to a five-year-old sells for a defined number of years and then the child moves on. A nine-to-twelve buyer is harder to predict, harder to advertise to, and much more likely to have noticed that the toy version is worse than the real thing.

What fills the gap falls into three directions: real tools, real projects and real equipment. None of them live in the toy aisle, which is why parents end up guessing.

## What nine to twelve actually want

- Competence over novelty. They want to be visibly good at something, not to own something new.
- Autonomy. A gift they can use without asking an adult beats a better one they cannot.
- Ownership of something private: a space, a box, a notebook, a camera roll that is theirs.
- A project with a real outcome, ideally one that can be shown to somebody afterwards.
- Physical challenge. Wheels, water, climbing, speed, being outside without a parent watching closely.

## Real tools and real materials

Nine to twelve is the age where the children's version of a tool becomes insulting. A plastic drill that does nothing is a demotion. A real hand drill with a small bit set, used with an adult nearby, is a different proposition entirely.

The categories that work: woodwork, cooking with a genuine knife, a sewing machine, electronics and simple soldering, gardening with proper secateurs, photography, bike maintenance, model making. Each has a real output. If you want a starting direction, [real tools sized for smaller hands](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=real+tools+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) is where to look, and the adult who supervises is part of the gift.

The same logic applies to materials: real canvas, real thread, decent paper. A twelve-year-old knows the difference between a good pencil and a bad one, and buying the good version is a form of respect.

## Projects measured in weeks, not afternoons

The difference between a toy that lasts and one that dies at this age is whether it has stages. A kit whose result appears in twenty minutes is a snack. Something that takes a week has a middle, and the middle is where the engagement lives.

The practical test is whether the work can be left out. If everything has to be packed away each night, the project will be abandoned by day three. A table in a corner or a tray with raised edges gives it somewhere to sit unfinished, which is a genuine constraint in most houses.

Anything that produces a visible object is stronger than anything that produces a score. A model, a set of photographs, a finished garment, a repaired bicycle, a grown plant. [Science and build kits for the older end](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=science+kits+for+kids+9+to+12&tag=cnxixi-20) covers the range, but check the listed completion time, because half of that shelf is a twenty-minute demonstration in a large box.

## Collections that turn into expertise

A collection at nine is different from one at seven. The child now has opinions about the subject and not just the objects, and the collection becomes a route into knowing something.

Buy the equipment of expertise rather than the items themselves: storage that protects, display that shows, and reference material that answers questions. A binder and sleeves, a case with proper dividers, a magnifier, a jeweller's scale, a lamp, a good catalogue or guide, a small shelf with a label on it.

Avoid completing the collection for them. A child allowed to decide what the last piece is will spend a year thinking about it, comparing prices and saving. Bought on their behalf, the same piece removes all of that and leaves an object with nothing behind it.

## Wheels, water and climbing

This is where a parent's attention actually matters, and it is worth being specific.

Nonmotorised scooters are the single biggest injury category in the toy statistics. They accounted for about 23% of estimated toy-related injuries in 2023, roughly 53,000 injuries, and for more than a fifth of toy-related injuries in children under 15. Skateboards have their own rule of thumb: children under five should not be on them at all, and between six and ten an adult should be watching.

Helmets carry the load here. Replace one after any impact, visible damage or not, since the foam does not regain its shape, and manufacturers often suggest replacing one after around five years. A bicycle helmet sold in the United States must meet the CPSC rule at 16 CFR Part 1203, and a helmet with only that mark is a bike helmet and nothing more. Skateboarding and trick roller-skating helmets are tested to ASTM F1492 for repeated impacts instead. There is no federal standard for skate helmets at all, so unless a helmet is marketed for cycling it can be sold for skating with no certification whatsoever. The sticker inside is the only claim worth trusting. If your child rides, [a youth helmet that fits properly](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=youth+bike+helmet&tag=cnxixi-20) is a better purchase than any toy on this page.

Water deserves a straight sentence too. For children aged 5 to 14, drowning is the second leading cause of unintentional injury death, behind motor vehicle crashes. That is a reason to care about swimming and about who is watching, not to be frightened of a paddling pool. One related rule from the American Academy of Pediatrics: nobody under 16 should ride or operate an electric scooter.

One clarification matters when you read a safety claim on a box. The mandatory toy standard, ASTM F963-23, covers toys for children under 14, but it does not cover bicycles, kites, playground equipment or non-powder guns. Those are regulated on their own terms, which is why the helmet and adult judgement end up doing the work.

## Devices, at an age with no hour limit

The official guidance for children six and older contains no universal number of hours. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends consistent limits on time, content type and location, agreed with the child. Family rules about balance, content, co-viewing and talking about media are associated with better outcomes than rules that only count minutes. Screen time above four hours a day has been linked with higher rates of anxiety, depression, behaviour problems and ADHD symptoms, and that link is strongest where the screen is pushing out sleep and exercise.

At nine to twelve this becomes a negotiation rather than a decision. Charges in shared rooms, phones out of bedrooms at night and a shared calendar are more workable than a stopwatch. If a gift is an app-connected toy, ask what remains when the app is discontinued. That question removes most of the category.

One piece of perspective while we are on risk. In 2023 there were 10 toy-related deaths in children aged seven months to six years, and later reports added 17 more from 2022 and 2023 combined, with ages running from 23 months to 14 years. Five of those involved children on nonmotorised scooters who were struck by a motor vehicle. The lesson is not that toys are dangerous at ten, but that the risk has moved onto the pavement and into the water.

## The budget problem

Gifts at this age cost more and are used for longer, so the arithmetic differs from the toddler years. One bicycle that lasts four years beats four toys that last four months, and the same is true of a sewing machine, a camera or a musical instrument.

That makes second-hand sensible rather than shabby for anything metal, wooden or mechanical, provided you check it properly. Bikes, instruments and workbenches are better bought used and good than new and cheap, and a child grows into equipment rather than out of it.

## What to skip

- Junior versions of adult equipment that will be outgrown or snapped within a season.
- Anything with a monthly fee the parents did not agree to and the child cannot cancel.
- Water beads in a house with a younger sibling. CPSC has recorded 52 reports of swallowing, with 47 confirmed ingestions in children aged nine months to five years, producing one death, 23 hospitalisations and 12 emergency treatments. One ten-month-old died after a bead swelled to about 28 mm and blocked her bowel. Beads keep growing after they are swallowed and are harder to find on an X-ray than a marble, so the advice is to keep them out of any household with a child under five.
- Toys clearly aimed at eight-year-olds. They read it as being talked down to.
- Sets of hundreds of tiny pieces in a house with a toddler. Small parts and loose magnets are a different risk to the youngest child in the room.
- Anything that requires you to be present every time it is used.
- Uninflated or broken balloons around children under eight.

## The bottom line

The toy industry stops designing seriously for this age, so stop shopping as if it had not. Buy real tools, one project with stages and somewhere for it to sit unfinished, storage and reference material for a collection the child owns, and equipment for the physical thing they actually do, helmet included. Buy fewer things, buy them better, and treat the child as competent.
