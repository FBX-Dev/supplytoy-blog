Wooden blocks occupy an odd position in the toy world. They are recommended by nearly everyone — paediatricians, nursery teachers, Montessori guides, grandparents — and yet a large number of the sets sold never get played with. The block set in a corner is usually not a bad set. It is usually a set that was bought at the wrong moment, in the wrong size, or with a very small child and no adult nearby to seed the first idea.

This piece is an honest look at what makes a wooden block set worth owning between one and three years old, including the parts that are less charming than the marketing suggests.

## The honest case for wooden over plastic

Wooden blocks are heavier, quieter, and considerably more expensive than plastic alternatives. The usual arguments in their favour hold up well:

- **Weight gives feedback.** A heavy block that topples teaches balance more convincingly than a light one that scatters.
- **They survive.** A decent hardwood set handles a decade of use and then can be passed on. This is genuinely rare in toys.
- **The sound is tolerable.** Plastic blocks on a hard floor produce a noise that wears parents down within a week. Wood is duller and easier to live with.
- **No batteries, no firmware, no app.** Nothing to expire.

The counter-arguments are equally real. Wooden blocks hurt more when thrown. They chip and splinter if the wood is soft. Cheap sets are often painted with finishes that wear off, and they are heavy enough that a full basket is genuinely not portable — you will not be taking this to a restaurant.

## What actually matters when choosing

### Wood species, not marketing language

You will see "natural wood" on almost every listing, which tells you very little. Rubberwood, beech, and maple are the species that hold up well; they are hard enough that a corner survives being dropped onto tile. Balsa-type softwoods dent and splinter and should be avoided for a child who throws.

### Size and count

This is where most parents get it wrong in both directions.

A set of twenty to thirty blocks with a mix of shapes is the practical sweet spot for one to three years. Fewer than fifteen and the child cannot build anything satisfying. More than fifty and the volume becomes a barrier — the child cannot see the options, and the adult spends ten minutes tidying for every two minutes of play.

Smaller is generally better than larger here. Cubes around two to four centimetres across are manageable for a two-year-old's hand. Very large blocks look appealing online but are usually too big to let a child build anything with any detail.

### Shape variety beats cube count

A set of forty identical cubes teaches exactly one thing. A set with cubes, rectangular prisms, cylinders, triangles, arches, and half-circles opens up doors, bridges, roofs, tunnels, and ramps. Arches in particular are valuable because they fail in interesting ways when loaded, which is precisely the lesson blocks exist to teach.

If you are comparing two sets at a similar price, count the distinct shapes rather than the total pieces.

### Finish

Untreated or oiled wood is the safest option for a child still putting things in their mouth. If a set is painted, look for water-based, saliva-resistant finishes, and check that the paint has not been used to cover a soft wood underneath. A block whose paint has already chipped in the product photos will chip in your house too.

## Matching the set to the age

### Twelve to eighteen months

Stacking and knocking down. Nothing else. Buy a small set — a dozen chunky pieces in two or three shapes — and expect it to be handled, banged together, and knocked over rather than built with.

At this stage a block is a mouthing object that happens to be square. Do not invest heavily yet.

### Eighteen months to two and a half years

The first real towers. Two or three blocks, placed deliberately, wobbling. This is the stage where a limited set genuinely helps, because too many options produces scattering rather than stacking.

The most useful thing an adult can do here is build alongside the child rather than for them. A tower built in front of a toddler, described out loud — "one more block on top, very slowly" — does more than any toy feature.

### Two and a half to three years

Enclosures, roads, and the beginnings of pretend. You will start to see blocks arranged in a line and treated as a train, or arranged in a ring and treated as a fence. This is where shape variety starts paying off, and where a set that felt too small at eighteen months suddenly feels too small again.

If you have bought a small starter set, this is the moment to add a second set with arches and wider planks.

## Alternatives worth considering

**Cardboard and foam blocks.** Cheaper and quieter, genuinely safer for a throwing toddler. They do not last, and they do not give the same weight feedback. Fine as a supplement, not as a replacement.

**Interlocking wooden planks.** A step up in complexity. Excellent from about three years, frustrating before that.

**Large hollow blocks.** Superb for building structures big enough to sit inside, but they need floor space and an adult willing to store them. Not a small-flat purchase.

**Magnetic tiles.** A different activity entirely — more about surfaces and enclosures than about balance. Worth owning in addition to blocks, not instead of them.

## What to skip

- **Sets marketed with colour-sorting activities.** The stated educational goal rarely matches how children play, and the colour charts add plastic clutter.
- **Blocks with printed letters or numbers on every face.** They narrow the toy into a flashcard and the print wears off.
- **Anything with a "recommended age" of six months.** No six-month-old builds anything, and this label usually denotes a very small piece size.
- **Very large sets at a very low price.** Something has to give, and it is almost always the wood hardness.

## Keeping blocks in rotation

Block sets die from tidiness, not from boredom. A single deep bin means the child has to dig past the cubes to find the one arch, so they give up and play with something else.

Two fixes that work: sort by shape into shallow trays rather than one deep box, and keep only fifteen to twenty pieces out at a time, rotating the rest back in every few weeks. The set feels new, the floor is easier to clear, and the child can see what they have.

## The bottom line

Buy a mixed-shape hardwood set of roughly twenty-five pieces, untreated or oiled rather than painted, and buy it when your child is around eighteen months old rather than at their first birthday. Add arches and planks at two and a half. Expect the blocks to sit unused for a fortnight at a time and then be played with daily for a month.

That pattern is normal. It is not a verdict on the blocks.
