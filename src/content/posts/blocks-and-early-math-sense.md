A one-year-old with a bucket of blocks is not doing maths. They are doing something older and stranger: testing whether the world is made of things that behave the same way every time. A block dropped from a high chair lands on the floor. It lands on the floor the next time too. That discovery is not trivial, and everything a four-year-old later does with numbers is built on top of it.

So when people ask what blocks teach, the honest answer is that they teach the preconditions. Before a child can count reliably, they need to sort, order, compare, and describe position. Blocks give all four, without a worksheet and without an adult hovering.

## Why stacking two blocks is a milestone

The CDC's milestone checklists, revised in 2022 with a "most children" threshold rather than an average, include stacking two blocks on the 15-month list. Parents often read that as a party trick. It is not. Stacking two blocks requires a child to hold one object steady while placing a second on top of it — planning, grip control, balance, and a working idea that one thing can sit on another.

That last part is the maths. It is the beginning of the intuition that objects have stable relationships to each other in space, which is the same intuition that later lets a child line up counters, compare two piles, or understand that a tower and a row are the same number of blocks arranged differently.

The other thing the milestone lists are good for is calibration. They are monitoring tools, not diagnoses, and the thresholds were deliberately reset so that they describe what most children can do rather than what the average child can do. If you find yourself keeping score in the block corner, you are using them for the wrong job. If you are worried, the answer is a pediatrician and a screening conversation at the recommended 9, 18 and 30 month points, not a wait-and-see month of informal testing at home.

## Sorting comes before counting

Sorting is the first mathematical act, and toddlers do it without instruction. Watch a sixteen-month-old with a mixed bucket and you will see them pull all the red pieces into one place, or all the cubes away from the cylinders, or all the small ones into a tin.

Adults tend to interrupt this by turning it into a lesson. "That's the red one! Can you find another red one?" is not wrong, but it replaces the child's classification with yours. The useful move is to describe what they did after they did it: "You put all the blue ones together." Naming the category they invented is what makes the category available to them again tomorrow.

Sorting by one attribute at a time is the developmental sequence, so resist the urge to add a second rule. Colour first, or size first, not both at once. Two-attribute sorting arrives much later, and asking for it early just produces a child who stops sorting.

## Ordering, and the long road to seriation

Nesting cups and graduated rings are the most efficient tool in this whole category, because they make order physical. A cup is either small enough to go inside another cup or it is not, and the toy tells the child immediately which it is. That is feedback no adult has to provide.

Seriation — arranging a set from smallest to largest — is a genuine cognitive achievement, and it arrives in pieces. First cups nested by trial and error with a lot of shoving. Then a mostly correct stack with one cup out of place. Then a correct stack, and only after that the same set lined up as a row on the floor, which is the step that links size to position.

The stack that falls over is doing more work than the stack that stands. A tower that survives because an adult steadied it taught the child nothing about weight, centre, or edges.

## Symmetry, pattern, and the first taste of structure

Give a child a tray of blocks and some duplicates, and eventually they will build one thing on the left and something matching on the right. That is symmetry, and it is not decoration. It is the first time a child deliberately repeats a structure, which is what a pattern is.

Pattern play later becomes the bridge to counting by groups and to arithmetic facts, but you cannot rush the road there. What you can do is leave duplicate blocks in the set. A bin of forty unique pieces cannot produce a pattern, because patterns need repeats.

## Spatial language is the quiet one

The most underrated thing blocks do is generate the words for space. There is a difference between a child who can put the block on the tower and a child who can put the block behind the tower, and only one of those children is being given the vocabulary to describe relations.

Use the words out loud while the child plays and while you play alongside: on, under, behind, next to, in front of, inside, between, over. Then use them to give small instructions the child can carry out. "Put the blue one under the red one." This is a listening task, a spatial task, and a pre-reading task at the same time, and it costs nothing.

If a child does not understand a spoken instruction at eighteen months, or has no words at sixteen months, that belongs with a pediatrician rather than with a block strategy. Language progress varies enormously and bilingually raised children should have both vocabularies counted together, not measured against one.

## What blocks to actually have

You need very few kinds, and the classic shapes are classic for a reason.

- **Unit blocks in a standard wooden set**, if you are buying one thing. Rectangles and cubes in a consistent size relationship let a child discover that two of one makes one of the other, which is arithmetic under the paint.
- **Large cardboard or foam bricks** for children at the demolishing stage, because the failure mode is a child who learns to avoid towers after getting hurt by one.
- **Chunky first blocks with letters or numbers on the faces**, used as blocks for the first year and as symbols much later. Buying them for the letters is a mistake; buying them for the shapes is fine.
- **Nesting and stacking cups**, still the best value in the category for a child between one and two and a half.
- **A sorting tray or a muffin tin**, which is not a toy but is what makes sorting visible.

## What we would not buy

- **Counting toys that announce the answer.** A toy that says "three" after the child presses the third block has removed the counting from the counting.
- **Sets where every piece has one correct home.** A shape sorter with four forgiving holes is a good toy. One with twelve exact holes and a lid that only closes one way is a puzzle with an adult-shaped bottleneck.
- **Blocks with instructions and a target picture on the box.** If the box shows the one thing to build, you have bought a model kit with extra steps.
- **Very large mixed sets.** Hundreds of pieces produce dumping, not building. Most toddlers build better out of twenty blocks than two hundred.
- **Anything marketed with claims about maths ability or school readiness.** There is no block set that produces that, and the claim is a reason to look elsewhere.

## The bottom line

Buy fewer blocks than you think you need, in a standard size relationship, plus one nesting set and one tray for sorting. Then get on the floor and describe what the child did rather than what they should do next.

The maths here is invisible and slow: same and different, bigger and smaller, in front of and behind, again and again. It will not show up in a report. It shows up years later, when a child meets numbers and finds that the categories were already familiar.
