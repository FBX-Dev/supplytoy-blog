The evidence on reading to young children is unusually consistent and unusually strong. Shared reading in the first years is associated with vocabulary, language comprehension, and later reading ability, and the effect comes from the interaction rather than the book.

That said, not all board books are equal in practice. Some survive a year of a baby's attention. Some are destroyed in a fortnight. And a surprising number are unreadable — badly typeset, or with illustrations that a child cannot parse.

## What a board book needs to survive

Board books are a physical object first and a text second. Judge them that way.

**Thick pages, genuinely thick.** Around one to two millimetres per page. Thin board books look sturdier than paperbacks and are not — they delaminate at the corners within weeks.

**Rounded corners.** Non-negotiable for a child who mouths and throws. Sharp board is stiff enough to hurt.

**Reinforced spine, or no spine.** A stitched or glued spine on a board book is a weak point. Books with a folded, continuous page construction survive far better.

**Wipe-clean surfaces.** Board books get chewed, dropped in puddles, and wiped with whatever is to hand. A matte paper finish will mark permanently.

**Small dimensions.** A book larger than the child cannot be held by the child. Roughly fifteen to eighteen centimetres square is the practical maximum for a one-year-old to handle independently.

**Not too many pages.** Twelve to sixteen pages is plenty for a baby. Longer board books exceed the attention they will get and add weight.

## What makes a book readable to a baby

The text matters less than the pictures at first, but not in the way most people assume.

**High contrast and simple shapes.** Young babies respond to strong contrast. Black and white, or bold single-colour illustrations, work better than soft watercolour.

**One clear object per page.** Two things competing on a page means neither is named.

**Faces.** Babies are drawn to faces more than to any other image, which is why the simplest possible face illustration outperforms an intricate animal for months.

**A rhythm you can read aloud.** Books with rhyme and repetition hold attention longer, and are easier for an adult to read fifty times.

**Something to point at.** Lift-the-flap, texture patches, holes to poke. Interactive board books engage a baby physically, which is what makes them work at eight months when a static picture does not.

**Avoid heavy branding and character licensing.** They add recognition but not language, and the books tend to be thinner on both.

## The board books worth having

Rather than specific titles, which date, these are the formats that consistently work.

**A high-contrast black-and-white book** for the first months, when visual acuity is still developing.

**A face book** — photographs or illustrations of babies and faces. Often the first book a baby responds to deliberately.

**A lift-the-flap book.** The physical action is the hook, and it survives until about two, at which point the flaps will need repairing.

**A texture or touch-and-feel book.** Same principle, different sense.

**A rhyme book** with a strong read-aloud rhythm. The one you can recite from memory is the one that will be requested nightly.

**A simple vocabulary book** with one object and one word per page. This is where first words and pointing connect, usually around twelve months.

**A bedtime book.** Predictable, quiet, and associated with a routine rather than an activity.

**A numbers and shapes book** with no more than five of each. Counting books with twenty objects on a page are for older children and are actively unhelpful at first.

## The "first learning toy" category, honestly assessed

Toys marketed for early learning deserve more scepticism than books.

**What is worth buying:**

- **Shape sorters with few shapes and forgiving holes.** Genuinely self-correcting, genuinely skill-building.
- **Nesting cups and stacking toys.** Size seriation, cheap, endlessly reusable.
- **A set of chunky first blocks** with letters or numbers on some faces. Used as blocks first and as letters much later.
- **A simple picture card set** if you like using them. Cards are portable and good for travel, though they are not necessary.
- **A counting and sorting set** — farm animals, vehicles, or fruit — used for counting from about two and sorting from two and a half.

**What is not worth buying:**

- **Electronic learning tablets and talking toys.** They ask a question, mark the answer, and remove the child's need to reason. They also tend to be used in a way that replaces adult interaction rather than supporting it.
- **Alphabet and number "programmes".** There is no evidence that early letter drilling produces an advantage, and it displaces play that does more.
- **Flashcards for babies.** Babies learn language from faces, voices and objects, not from cards.
- **Toys marketed with claims about IQ or reading age.** Claims of that kind are a reliable sign to look elsewhere.
- **Large multipacks of "educational" plastic.** Volume is not learning.

## The thing that matters more than the toys

The measurable benefit of shared reading comes from the interaction: pointing, naming, waiting for a response, following the child's interest rather than the text, and reading the same book repeatedly.

This means the highest-value "learning toy" for a child under three is an adult with time and a small stack of well-chosen books. No product substitutes for it, and several of the products marketed as substitutes actively get in the way.

Two practical notes that genuinely help. First, follow the child's attention rather than the page order — if they want to stay on the dog for four minutes, stay on the dog. Second, keep the books where the child can reach them, in a low basket rather than a high shelf. Accessible books get brought to adults; stored books get read to order.

## Building the collection without spending much

Library board books and second-hand books are as good as new ones, with two caveats: check for chewed corners and stuck-together pages, and check against current safety recalls if the book has any attached parts.

A collection of ten to fifteen board books is plenty for the first two years. More than that and the child is choosing from an unmanageable pile and the same three books get read anyway.

## The bottom line

Buy ten to fifteen thick-paged, rounded-corner board books: high contrast, faces, a lift-the-flap, a texture book, a rhyme, a first vocabulary book, and a bedtime book. Add a shape sorter, nesting cups and a set of chunky blocks as the first learning toys.

Skip the electronic learning products for the first three years, and spend the difference on board books and time.
