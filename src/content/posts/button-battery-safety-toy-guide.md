A two-year-old is playing with a musical book. The battery cover has a screw, but the screw has been missing for weeks and nobody noticed, because the cover still sat flush and the book still worked. That is the entire hazard in one sentence: a cover that stays in place right up until the moment it does not.

Button and coin batteries are not a choking risk in the ordinary sense. They are a chemical burn risk, and the clock is short. Most of what you need to do about them happens before anything goes wrong.

## Why a swallowed button battery is different

When a coin cell lodges in a child's esophagus, it does not simply sit there. The battery completes a circuit through the moist tissue around it and generates hydroxide ions at the negative terminal. Those ions burn. The result is an alkaline chemical burn and liquefactive necrosis, tissue destroyed in a way that can keep progressing even after the battery is out.

The official warning language is blunt: swallowing a button or coin battery can cause internal chemical burns **in as little as two hours**. Not two days. Two hours.

Two more facts explain why this is so frequently missed. Between 2000 and 2020, US poison centers logged **more than 70,000 button battery ingestions**, and the injury rate rose **more than fivefold** over that period. The driver was not an increase in batteries generally but the **lithium coin cells 20 mm and larger**, which are the size most likely to lodge in a small child's esophagus. And **nearly half of ingestion events are unwitnessed** — the child swallows the cell, shows no obvious symptom, and the two-hour window starts running in silence.

That is the reason the correct response to a suspected swallowed battery is never to watch and wait.

## What the law now requires

Battery compartments on consumer products in the United States are governed by **Reese's Law**, Public Law 117-171, signed on **August 16, 2022**. The implementing rule is **16 CFR part 1263**, which references **ANSI/UL 4200A-2023** as the mandatory standard.

The core requirement is worth understanding, because it tells you exactly what a correct compartment looks like. A compartment holding a replaceable button or coin battery must require either a tool or **at least two independent and simultaneous hand actions** to open. After use-and-abuse testing, the battery must not come out. The packaging must carry a warning, the product itself must carry a warning where feasible, and the instructions must include one.

The dates matter if you are buying older stock or from import channels. The general consumer product requirement took effect **October 23, 2023**, and the children's product requirement on **December 20, 2023**, with enforcement discretion running to **March 19, 2024**. Battery packaging labeling requirements took effect **September 21, 2024**, and special child-resistant battery packaging under **16 CFR 1700.15** has applied since **February 12, 2023**.

Two definitions are easy to get wrong. A button or coin battery is a single cell whose **diameter is greater than its height** — a flat disc. **Zinc-air batteries fall outside part 1263.** And there is an exemption that surprises people: **toys complying with 16 CFR part 1250**, meaning **ASTM F963**, whose section 4.25 covers batteries, are **exempt from the section 2 requirements of part 1263**. Toys are not unregulated here. Their battery risk is handled by the toy standard instead.

The official warning phrase is worth memorizing exactly as written: **KEEP new and used batteries OUT OF REACH OF CHILDREN**. Used matters as much as new. A spent cell still burns.

## Checking a battery compartment at home

This takes under a minute per toy, and it catches almost everything.

- **Look for the fastener.** A screw, a captive screw, or a latch that needs two motions. If the compartment opens with a single push and slide, it does not meet the current requirement.
- **Check that the screw is still there.** A missing fastener is the most common failure mode on toys in active use, and it is invisible until the cover shifts.
- **Press the cover.** A cover that flexes or lifts at one corner is on its way out.
- **Drop it once, deliberately, onto a soft surface.** Then check the cover again. Abuse testing is exactly what the standard is built around.
- **Shake it and listen.** If you hear a loose cell rattling inside, retire the toy rather than repairing it.
- **Check your spares, too.** Loose cells in a drawer, a handbag, or a remote control are a common source, and they are not protected by any compartment at all.

If a toy fails any of those checks, stop using it. Tape is not a fix. Tape is removed by children.

## If you suspect a battery has been swallowed

Do not wait for symptoms, and do not try to make the child vomit. Make contact with a professional immediately.

- **Poison Help: 1-800-222-1222**, free and available 24 hours a day.
- **National Battery Ingestion Hotline: 800-498-8666.**
- **CPSC: 800-638-2772** for product reports, and **SaferProducts.gov** to file an injury report.

One narrow piece of guidance circulates widely and is routinely applied incorrectly: honey. Honey is not a treatment and not a substitute for care. Per the National Capital Poison Center guidance, it applies only when **all** of the following are true — the battery is suspected to be a **lithium coin cell**, the child is **at least 12 months old**, the ingestion happened **within the last 12 hours**, the child **is able to swallow**, and **honey is available**. Under those conditions the dose is **10 mL (2 teaspoons) every 10 minutes, up to 6 times**, and it is given **only on the way to medical care**.

Two absolutes follow. Honey must **never substitute for or delay** endoscopic removal, and **do not induce vomiting**. Children **under 12 months must not be given honey** at all.

## Sizing: the coin comparison

If you find a cell and are unsure whether it is in the dangerous size range, compare it to coins you already have. A **US penny is 19 mm** across and a **US nickel is 21 mm**. Coin cells **20 mm and larger** are the ones most often responsible for esophageal injury, because that is the size that lodges in a small child's esophagus rather than passing through.

The practical implication is slightly counterintuitive: the largest, most obviously oversized cells are the ones to handle most carefully. A small cell may pass. A nickel-sized one may not.

## Buying choices that reduce the risk

Most of the risk reduction is in what you own, not in what you do daily. A toy whose battery compartment is closed with a **captive screw** removes the failure mode you cannot supervise.

For toys that light up or play sound, [screw-secured battery compartment toys](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=screw+secured+battery+compartment+toys&tag=cnxixi-20) are a reasonable starting filter, though you should still open the box and check the fastener yourself before handing anything over. In households with both older and younger children, storage matters as much as the toy: a [battery storage box](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=battery+storage+box&tag=cnxixi-20) keeps spares out of reach of the child who cannot read a warning label.

For a child under three, the cleanest choice is a toy that does not use replaceable button cells at all. Many [musical toys for toddlers](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=musical+toys+for+toddlers&tag=cnxixi-20) either run on sealed internal batteries or make sound with no electronics, which removes the hazard instead of managing it.

## What we would not buy

- **Toys with a press-and-slide battery door and no fastener**, regardless of what the age label says.
- **Greeting cards, key fobs, and small electronics** sold alongside toys but not regulated as toys. These are frequently the actual culprit in case reports.
- **Loose coin cells in bulk without child-resistant packaging.** The packaging requirement exists for exactly this reason.
- **Any toy whose battery cover has been repaired with tape, glue, or a rubber band.** A repaired cover fails again.

## The bottom line

A button battery is a two-hour emergency, and roughly half the time nobody sees the swallow happen. Check every battery compartment for a screw or a two-action latch, check the fastener itself, and retire anything that fails rather than repairing it. Keep new and used cells out of reach, in a container rather than a drawer.

If you suspect a swallow, call Poison Help at **1-800-222-1222** or the National Battery Ingestion Hotline at **800-498-8666** immediately. Do not wait for symptoms and do not induce vomiting. Honey has a narrow, specific role and no role at all for infants, and it never substitutes for or delays endoscopy.
