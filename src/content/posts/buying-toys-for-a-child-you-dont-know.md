The invitation says the child is turning six. You have met the child once, at a school gate, and what you remember is a green coat. This is the situation most gift advice ignores, and it is the situation most gift buyers are actually in.

The good news is that you do not need to know the child. You need to know the constraints, and there are only a few that matter: the child's age band, the ages of the other children in the house, and how much space the family has. Everything past that is taste, and taste is something a child is allowed to have independently of you.

## Start with what you actually know

Before you open a browser, write down four things.

The child's age in years rather than in school grade, since grades vary by country and by birthday.

Whether there are younger siblings. A gift a toddler can reach and open is a gift that will be tested by the toddler.

Whether the family lives in a house or a flat. Large gifts are generous in one and a burden in the other.

Whether the parents have said anything. "We have too many toys" is a complete instruction, not a hint to be politely ignored.

Those four answers eliminate most bad gifts before you have looked at a single product page.

## How to read an age label

Age labels are written for two different purposes and they get confused constantly.

The first purpose is safety. A marking such as 3+ is usually a statement about small parts: it means the set contains something that would not pass the small parts test and must be kept away from children under three. The reference gauge is the small parts cylinder under 16 CFR 1501.4, and if a piece fits entirely inside it, that piece is a choking hazard for that age group. That is not a marketing decision. It is the line the law draws.

The second purpose is marketing. A band such as ages 8 to 12 is usually describing who the toy was designed to appeal to, and a bright nine-year-old may be bored by it within an afternoon. Marketing bands are a suggestion. Safety markings are not.

So read the label twice, once for the safety marking, which is binding, and once for the play-age band, which is only a starting point.

## The gifts that almost never go wrong

Some categories carry very little risk of a miss, and they share three properties: they get used up, they grow with the child, or they need no knowledge of the child's current interests.

- Art supplies. Consumable, usable across a wide age range, and welcome in any house with a flat surface.
- Books. A picture book spans a broad band, and a duplicate book is not a disaster.
- Building pieces that add to an existing collection. The generic version is usually safer than a themed one, because it can join whatever the child already has.
- A family game that plays in a short time. Gifts a sibling can join get used more.
- Experience and consumable gifts such as a museum membership, a class or a voucher. Nothing new to store.

Notice how many of those are not really toys. That is not a coincidence.

## The gifts that almost always go wrong

The failure pattern is equally consistent.

Loud gifts for a child you do not know. You are not the one who will hear it at bedtime.

Gifts that require batteries or an app in a house whose device situation you cannot see. A toy that needs a phone is a gift with homework attached.

Large plastic activity centres for a small flat. The parent has to find somewhere to put it.

Anything themed around a franchise you are guessing at. Children are specific and current about these things in a way adults are not.

Anything that assumes a skill level. A complex model kit for a child who has never built one produces an afternoon of frustration and a parent who has to finish it.

Anything with many small pieces for a household with a toddler in it. The gift can be correctly labelled and still be a problem in that particular house.

## The small parts rule in one line

If a part fits entirely inside the small parts cylinder used under 16 CFR 1501.4, it is not appropriate for a child under three. That is the whole rule. A toilet roll tube at home is a rough approximation, and a more generous one than the real gauge, so do not use it to overrule a label.

## Button batteries and magnets

Two hazards deserve their own mention, because both are easy to miss in the ten seconds of handing over a gift.

A swallowed coin or button battery can cause internal chemical burns in as little as two hours, which makes it a genuine emergency rather than a wait-and-see. Where a toy contains one, the rules require the compartment to need a tool, or at least two independent and simultaneous hand actions, to open. Screw-fixed compartments are the standard to insist on. If a gift you are about to give has a taped or press-fit battery cover, that is a reason to choose something else.

Magnet sets are regulated so that magnetic toys must have a flux index below 50 kG²mm². Swallowed magnets can attract each other through body tissue and cause perforation, obstruction, or worse. For a child in the age band where everything goes in the mouth, this is not a category to guess at.

One more, easily forgotten: underinflated or broken balloons are a choking hazard, and children under eight should be kept away from uninflated balloons and their fragments. A gift set decorated with balloons is a poor fit for a house with a baby in it.

## When you know nothing at all

A few options work when you have no information and no way of getting any.

A gift card to a bookshop or a general retailer is not lazy. It transfers the decision to the person who has the information.

A consumable wins. Art paper, modelling material and craft kits get used up, which means they do not add permanently to a toy collection.

And asking the parent is allowed. Most parents are pleased to be asked, and one short question removes all of the guessing.

## Delivery details that matter

Open the packaging before wrapping if you can do it without spoiling the surprise. Hard clamshell plastic, wire ties and the small desiccant sachets that travel with them are hazards of their own, and a parent will not thank you for adding them to the pile on a birthday morning.

Keep the receipt. A gift receipt is a kindness in a category where duplicates and sizing problems are routine.

If the item was bought second-hand, check it against the recall list before giving it. Federal law prohibits selling recalled products, and that applies to resale and to hand-me-downs as well, unless the remedy set out in the recall notice has been carried out.

## What we would not buy

- Anything with a coin cell battery and a cover that is not secured with a screw.
- Loose, separable magnets in a set for a child young enough to mouth things.
- Uninflated balloons for a household with children under eight.
- A toy box with a self-latching lid or no ventilation holes.
- Anything you would have to explain to the parent in more than one sentence.

## The bottom line

You do not need to know the child to give a good gift. You need the age in years, whether there is a toddler in the house, and how much room the family has. Then choose something consumable, something that grows with the child, or something the parent has asked for.
