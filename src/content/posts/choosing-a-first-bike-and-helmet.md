Most first bikes are bought one size too large. The reasoning is understandable, since children grow and a bike that lasts an extra season feels like better value, and it produces the same outcome nearly every time. A child who cannot put both feet flat on the ground while sitting on the saddle will not learn to ride. They will learn that the bike tips over, and that tipping over is what bikes do.

Two decisions matter more than the frame colour. Does the bike fit the child today, and what is the helmet actually certified to do? The second question has a real answer printed inside the shell, and most parents never look at it.

## Size is a fit problem, not an age problem

Age labels on bikes are a rough sorting convenience, not a measurement. Two children of the same age can differ by a head in height and by considerably more in leg length.

The test takes ten seconds and you can run it in the shop. Sit the child on the saddle with the bike held upright. Both feet should reach the ground flat, not on tiptoe. On a first pedal bike, the child should also be able to touch the ground with their feet while the pedals are at their lowest point. If they cannot, the bike is too big, and training wheels are not a correction. They are a way of postponing the problem until the child is heavier and the fall is longer.

A balance bike removes the whole difficulty. With no pedals there is nothing to reach for, the saddle can be set low enough for flat feet, and the child learns the two skills that actually matter, which are steering and balancing. Pedals can arrive later on a properly sized frame. Buying up a size so the bike lasts longer is a very common way parents get this wrong.

## A bike is not covered by the toy safety standard

Worth knowing before you compare specifications. ASTM F963-23, the mandatory US toy safety standard, covers toys intended for children under 14, and it explicitly excludes bicycles, along with non-powder guns, kites and playground equipment. Bicycles fall under their own set of rules instead.

That means you cannot look for a single certification mark on the frame that does for a bike what a sticker does for a helmet. What you can do is inspect the parts that fail in use. Are the brake levers small enough for a child's hand to reach and squeeze? Do the grips stay put when pulled? Are there sharp edges anywhere on the frame, the hardware, or the ends of the handlebar? Is the chain enclosed? Those checks do more practical work than a specification sheet.

## What the helmet sticker actually tells you

Every bicycle helmet sold in the United States must meet CPSC 16 CFR Part 1203, which has been mandatory since 1999. The standard tests a single high-energy impact: a drop from about two metres onto steel anvils, with headform acceleration not exceeding 300 g. It also covers retention system strength, peripheral vision, and a roll-off stability test that checks whether the helmet stays on the head in a fall.

So a CPSC sticker means the helmet passed a bicycle standard. That is the whole of what it means, and it is worth reading the next section before you buy a second helmet for a scooter or a skateboard.

## CPSC 1203 versus ASTM F1492

This distinction matters as soon as a child moves from a bike to a skateboard, a scooter or roller skates.

The CPSC 1203 test is a single high-energy impact test. ASTM F1492 is the skateboarding and trick roller skating standard, and it tests multiple impacts, because a skate helmet is expected to take more than one hit in a session. ASTM F1447 is a separate standard for recreational bicycle helmets. ASTM F2032 covers BMX, and ASTM F1952 covers downhill mountain biking.

A helmet carrying only a CPSC bicycle sticker is not wrong for a scooter. CPSC has stated that tricycles and scooters have no separate helmet standard, so a bicycle helmet is the appropriate choice for both. But if the child is skating, look at the label before assuming a helmet was built for it. The only reliable signal is the certification sticker inside the shell.

## The gap nobody fills

There is no US federal standard for skateboard helmets at all. A helmet can be sold for skateboarding with no certification whatsoever, as long as it is not marketed for cycling. The word "skate" on the packaging is not a claim about testing.

If you want a helmet that has genuinely been tested against the skateboarding standard, the label inside needs to say ASTM F1492. Without it, you are buying a shell and hoping. The European EN 1078 standard covers cycling and roller sports and is broader in scope than CPSC 1203, but it is not what a helmet sold in the US is required to meet.

## Fit is not a preference

A loose helmet fails in precisely the situation it exists for, and the fix is not to tighten everything as far as it goes.

Worth knowing before you size one: CPSC states that helmets for children under five must cover a larger portion of the head than helmets for older riders. That is a design rule rather than a marketing line, and it is a good reason to check that a hand-me-down is a child shell and not a small adult one.

Four checks take a few seconds once you have the helmet on.

- The front edge should sit about two finger-widths above the eyebrows, not pushed back onto the crown.
- Only one finger should fit between the chin and the strap.
- The straps should form a V shape just below the ear.
- With the chin buckle undone, a gentle shake of the head should not move the helmet much at all.

If the shake test fails with the buckle open, tightening the chin strap is the wrong answer. The helmet is the wrong shape or the wrong size.

## When a helmet has to go

Replace a helmet after any impact. The foam crushes and stops absorbing energy, and the damage is usually not visible from the outside. A helmet that looks untouched after a fall may already be compromised, and you will not be able to tell by squeezing it.

Do not use a second-hand helmet of unknown history. It may have been dropped, left in a hot car, or crashed in, and none of that shows on it. Many manufacturers suggest replacing a helmet after about five years even with no impact, because the materials age.

## One rule that is not about riding

Take the helmet off before playground equipment and trees. There was a first US death from a bicycle helmet catching on playground equipment in 1999, and near-misses since then. The object that protects a rider becomes a hazard on a climbing frame.

The same logic runs in the other direction at the motorised end of the category. The AAP advises that children under 16 should not ride or operate a motorised e-scooter. If the child in question is moving toward wheels with a motor, that is a separate decision from a first pedal bike.

## What we would not buy

- A bike the child cannot stand over with both feet flat, bought on the argument that they will grow into it.
- A helmet without a CPSC sticker for cycling, or without an ASTM F1492 sticker if it is going to be used for skateboarding.
- A second-hand helmet whose interior label is missing or unreadable.
- Training wheels presented as a substitute for a bike that fits.
- Any helmet that needs the chin strap cranked tight to stop it sliding around the head.

## The bottom line

Buy the bike that fits this week rather than the bike that fits next spring. Use a balance bike before pedals and let the child's ability decide when they move up. Buy a helmet with a sticker you have read, in a shell sized for the child's age, and replace it after any impact.
