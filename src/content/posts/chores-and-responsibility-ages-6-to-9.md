## Chores are not "helping mom"

The phrase most families use is "come help me." It sounds warm, and it quietly makes the job your job. The American Academy of Pediatrics' health site frames it the other way: "Age-appropriate chores and responsibilities are an important part of a child's development." A six-year-old setting the table is not doing you a favor. He is doing the developmental work of his age.

The second half of the AAP's point is the part parents skip. "Helping to keep the home running smoothly boosts a child's self-esteem." The beneficiary is the child, not the kitchen. A tidier house is a side effect; the real output is a kid who has evidence, in his own hands, that he is useful.

The AAP notes that "young children are naturally eager helpers," and that "most preschool age kids can start pitching in with some simple household tasks." By six or seven that eagerness is usually still there. It is our systems, not the kids, that wear it down.

## What the AAP says children this age can actually do

The AAP publishes a rough age table. For five- to seven-year-olds it lists: "Make their own beds, set and clear the table for mealtimes, weed and rake leaves, make and pack school lunches, dust, put toys away, neaten bookshelves, put dirty clothes in a hamper, fill pet's food dish, empty wastebaskets, sweep floors, sort laundry, bring in mail/newspaper, water flowers, and wash plastic dishes in the sink."

For eight- to ten-year-olds it adds: "All of the above, plus vacuum, help make dinner, make their own snacks, take a pet for walk, put away their own laundry and put away groceries."

Eleven- and twelve-year-olds get "all of the above" again, plus bigger jobs like cleaning the kitchen and cooking a simple meal with supervision. The five-to-seven list is longer than most parents expect, and almost every item is something a child can genuinely finish. Treat it as a menu, not a syllabus.

## Don't expect perfection

Here is the AAP's most useful sentence, and the one that decides whether this survives the second week: "Just don't expect perfection; this is a learning process, and children will develop their skills over time. Praise your child's effort in each task, not the outcome. Small accomplishments lead to bigger ones."

A seven-year-old who folds a towel into a lumpy triangle has done the job. If you refold it in front of him, you have taught him that his effort does not count. Let the lumpy towel sit in the cupboard; the skill comes with repetition, not correction.

## What the CDC adds for 6-8 and 9-11

The CDC's positive parenting guidance splits this range in two. For six- to eight-year-olds the instruction is concrete: "Help your child develop a sense of responsibility—ask him to help with household tasks, such as setting the table." You are not delegating a chore; you are inviting a child into the running of the household.

For nine- to eleven-year-olds the framing shifts: "This is an important time for children to gain a sense of responsibility along with their growing independence." The same chores now carry a different meaning. A nine-year-old is not learning to set a table; she is practicing being someone the household can rely on.

## Praise the effort, not the outcome

The CDC is specific about where praise should land: "It's best to focus praise more on what your child does ('you worked hard to figure this out') than on traits she can't change ('you are smart')."

For the older end of the range the CDC offers a subtle upgrade: "Saying 'you must be proud of yourself' rather than simply 'I'm proud of you' can encourage your child to make good choices when nobody is around to praise her."

That last line is the whole game for nine- to eleven-year-olds. The goal is a child who does the dishes when you are not watching. Praise that points back at your approval does not build that; praise that points at her own standards does.

## Chores and allowance: a family choice, not a rule

Keep two ideas separate here, because a lot of advice blurs them.

The AAP's money guidance says: "Chores may be one way children can earn an allowance. If your children get an allowance, make the earnings matter." It adds that "assigning chores provides a sense of accomplishment and meaning to the money earned." For seven- to eight-year-olds the money page lists making their bed, bringing in the mail, setting or clearing the table, and helping fold and sort laundry. For nine- to eleven-year-olds it lists feeding pets, taking out the trash, helping bring in groceries, preparing simple meals with supervision, being responsible for homework, and watering the plants.

Notice the conditional. The AAP says chores *may* be one way to earn an allowance, and leaves the decision to each family. There is no pediatric recommendation that you must pay for chores. Some families pay for extra jobs on top of baseline chores; some pay nothing and treat chores as membership in the household. Both are defensible. Presenting a family choice as an expert mandate is not.

## Five ways this goes wrong

### You hand over a long list at once

The AAP is direct: with a school-age child, "introduce one new task at a time; a long list can be overwhelming." If you want three jobs done, start with one. Add the second only when the first happens without a reminder.

### You nag

"Badgering or scolding a child is not an effective method to get them to accept more responsibility; rewarding successes and providing encouragement is always much more effective." The AAP adds the line that changes the leverage: "Remember that electronic devices and screen time are privileges, not rights." If screens are the default, you have nothing left to negotiate with.

### There is no fixed rhythm

"Build structure and routines into your family life. This can give your child a greater chance of remembering their chores. Encourage them to do their chores at the same time each day." A chore that happens "sometime today" happens never. A [simple chore chart or sticker board](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=chore+chart+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) helps here, not as a reward system but as a visual reminder that keeps the whole family on task.

### Your expectations are unrealistic

"Sometimes parents may demand too much of their children or criticize the outcome of the chore. Or, they may assign too many responsibilities. Kids may feel overwhelmed and resist taking on any responsibilities at all." The failure mode is not a lazy child; it is a child who decided that nothing he does is good enough, so he stopped.

### "We're too busy"

Overscheduled families often decide the child is too busy for chores. The AAP calls this "a mistake": "Children need to have obligations and duties within the family, so they learn to accept responsibility." Chores are not what you cut when the calendar fills up.

## A short list on the fridge

Three items, not twenty. A seven-year-old can hold three things in mind, and finishing all three feels like something.

Pick a fixed slot. The AAP's own seven-year checkup guidance names jobs like "making their own bed, picking out clothes for school and setting the table," and notes that "learning to complete tasks like these can help your child grow a sense of accomplishment and become confident in their abilities."

Then hold a short family meeting once a week. The AAP suggests scheduling "regular family meetings to review your child's progress" and asking "for their ideas about chores and other responsibilities." Letting a child choose which job is his is the cheapest way to get buy-in.

A [kid-sized apron](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+apron&tag=cnxixi-20) signals that a kitchen job is really his, not a visit to yours, and a [basket for toys](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=toy+storage+basket&tag=cnxixi-20) gives the "put toys away" line a defined home, which makes tidying a task instead of a puzzle.

## The bottom line

The point of chores is not to move work off your plate. It is to give a child a place in the family where he can say "I did that." Start with three small jobs, fix them to the same time each day, introduce new ones one at a time, and praise the effort rather than the result. A shorter list you actually keep beats a long list you both abandon.
