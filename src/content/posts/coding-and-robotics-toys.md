Picture the second weekend of January. The robot that topped the December list is on a shelf with a flat battery, one missing wheel and a phone app nobody has opened since the first week. The child liked it. The child is now building a bridge out of plain blocks on the floor beside it.

That scene is the category in miniature. Coding and robotics toys are not a scam, and some of them are genuinely good. But the boxes describe benefits the pediatric evidence does not yet support, and the features that keep a child coming back are rarely the ones printed largest.

## The claim on the box is ahead of the evidence

The American Academy of Pediatrics set out its position in a 2019 clinical report, Selecting Appropriate Toys for Young Children in the Digital Era, published in Pediatrics, volume 143, issue 1, as e20183348. The report looks at the shift from traditional toys to digital and virtual ones, and its central finding is the lack of evidence for similar benefits in child development.

That sentence is careful, and it is worth reading slowly. It does not say digital toys harm children. It says the developmental benefits claimed for them have not been shown to match what traditional toys do.

The report's first author put it more plainly: "Research tells us that the best toys need not be flashy or expensive or come with an app." A co-author added: "The best toys are those that support parents and children playing, pretending and interacting together."

So when a box promises that a robot teaches programming, the honest reading is that the promise runs ahead of the evidence. The robot may still be a good toy. It is just not a curriculum.

## Three questions that replace the box copy

If the claimed benefit is unproven, judge the object instead. Three questions do most of the work.

1. Does it still do something with the phone switched off? A robot that only moves, speaks or lights up through an app is a phone accessory in a plastic shell, and it stops existing when the app is retired or the tablet is in another room.
2. What is the age number actually telling you? On this product type it is a safety statement first, which a later section covers.
3. Will the child pick it up again in the second week? The honest answer usually comes down to how much the child can do without an adult.

The first question separates a toy from a screen. There is a real category of [screen-free coding robots for kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=screen+free+coding+robot+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) that takes its instructions from buttons on the device itself, and those are the ones worth a second look. A toy that cannot run without an app is a subscription to someone else's software with a plastic body attached.

## "Educational" on the label, and what it usually means

The word is unregulated in this context, and the pediatric advice is blunt about it.

The AAP's HealthyChildren guidance says: "Use caution when you see 'educational' on the label. The truth is most tablets, computer games, and apps advertised as 'educational' really aren't." It goes further: "Most 'educational' apps target memory skills, such as ABCs and shapes. These skills are only one part of school readiness. The skills young children really need to learn for success in school (and life) include impulse control, managing emotions, and creative, flexible thinking."

That is a useful list to hold against any box. If a coding toy's selling point is that a child memorises a sequence, ask what else the play is building. And keep the ceiling in view: the same guidance states that a certain toy is not necessary for your child to reach his or her next developmental milestone, and that there is no one app that will teach your child to read.

## Screen time, in the numbers the AAP gives

Most of these toys route through a screen for at least part of their use, so the screen limits apply to them.

The AAP report gives three: total screen time, television and computers included, of less than 1 hour a day from age 2; avoidance below 18 to 24 months, with video calls the exception; and, for children under 5, only developmentally appropriate computer or video games, ideally with a parent or caregiver alongside.

There is one more line worth knowing when a toy is sold for a very young child. The same guidance says: "Research suggests tablet-based toys may actually delay social development for infants and young children, because they don't include real-life facial expressions, gestures, and vocalizations."

The action is not complicated. If the main mode of a toy is a screen, it belongs inside those limits, and it is not the thing you hand a toddler to do alone.

## What each age band can actually use

The useful question for each age is not what the toy claims to teach but what the child can do unassisted.

### Three to five

Coding at this age means sequencing: do this, then that. A screen-free robot that follows a short line of button presses is the honest version of the idea, and a plain [construction and building set for kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=construction+and+building+set+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) does more for the same age than most app-linked robots, because a child can play it alone and finish a game in one sitting. What does not work is anything that needs a phone to move, because the play then depends on a parent surrendering a device.

### Five to seven

This is where building starts to matter as much as coding. A kit that has to be assembled before it can be programmed gives a child two tasks rather than one, and the assembly is the part that survives the novelty wearing off. Look for instructions a child can follow, not a build that only an adult can complete.

### Seven to ten

A [build and code robotics kit for kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=build+and+code+robotics+kit+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) finally fits here, and the test is what the child can change without help. A kit the child can alter, re-code and repair is reusable; one that only runs the preloaded demonstration is a model, not a toy.

## An age grade is a safety grade

The single most misread number in this aisle is the one after the plus sign.

A 3+ or 8+ age grade on a robot is a safety statement about small parts, sharp edges and the battery compartment. It is not a difficulty rating, and it is not something to graduate past because your child is quick. The small-parts rule behind it is a hard line: small parts are not for children under three, and a clever two-year-old is still a two-year-old where a choke hazard is concerned.

Read the grade as a floor for the product, not a ceiling for the child. If the number is higher than your child's age, the answer is to wait, not to supervise harder.

## Button batteries and high-powered magnets

Two components deserve their own check before the toy comes home.

The AAP guidance is direct: "Avoid toys with button batteries or high-powered magnets." A button battery swallowed by a young child is a medical emergency, and small high-powered magnets can attract each other through tissue.

The response is a ten-second inspection rather than a decision to avoid the whole category. Check that the battery compartment closes and is secured with a screw, that no magnet is small enough to be prised out, and that the toy is kept away from children under three.

## What the injury data actually says

It is worth seeing where toy injuries happen, because the numbers point at ordinary moments rather than exotic products.

In the CPSC's report for calendar year 2024, there were 267,100 toy-related emergency department visits across all ages, and 34 per cent of them involved children four and under. About 45 per cent of the injuries were to the head or face, and 95 per cent of patients were treated and released. The report is careful to note that a toy being associated with an incident is not the same as the toy causing a death.

The practical takeaway is unglamorous. Keep small pieces in a lidded box and out of reach of children under three, and sweep the floor rather than trusting that everything stayed on the rug.

## What we would not buy

- A robot that only works through a phone app, because the toy ends when the app does.
- A coding toy marketed for children of 2 or 3, because the age grade is a safety line rather than a difficulty setting.
- A tablet presented as a coding curriculum for a preschooler.
- A kit with a sealed battery compartment that cannot be checked, or with loose magnets inside.
- Anything bought on the promise of a skill the evidence does not show it teaches.

## The bottom line

Buy the coding toy that still works when the wifi is off, and read the age grade as a safety statement rather than a challenge to beat. The AAP's 2019 clinical report found a lack of evidence that digital and virtual toys deliver the developmental benefits claimed for them, and its advice is that the best toys need not be expensive or come with an app. Keep screen use inside the limits that report gives, treat "educational" as a label rather than a finding, and avoid toys with button batteries or high-powered magnets. A robot a child can use alone, without a phone, is the version that survives past the first week.
