A four-year-old can chant the numbers to twenty at speed and then set four forks for two people at dinner, in the same minute. The counting is real. The number is not attached to anything yet.

That gap is the whole subject of early maths. Reciting number words is a language skill, and it arrives early and easily. Knowing that "four" means this many objects, and that the pile in front of you is that many, is a different piece of knowledge and it takes longer to build.

## Counting words are not counting

Children learn the sequence the way they learn a song. Order and rhythm first, meaning later. A two-year-old can chant "one, two, three" with no idea what any of it refers to, and that is not a failure. It is the normal first step.

The developmental lists reflect this. At four, most children name some numbers and understand the idea of counting. That is genuinely all the list asks for. The idea, not the arithmetic.

The more specific numbers in the developmental literature are the ones parents quote at each other: counting to four at four years, and counting to ten at five. Those are the whole target for this age band, and they are smaller than most people expect.

Note what the five-year milestone says: most children can count ten or more things. Read the word "things" closely. It is about counting objects in front of them, which is a far more demanding job than reciting a sequence from memory.

## What the milestones actually measure

The four-year list includes naming some colours and some numbers, understanding the idea of counting, and understanding the idea of same and different. Read those together and a picture forms. The four-year-old is being asked to notice, match and group. They are not being asked to add.

The five-year list adds counting ten or more objects, and that is the ceiling for this whole age band. There is no addition on either list, and no numerals beyond copying a few.

This is why a drill on number cards fits the lists so badly. Recognising a digit is the thinnest layer of the subject, and it is the layer a child can pick up in a week at six.

## Four ideas that come before arithmetic

Almost everything a four or five-year-old needs from maths sits in four ideas.

- One-to-one correspondence: one object gets one number word, and you stop when the objects run out.
- Sorting and classifying: putting things into groups by a rule, and knowing whether a thing belongs or does not.
- Patterns: seeing that something repeats, and being able to say what comes next.
- Number sense: knowing which group is more, which is fewer, and when two groups are the same.

None of these need a worksheet. All of them need objects.

## One-to-one correspondence

This is the skill that makes the fork problem interesting. A child who counts to twenty but sets four forks for two people has not yet joined the number to the quantity. A child who hands out one plate per person has.

The toys that carry it are almost insultingly simple. One lid on each pot. One peg in each hole of a pegboard. One pom pom in each section of an ice-cube tray. One cup in front of each stuffed animal at tea.

Counting bears, sorting trays and stacking rings all place one object against one position, which is why they do more for a four-year-old than a poster of numbers does. ## Sorting and classifying

Sorting is maths with no numbers in it, and it is the workhorse of this age. Same and different, which is on the four-year list, is exactly the skill underneath it.

Anything can be sorted: by colour, by size, by shape, by what it is made of, by how many wheels it has. A tray of buttons, a bowl of mixed dried beans, a pile of toy vehicles. The question that moves it forward is "why did you put that one there?", because the rule a child is using is where the thinking becomes visible.

Blocks are the quiet champions here. Long and short, thick and thin, flat and square. A set of [wooden blocks](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=wooden+blocks+set+for+preschoolers&tag=cnxixi-20) does more maths across four years than most toys that are labelled as maths toys.

## Patterns

Pattern is the other place four-year-olds surprise people. Once a child can continue a red-blue-red-blue line, they have the first version of a rule that will still be running in algebra class a decade later.

Start with body and sound patterns, which need no materials at all. Clap, stamp, clap, stamp. Then move to objects: a lace beaded in two colours, a block wall built in a repeating stripe, toy animals lined up in an alternating row. Ask what comes next, and let a wrong answer stand while the child checks it against the row in front of them. ## Number sense: more, less, the same

Before a child can tell you what four plus one makes, they need to see that four is more than three without counting. That ability to read a small group at a glance is what makes a dice face readable, and it is built by comparing things casually.

Two piles of grapes at snack, two towers of blocks, two handfuls of stones from the garden. "Which has more?" and then "how do you know?" If a four-year-old consistently cannot say whether two groups are the same or different, that is one of the things the milestone list flags as a reason to talk to a doctor. The list is a monitoring tool, not a diagnosis.

## Board games, dice and the maths nobody labels as maths

Four-year-olds playing board or card games is on the milestone list, and a game with a dice is arithmetic in disguise. Moving one space per pip joins the number word, the quantity and a physical movement, which is the one-to-one idea again with a rule attached.

At this age the rules do bend. Four and five-year-olds can follow a rule-based game, but the rules often drift toward whichever child is running the show. That is normal. Enforcing the printed rule through tears defeats the purpose of playing. So play a short round, let the dice do the counting, and let the argument about rules be part of it. A [first board game with dice](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=first+board+games+for+4+year+olds+with+dice&tag=cnxixi-20) is a maths purchase that nobody has to call one.

## Why number cards and counting apps do so little

A flashcard asks a child to name a symbol. That is a reading task wearing a maths coat, and it is the one part of the subject a five-year-old will pick up cheaply later anyway.

A counting app usually does the work the child should be doing. The screen counts, the screen moves the objects, and the child watches. In the physical version of the same activity the child's own hand moves the bear into the cup, and that is where the correspondence gets built.

## What to skip

- Number cards and numeral posters used as a teaching plan for a four-year-old.
- Counting apps where the machine moves the objects.
- Workbooks with written sums, which ask for a skill two years away.
- Toys that only count to ten with no way to sort, match or compare.
- Buying a maths toy at all when a box of mixed buttons and an ice-cube tray will do more.

## The bottom line

Counting words to twenty is a party trick. The maths that matters at four and five is quieter: one thing matched to one thing, groups sorted by a rule, patterns continued, and piles compared by eye. Buy something that lets a child sort and match, add blocks, a game with dice, and an ice-cube tray from the kitchen.

Then ask "how do you know?" and hold the pause. The answer to that question is the actual lesson.
