The game looked right in the shop. It got played twice in January, once in March under mild protest, and then settled at the bottom of the cupboard. This happens in most houses, and the reason is almost never that the game is bad.

It is that the game failed one of three tests that no review ever mentions: how long the shortest possible game is, whether it works at the number of people who actually live in your house, and whether anyone has to sit out and watch. Those three explain more abandoned games than difficulty, theme, or quality ever will.

## Three gates, and the order to check them in

Every family game has to pass the same three gates, and they go in that order.

The first is time. A game has to fit the window you genuinely have, which is usually much shorter than the window you imagine you have.

The second is player count. A four-player game in a two-player house is a shelf ornament with nice art.

The third is elimination. If the game removes players before it ends, one child stops playing while the others continue, and that child will find something else to do.

Nothing about the box art, the review scores, or the theme will tell you whether a game passes. All three are checkable in about two minutes.

## The shortest game, not the average one

Boxes print an average play time, or a range. What matters is the bottom of that range, and even then you should discount it, because published times are measured with players who already know the rules. A game that says forty-five minutes will take well over an hour on the first evening and closer to forty once everyone knows it.

So the real question is not how long a typical game lasts. It is how long the fastest possible game is, because that is what you will get on a school night. If the shortest version is longer than the stretch between clearing the table and the youngest child's bedtime, the game will not get played, however good it is.

Short rounds also solve a problem that long games create. A game that can be played three times in an evening gives a child three chances to win, and three chances to lose without it defining the whole night.

## Your real table size

Count the people who will actually sit down, not the people who could. In most houses that number is smaller than the family, because one adult is usually clearing up, and one child has lost interest by the time the box is open.

This is where two-player games quietly outperform four-player games. If one parent plays with one child at a time, a good [two-player card game for kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=two+player+card+games+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) will be opened dozens of times a year, while a four-player board game waits for a night when everyone is free and willing at the same moment. Those nights are rare. A deck of cards travels in a bag, sets up in ten seconds, and works on a train.

There is also a category of game that claims to play with anywhere from two to six people and is genuinely good at neither end. If a listing says "two to six players," look for a review that says the game actually works at two, because many do not.

## Does anyone sit out

Player elimination is the single most reliable way to end a family game night early. In adult groups, being knocked out is mildly annoying. With an eight-year-old, it ends the evening, because a child who is out of the game has nothing left to do and no reason to stay at the table.

Cooperative games avoid the problem entirely by making everyone lose together, which is a particular advantage in the six-to-nine range, where losing is still genuinely hard. A shared loss is much easier to handle than a personal one, and it gives the adult something to debrief afterwards. If you have not looked at them before, [cooperative board games for families](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=cooperative+board+games+for+families&tag=cnxixi-20) are a good place to start, because almost every title in that group is built to keep everyone in the game until the end.

If the family prefers competition, look for games where players fall behind but keep taking turns. Being in last place is a motivation. Being out is not playing.

## The rules read-out is the real cost of entry

A twelve-page rulebook is not a game problem, it is a logistics problem. Someone has to read it, someone has to explain it, and someone has to answer questions for the first two rounds, and that person is usually the adult who least wants to spend their evening teaching.

The games that survive in a busy house tend to be the ones where a first turn can be taken after two sentences of explanation, and the rest is learned by playing. That is a much better predictor of use than any rating. If the rules cannot be summarised standing up, the game needs a dedicated evening, and dedicated evenings are scarce.

## A rough age map

At six to eight, look for short rounds with enough luck that a younger player can win sometimes. Games with a strong memory or observation element often work better across ages than games built on reading and arithmetic, because the younger child is not automatically behind.

At nine to twelve, strategy, bluffing, and negotiation come in properly, and children in this band will happily accept a longer game if it is genuinely interesting. A game with trading and persuasion is often at its best here, though be prepared for the persuasion to be ruthless.

For a mixed-age table, pick for the youngest player and let the older ones play with a handicap or a secondary objective. Very few games handle a four-year age gap well on their own, which is why it is worth browsing [family board games for four players](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=family+board+games+for+4+players&tag=cnxixi-20) with the age on the box read as a floor rather than a promise.

## Small pieces, and the toddler in the room

Board games are an underrated small-parts problem, because they get played on the floor and tokens end up under the sofa. The cylinder test under 16 CFR 1501.4 is the rule to know: if a token drops in whole, it is a small part, and small parts are not for children under three.

That matters even if the game is a birthday present for a nine-year-old, because a nine-year-old often has a two-year-old sibling. Count the loose pieces at the end of a session the way you would for any toy with a toddler in the house, and keep the box somewhere a small child cannot reach, rather than in the bottom of a low cupboard.

## Games as a boundary, not a lesson

One of the most practical uses of a board game in a house with an older child is as a replacement for a screen hour, which is a more honest framing than treating it as an educational activity. It does not need to teach anything to be worth the shelf space.

The guidance here is worth knowing. For children aged six and over, the American Academy of Pediatrics does not set a single hourly limit, and instead recommends clear and consistent boundaries around time, content, and location, ideally agreed with the child in the form of a Family Media Plan. It also recommends screen-free zones such as bedrooms and the dining table, and screen-free times such as mealtimes and the hour before bed.

A game on the dinner table covers a screen-free zone and a screen-free time simultaneously, and the evidence about household rules is interesting: rules covering balance, content, and talking about what is being watched are associated with better outcomes than rules that only restrict the number of minutes. Separately, children who spend more than four hours a day on screens are more likely to show higher anxiety, depression, and behavioural problems, with the association strongest where screen time is displacing sleep and physical activity. None of that requires a lecture. It just argues for keeping a couple of games within reach of the table.

## What we would not buy

- Games that claim two to six players but are dull below four.
- Anything built around knocking players out, however short the rounds are.
- Games with a long setup before the first turn, which is a different cost from a long game.
- Speed and reflex games at a mixed-age table, where the older players win every time for reasons nobody can fix.
- Licensed games tied to a film, bought because the child likes the film. That affection rarely survives the third play.

## The bottom line

Buy for the shortest game, the real number of players, and a table where nobody gets knocked out. Prefer cooperative games or a good two-player card game, keep the rules short enough to explain standing up, and put it on the table at the hour a screen would otherwise take.

A game that gets played three times a week beats a better game that gets played twice a year.
