Every August the money goes to the wrong place. New backpack, new pencils, a lunchbox with the right character on it, and then the first Monday arrives and the actual problem turns out to be that nobody can get out the door before eight o'clock without shouting.

The backpack is a purchase. The morning is a routine, and routines are built in the two weeks before school starts, not on the first day.

## What the first week actually decides

The American Academy of Pediatrics puts sleep first in its back-to-school guidance for families, ahead of supplies and schedules. Its advice is to help a child adjust to earlier bedtimes a week or two before the new school year starts, and to set a consistent bedtime and stick with it every night.

Note the word every. A bedtime that moves by an hour on the weekend is a suggestion, not a bedtime. The reason the pediatricians lead with sleep is blunt: not getting enough sleep is linked with lower academic achievement, as well as higher rates of absenteeism and tardiness. Read that as what a bad morning routine costs over a term.

So the first practical move is not buying anything. It is deciding, two weeks out, what time lights go off, and holding that line through the last stretch of the holidays.

## How much sleep a school-age child actually needs

The CDC's sleep guidance, drawing on the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, gives a school-age child between 6 and 12 years old a recommended 9 to 12 hours a night.

That range is a recommendation for that age band, not a diagnosis. A child who sleeps at the low end and functions well is not sick. But it is useful as a planning tool: count backwards from the time the alarm has to ring, and you get the time the lights have to go off. Do that arithmetic once, before the term starts, and the nightly argument largely disappears, because the number is not yours to negotiate.

Screen time is the part that quietly eats the margin. The AAP recommends keeping all screens, including televisions, computers, laptops, tablets and phones, out of children's bedrooms, especially at night, and turning off all screens at least 60 minutes before bedtime to prevent sleep disruption. The CDC's version is to turn devices off at least 30 minutes before bed. If you only manage one of those, take the hour. The easiest way to enforce it is that charging happens in a kitchen or hallway, not a bedroom.

## Rehearse the first week like a fire drill

The AAP is direct that many children get nervous about new situations, including changing to a new school, classroom or teacher, and that it can be helpful to rehearse heading into the new situation.

Rehearsal here means something ordinary. Take your child to visit the new school or classroom before the first day, attend any orientation you can, and walk the route. If you can, go a few days early just to play on the playground, so the building stops being a rumour.

Then ask. If your child seems nervous, ask what they are worried about and work through it together, rather than reassuring in the abstract. "You'll be fine" answers nothing. "Which part worries you, the bus or finding the classroom?" gives you a problem you can solve on a Tuesday afternoon instead of a mystery on a Monday morning. Finish by pointing out the fun parts, so the week has a shape other than dread.

## The twenty minutes before the door

Most morning fights are not about the morning. They are about decisions being made at the worst possible moment, by people who are not awake.

Move those decisions to the night before. Clothes get picked and laid out. The bag gets packed and left by the door. Lunch gets assembled, or at least planned, the evening before. None of that requires a system; it requires doing at nine at night what you would otherwise be doing at seven in the morning with a clock running.

On the first day, the AAP's advice is to get there early to cut down on unnecessary stress. Arriving with ten minutes to spare buys you a calm entry into a room full of strangers.

A few pieces of gear genuinely help. A [simple kids alarm clock](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+alarm+clock&tag=cnxixi-20) in the room, so waking up is something the clock does rather than something you do to them. A [water bottle that seals properly](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+water+bottle&tag=cnxixi-20) and a [lunch box that opens without help](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+lunch+box&tag=cnxixi-20) each remove one small morning failure mode. Everything else in the school aisle is optional.

If your child is truly stuck, the AAP describes a step-wise return that is worth knowing about even if you never need it. On day one, they might get up and get dressed, and you drive them by the school so they get some feel for it before going home together. On day two, they might go for just half a day, or a class or two. On day three, they return for a full day.
## When it is anxiety, not a bad mood

There is a difference between a child who is grumpy on a Monday and a child who regularly resists going to school. The AAP describes school avoidance, sometimes called school refusal or school phobia, as a term used when a child regularly resists going to school.

The physical picture is specific. These children may experience headaches, stomachaches, hyperventilation, nausea or dizziness, symptoms that happen most often on school days and are usually absent on weekends, and when they are examined by a doctor, no physical illness is detected or diagnosed.

That pattern, not the complaint itself, is the thing to notice. It does not make you a diagnostician; it means you describe the pattern to the pediatrician instead of arguing with the symptom.

If it is avoidance, the pediatricians are unusually clear about the stance: acknowledge that you understand your child's concerns, but insist on their immediate return to school, because the longer they stay home, the more difficult their eventual return will be. That is the one hard consequence in this section, and it comes with a direction attached: back to school now, not after the feelings settle.

Three things make that stance easier to hold. First, let your child know that school attendance is required by law, so the decision is not a matter of family opinion. Second, be extra firm on school mornings, when complaints peak, and keep discussion of symptoms to a minimum, including not asking how they feel; the AAP's test is that if a child is well enough to be up and around the house, they are well enough to attend. Third, if they do stay home, keep them safe but give no special treatment, meaning no screen time privileges, no special snacks and no visitors.

And there is a threshold for asking for help. If your child's school avoidance lasts more than one week, the AAP says you and your child may need professional assistance to deal with it. Talk to the teacher, the principal and the school nurse as well.
## What to skip

- Buying the whole school list in July. Supplies are not what goes wrong in week one; the routine is.
- Making up a bedtime the night before term starts. The AAP's advice is to shift it a week or two early.
- A phone or tablet in the bedroom. The guidance is to keep screens out of bedrooms, especially at night.
- Turning the first morning into a photo shoot. If you want to get there early, being early means being early.
- Treating a nervous child as a discipline problem. The AAP's framing is to rehearse, not to push harder.
- Calling a week of school refusal a phase. One week is when the pediatric guidance says to bring in help.

## The bottom line

The first week of school is not an adjustment period that happens to you. It is a sequence you can rehearse: bedtime moved earlier a week or two out, the route and the classroom visited before day one, clothes and bags and lunches sorted the night before, and a plan for the child who cannot yet walk through the door.

None of that lives in a shopping cart. Buy a bag they can open and a clock that does the waking, then spend your effort on the twenty minutes before the door, because that is the part of the year you actually get to build.
