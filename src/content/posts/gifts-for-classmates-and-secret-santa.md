The class list arrives in a message with twenty-eight names and an instruction to bring something small for the exchange on the last day of term. You know maybe four of the children. One of the names is spelled in a way you have never seen before, and the note says the budget is small and please stick to it.

That is the whole brief, and it is enough. Buying for classmates is a different job from buying for a child you know, and once you accept the constraints it gets considerably easier.

## The three conditions you are shopping under

There is a budget cap, which is real and usually enforced by school policy. You are buying several gifts at once, which changes what a good option looks like. And you are buying for children you do not know, which removes anything personal from the list.

Those three conditions are the entire design problem. A gift that ignores any one of them either blows the budget, takes a week to assemble, or lands badly because you guessed wrong about a child you have never met.

## Narrow the shelf with three questions

Three questions take almost everything off the shelf, and they are all versions of the same idea: reduce the chance of being wrong.

1. Is there an age floor, and does this child clear it?
2. Does it need batteries, or setting up, or an adult?
3. Does it come in a size, a colour, or a flavour that has to be guessed correctly?

Anything that fails one of those tests is out. What is left is a short list, and it is the list that works for a stranger's child.

## The age floor is not adjustable

A school class mixes children of the same grade, but the useful line is not the grade, it is the age. Children under three should not have small parts or small balls within reach, which matters mainly at the nursery and reception end of a school, and it is the reason a cheap bag of tiny figures is a poor class gift even when every other condition is met.

The scale of the issue is worth knowing rather than fearing. Of an estimated 267,100 toy-related emergency department visits in calendar year 2024, 34 percent involved children four and under. For a class of six-year-olds the practical application is simpler than the statistic suggests: look for a small-parts warning on the box, and believe it.

## No batteries: the rule that saves the present

A gift that needs batteries is a gift that may not work on the morning it is opened. It also quietly hands the receiving family a job, because somebody has to find the right cells, and the wrong size means the object sits on a shelf until a shopping trip happens.

The same logic covers anything that needs assembly, an app, or an adult to read the instructions first. A class gift should work straight out of the bag, in a room full of excited children, in under a minute.

## No sizing: why clothes and anything fitted are out

Clothing is a lovely gift for a child you know, and a poor one for a child you do not, because it requires guessing a size, a preference, and whether the family wants it at all. The same is true of anything fitted or personal: hair accessories for a child with a short cut, a sports item for a child who does not play, a book aimed at a reading level you are only guessing at.

The safe rule is to buy objects that do not depend on knowing the child. Consumables, books in a wide age band, stationery, and small games all survive knowing nothing about the recipient.

## What actually works for a child you have never met

- A book in a broad age band. A short, funny chapter book travels well and is genuinely useful, and [books for kids in the seven to nine range](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=books+for+kids+7+to+9&tag=cnxixi-20) covers a wide slice of a primary class.
- A good stationery set: pens, a notebook, a pencil case. Useful, unisex, no size, no batteries.
- Stickers or a small craft kit. Consumable, which means it leaves no clutter behind, and a [sticker and stationery set for a class bag](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=sticker+sets+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) is about as safe as a class gift gets.
- A small card game in a tin. It seats two to four children, works at a table at break, and needs nothing plugged in.
- A puzzle or a small model with a short build time, provided the box carries no small-parts warning for the age group.

If you want one option to buy in multiples without thinking too hard, [a small craft kit with no size to guess](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=small+craft+kit+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) is the most reliable class gift, because every child can do something with it and no family has to store it forever.

## Secret Santa, where you only know one name

Secret Santa is the same problem with a single target, so you can spend the whole budget on one child rather than dividing it. That is an advantage, because it means one slightly better version of the class-gift shortlist instead of five small identical ones.

The one addition is the wrapping. A Secret Santa gift is opened in front of a group, so a parcel that needs a long explanation, or that requires the giver to say anything aloud, is a small social risk. Buy something a child can understand from the outside of the box.

## If you have several children in the same class

The fastest way to blow a budget is to plan each child's gift separately. Do it once instead.

- Set a total for the class, then divide by the number of parcels.
- Buy multiples of one good item rather than a different item for every name, unless the school asks for personalisation.
- Keep two or three generic spares in a cupboard, because names get added and children forget their own.
- If the exchange is a book swap, buy the same title in several copies and skip the decision entirely.
- If two of your children are in different classes, run one shopping trip for both lists rather than two.

## A note on food and school rules

Many schools now ask families to avoid food gifts, because of allergies, dietary rules, and the practical fact that a class of children opening sweets at the end of term is a management problem for a teacher. Unless you know the policy allows it, keep food out of the parcel. Consumable does not have to mean edible.

## What to skip

- Anything with a small-parts warning for a class whose youngest children are under three.
- Pieces that come apart into very small parts, and anything with a loose button battery, particularly for the youngest end of a school.
- Gift sets built around a character that only some children will recognise.
- Toys that make noise, because twenty-eight of them at once is a genuine problem for the teacher.
- Anything that needs an app, a subscription, or a parent to finish setting it up.

## The bottom line

Buy the same sensible thing in multiples and let three conditions do the choosing: it must clear the age floor, it must not need batteries or assembly, and it must not come in a size you have to guess. A book, a stationery set, a sticker pack, or a card game in a tin will do for almost every name on the list, and spending the budget once across the whole class is far easier than solving each child separately. Buy multiples, wrap them the same way, and do not overthink a gift that is, in the end, a small object exchanged between seven-year-olds on the last afternoon of term.
