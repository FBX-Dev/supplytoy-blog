The crib is in the attic because you could not throw it away, and it was a good crib. That is exactly the problem. A cot built before June 2011 does not meet the safety standards in force today, and the same is true of an old play yard that has been waiting in the garage for a decade.

The grandparents' house is a second household, with its own hazards, its own storage limits and its own reason for existing, and none of those match the ones at home.

## The numbers are small enough to be personal

The honest starting point is that the survey data here is thinner than the confident advice built on it.

The Pew Research Center, analysing the University of Michigan's Health and Retirement Study, found that 21 percent of grandparents said they or their spouse had spent 100 hours or more looking after a grandchild or great-grandchild across 2022 and 2023. The same analysis puts that in perspective: 100 hours over two years is about an hour a week. Pew also flags that part of the period fell during the pandemic, when many families limited contact, so the figure may understate how much caring happens now.

Separately, the Census Bureau's American Community Survey found that in 2024, eight percent of children lived with a grandparent who was the head of the household. And in a Pew survey run in September 2025, seven in ten grandparents aged 65 and over said they were in touch with their grandchildren at least a few times a month, including four in ten at least a few times a week.

The pattern is not a full-time nursery. It is regular, repeated, unsupervised-for-a-while contact, which is exactly the situation where a room has to be safe before a toy arrives.

## Clear the room before you buy anything

The American Academy of Pediatrics keeps a page aimed squarely at grandparents.

The sleeping area is the first pass, and it is the one people resist. A crib made before June 2011 should be replaced rather than reused, and an old play yard goes the same way, because the standards changed and the furniture did not. The academy is equally direct about the obvious temptation: do not let a grandchild sleep in your bed.

Then the rest of the house, in the order the academy lists it.

- Safety gates at the top and the bottom of the stairs.
- Medication out of sight and out of reach, in child-resistant containers, and the same for any bag or handbag that has pills in it.
- Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in the right places.
- Pets and pet food kept out of reach, for the pet's sake as much as the child's.
- Soft covers on sharp or hard furniture edges.
- Kiddie locks on low cupboards, and cleaners moved somewhere a child cannot reach even with the door open.
- Nothing left in a bathtub or a sink full of water, ever.

Two items on that page deserve naming. Toy boxes with lids should not be in the house at all: the academy says to keep them out, or choose a box with no top. And mobile baby walkers are on the do-not-use list, a position Canada settled by banning them outright.

There is also a small, easy-to-miss entry that is worth reading twice. Button batteries turn up in hearing aids and in some remote controls, and those are two objects that live in a grandparent's house far more often than in a young family's. Swallowed or inhaled, or pushed into a nose, they can cause chemical burns severe enough to be life-threatening.

## The one risk that is genuinely different here

At a grandparent's house, one risk deserves its own section, because it is the one that scales with age rather than decreasing.

Medication. The academy's medication safety guidance reports that each year about 50,000 children under five end up in an emergency department for poisoning after swallowing medicine they should not have had. It also makes the point that people get wrong: a child-resistant cap is resistant, not proof. A determined four-year-old can sometimes open one, which is why the container goes out of reach and out of sight as well as closed.

The specific habits that matter at a grandparent's house are not complicated. Keep everything in its original packaging, because a pill in an unlabelled tub is a guess. Put medicines back the moment you have taken one rather than leaving the bottle on the counter. Consider a small lockbox for the regular prescriptions. If you have to answer the door mid-dose, take the medicine with you rather than leaving it beside a curious child. And never call a tablet candy, not once, because that is the word that turns a rule into an invitation.

Two more are worth passing to visitors. The academy tells parents to ask sitters, grandparents and other visitors to keep purses and jackets with medicine in them out of reach, and it warns that even a small amount of some opioid medicines, including liquids and patches, can be life-threatening if swallowed or absorbed through the skin.

If something does happen, the response is fast rather than clever. If a child is unconscious, not breathing or having a seizure, that is an emergency call. If they have mild symptoms or none, the Poison Help line on 1-800-222-1222 is staffed for exactly this question.

None of which argues against having the grandchildren over. It argues for a locked drawer and a habit.

## Then buy three things, not thirty

Here is where the second household gets its own logic. A toy shelf at home can be large, because it is used every day and rotated. A toy shelf at a grandparent's house is used in bursts, and it has to fit in a spare room that is also a bedroom.

Three purchases cover almost all of it.

The first is a small set of things that can be played with repeatedly and in different ways: blocks, a ball, a doll, a few vehicles, board books. Nothing there has a battery, nothing makes a sound you cannot turn down, and a three-year-old and a seven-year-old can both find something to do.

The second is one container that can be closed and put away. A shelf that clears in a minute is what lets a spare room go back to being a spare room, and it means the toys that come out are chosen rather than tipped.

The third is the deliberate gap. The grandparents' house should not duplicate the parents' house, because the value of a second toy box is that it is different. If the child already has a mountain of plastic at home, the useful thing here is the plain version: [a set of plain wooden blocks](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=wooden+blocks+for+toddlers&tag=cnxixi-20) that can be towers today and a shop tomorrow, [a basket that closes](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=collapsible+toy+basket&tag=cnxixi-20) so the room can reset, and a short shelf of [sturdy board books](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=board+books+for+toddlers&tag=cnxixi-20) that will still be readable when the child is old enough to read them aloud.

## What survives ten years

Grandparents buy for the long term more than parents do, and the toys that reward that instinct have no moving parts to break and no software to stop working.

Solid wood blocks, a good ball, a doll, a set of board books, a wooden train set. All of them can sit in a cupboard for two years and come out unchanged. None of them needs a recall check before the next visit, and none of them will beep at three in the morning.

The things that do not age well are the ones that arrive switched on. A talking toy with a fixed phrase becomes embarrassing at six. A battery toy with an app becomes useless when the app is retired. And anything that was a safety compromise when it was bought does not improve with storage.

## A shelf that belongs to the grandparent

One line from the academy's grandparent page is worth more than the rest of the buying advice put together: no matter how fancy the toy, your own interaction with your grandchild matters more.

That is not a sentiment but an instruction about what to keep in the house: the toys that earn their space are the ones that give an adult something to do with a child. A deck of cards, a puzzle with too many pieces for one person, a set of blocks that needs someone to hold the door. The toy is the excuse for the hour.

## What we would not buy

- A lidded toy chest, in any size.
- A wheeled baby walker.
- Any old crib, play yard or high chair of unknown age.
- Battery toys that play one sound with no volume control.
- A shelf of toys that duplicates everything the child already owns at home.
- Anything with loose button batteries that a child can reach.

## The bottom line

Clear the room first, and the buying gets easy. Remove the pre-2011 crib, the lidded toy box and the walker before anything else arrives, put the medication in a locked drawer with the handbags, and check where the button batteries live. Then buy small: a handful of open-ended toys, one basket that closes, and a deliberate difference from what is already at home. The grandparent's house is not a second toy shop. The best evidence for that is the line the pediatricians keep repeating, which is that the adult in the room is the thing the child will remember, and the toys only have to be good enough to keep both of you there for an hour.
