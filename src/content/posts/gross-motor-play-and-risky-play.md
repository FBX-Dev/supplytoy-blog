The phrase you hear most in a house with a toddler is "be careful." It is also the least effective sentence in parenting. It names a feeling rather than an action, arrives after the decision has been made, and gives the child no information they can use. Say it to a two-year-old on a chair with both hands full of cushions and you will get a polite moment of eye contact, followed by the exact behaviour you were worried about.

Meanwhile the thing that actually reduces injuries is almost never the child. It is the room: the table the child is under, the depth of the surface below them, the gap between the radiator and the wall, the coffee table that meets their eyebrow at exactly the wrong height.

## The milestones here are physical, and they are not small

The CDC milestone lists are a useful corrective to how this stage gets talked about at home. Most two-year-olds can run, stand on tiptoes, walk up and down stairs with a hand held, and kick a ball. Those are four different systems working together: balance, ankle and foot strength, coordination, and the ability to plan a movement before making it.

Back up a year and most fifteen-month-olds walk a few steps on their own and feed themselves with their fingers. Back up further and you have a baby who could not sit unsupported. The distance covered in fourteen months is enormous, and it is all built from the same raw material: a child repeatedly attempting slightly more than they can currently do, and surviving the failure.

That is why the physical milestones and the "risky" behaviours arrive together. The climbing, the tipping, the launching off the sofa, the fascination with stairs — this is not a personality defect. It is the mechanism. A child who has never been slightly off balance has never had to correct their balance.

Two flags are worth knowing so you do not confuse ordinary variation with a problem. If a child cannot walk alone by around eighteen months, or does not seem to be using newly learned movement skills the way you would expect, that belongs with a pediatrician rather than with more practice at home. And losing a skill a child already had, at any age, is a reason to seek care promptly. The milestone lists are monitoring tools, not diagnoses; they are not a screening test, and routine screening is recommended at 9, 18 and 30 months.

## Why "be careful" fails, and what to say instead

Instructions work better when they describe a body position rather than a feeling. Some swaps that hold up under pressure:

- Instead of "be careful," try "hold the rail with one hand." One concrete action, checkable by the child.
- Instead of "get down," try "feet first, turn around." This teaches the exit rather than blocking the activity.
- Instead of "that's too high," try "you can climb to here." A boundary the child can see is a boundary a child can respect.
- Instead of "stop," try "show me where you're going and I'll spot you." This keeps the child in charge of the plan.
- Narrate the surface rather than the danger: "that cushion is squashy, that board is hard." Children learn to read surfaces before they learn to fear heights.

The general rule is that a warning which cannot be converted into a movement is just noise, and after enough noise both of you stop hearing it.

## Change the environment, not the child

The single most useful reframe for this stage is that your job is not to stop the climbing. It is to decide what is underneath it.

- Put the climbing thing over a soft, stable surface, not near a corner or a hard edge.
- Fix what wobbles. A bookcase that tips is a real hazard; a shelf bolted to the wall is furniture a child can use.
- Move the glass, the lamp, the hot drink, and the bowl of grapes out of the radius of anything the child climbs on. The radius is bigger than you think, and it is diagonal.
- Keep a clear fall zone of about a metre in every direction from the top of the highest thing they climb.
- Leave the low route open. If everything low is blocked, the child will simply choose a higher route.

## Hazards and risks are not the same thing

The word "risky" does a lot of hiding in this discussion, so it is worth separating two things that get spoken about as one.

A hazard is something a child cannot see and cannot assess: an unanchored bookcase, a glass at the edge of a table, a pond, a road, a hot pan. There is no lesson in a hazard and no benefit to exposure. You remove it, quietly, without a discussion.

A risk is something a child can see and is choosing to take: the height of the step, the wobbliness of the log, the speed of the run downhill. This is the useful category. A child who takes a visible risk is doing three things at once — estimating, committing, and then living with the result, which is exactly the loop that makes a better mover.

The mistake most of us make is treating risks as hazards, which means removing everything interesting, and treating some hazards as risks, which means leaving the bookcase. The tool for the first error is your own knees: get down to floor level and look for what is invisible from an adult height. The tool for the second is a bolt.

## One thing the safety standards will not tell you

Here is a fact worth having when you read product pages. The mandatory US toy safety standard, ASTM F963, covers toys intended for children under fourteen. It does not cover bicycles, non-powder guns, kites, or playground equipment.

That matters for this category specifically. A large climbing frame, a slide, or anything designed to be installed and used as play equipment sits outside the toy standard that most parents assume is protecting them. Independently, ASTM F963 does cover the small parts, sharp edges, lead in paint and battery-accessibility rules that apply to the smaller things in your living room.

The practical upshot is not that climbing equipment is dangerous. It is that the safety of a climber in your home is a question about your floor, your wall, your anchoring, and your supervision, and no label is able to answer it for you. Assemble it properly, put it on a soft surface, and look at it from a toddler's height before you let them on it.

## What we would not buy

- Portable slides with feet that flex. A slide that slides across the floor while a child is on it is worse than no slide.
- Climbing frames that need a wall anchor and do not include one. If it can tip sideways, it will, at the least convenient moment in the day.
- Trampolines and bouncing equipment marketed for children under three, whose bounce is usually more powerful than a small child's joints are ready for.
- Baby walkers with wheels and a seat. They move a child faster than they can control, and doorways and stairs arrive quickly.
- Anything that requires constant adult hands to keep it stable. Equipment that needs you holding it is furniture you will resent, and you will stop holding it within a fortnight.

## The bottom line

Let the child climb, tip, tiptoe and fall from low heights, and spend your energy on the room instead of on warnings. Fix the wobbling furniture, clear the fall zones, put the climber on something soft, and say where to put their hands rather than "be careful."

Injuries in this age group are usually about the environment, not the child's judgement, and the child's judgement is the thing you are trying to build. That only happens if something occasionally goes slightly wrong at a height that does not matter.
