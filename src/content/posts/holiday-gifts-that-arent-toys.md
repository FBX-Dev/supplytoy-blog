The most honest sentence in December is the one a parent says quietly at the school gate: we cannot take another toy. It is not a comment on your taste. It is a statement about floor space, about the four unopened boxes still in the cupboard from last year, and about the fact that a child with two hundred objects plays with about six of them.

Once you accept that, the interesting question is what to give instead. The answer is not nothing, and it is not always a gift card. There are four categories that work, and they work for different reasons.

## When a family says please, no more toys

Take the request at face value rather than as a hint that you should buy something smaller. Families who say this have usually done the arithmetic. Every new object has to be stored, cleaned, repaired, and eventually removed, and the person doing that work is the parent.

The other useful thing to know is where the request comes from. Some households are short on space. Some have children who are already overwhelmed by choice. Some are trying to reduce how much plastic comes into the house. The right substitute is different in each case, which is why it is worth asking one question before you buy anything.

## Consumables do the most work per pound

Consumables are the most underrated gift category in existence. They get used, they run out, they leave no permanent footprint, and the child gets the pleasure of having something new without the household getting another storage problem.

The list is longer than people think.

- Art supplies that will actually be used up. Paper, pencils, markers, clay, tape, glue, cheap canvas.
- Craft materials the child can ruin without guilt, which is the whole point of a five-year-old with paint.
- A big box of something that disappears by nature: stickers, bath items, modelling clay, origami paper.
- Food that belongs to the child rather than the household. A specific treat they do not normally get.
- Seeds and a small pot, if there is a windowsill and someone willing to water things.

The one rule is to buy consumables the child will use rather than the ones you would enjoy. A set of fine drawing pens is a bad gift for a child who likes to build, and a box of modelling clay is a bad gift for a child who only wants to draw.

If you want a direction that nearly always works, [art supplies for kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=art+supplies+for+kids+8+to+12&tag=cnxixi-20) are consumable, useful, and impossible to get the size wrong.

## Books, and the trap of the impressive book

Books are the obvious non-toy gift and the easiest to get subtly wrong. The failure mode is buying a beautiful edition of something you loved at that age, which is a gift to yourself that sits on a shelf.

There are two rules that fix it. First, find out what the child already reads and buy the next one in that series, or something genuinely adjacent. Second, accept that a graphic novel or a book of facts about their specific obsession beats a classic every time. Reading for pleasure at eight is not about literary quality. It is about the child discovering that a book can be better than the alternative.

For a child you know well, [books for kids nine to twelve](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=books+for+kids+9+to+12&tag=cnxixi-20) cover the years where a series can carry a whole winter. For younger readers, a stack of cheap paperbacks is a better gift than one hardback.

One practical note: if you are buying second-hand books, which is a genuinely good idea, that is fine. Second-hand rules are different for the categories further down this page.

## Experiences, and the follow-through problem

An experience gift is only a gift if the adults involved can actually deliver it. A voucher for a trip that never happens is worse than a toy, because the child has been given something and then had it taken away.

So judge an experience by three questions. Who is driving? Who is paying for the extras? What happens if the weather is wrong on the chosen day?

The experiences that survive those questions tend to be small and near, rather than large and theoretical. A single afternoon at a climbing wall with a parent. Tickets to something the child has mentioned twice. A class that meets weekly for a term. A promised camping night in the garden.

One category deserves a specific mention, because it is both a gift and a genuine safety investment: swimming. Drowning is a leading cause of death for young children, and children aged one to four die from drowning more than from any other single cause, while for ages five to fourteen it is the second leading cause of unintentional injury death after motor vehicle crashes. Lessons are an experience gift with a very long half-life. If you want a statistic, roughly four thousand people a year die from unintentional drowning in the US, and there are around twice as many non-fatal drownings that end in emergency care.

## Gear upgrades, and how to buy one safely

The best-value non-toy gift for a family that already has everything is a better version of something they already own and use. Not a second one. A better one.

Things that wear out and get replaced: helmets, scooters, bikes, sports kit, school bags, water bottles, lunch boxes, bedding, headphones.

Two cautions apply. The first is that an upgrade should be a genuine upgrade, which means the old one leaves the house. A better scooter alongside the old scooter is two scooters. The second is that for anything with wheels, the certification matters far more than the styling, and it is worth knowing the difference before you buy.

- A bicycle helmet sold in the US must meet the federal bicycle helmet standard, which tests a single high-energy impact plus the straps and how well the helmet stays on.
- A helmet for skateboarding or trick rolling should carry the ASTM F1492 mark, which tests repeated impacts. A bicycle-only helmet has not been tested to that standard.
- There is no federal standard for skateboard helmets in the US, so an uncertified helmet can legally be sold as one. The sticker inside the shell is the only thing that tells you.
- A helmet that has taken an impact is replaced even when it looks undamaged, because the foam does not recover. Many manufacturers suggest replacing around five years, so a second-hand helmet with unknown history is a poor upgrade.

If you are upgrading a rider, [kids' helmets that carry a certification sticker](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+bike+helmet+certified&tag=cnxixi-20) are the safe default, and the safety commission's view is that a bicycle helmet is the right one for scooters and tricycles too.

If you are buying anything second-hand, including gear, check it against the official recall list before you hand it over. The commission publishes recalls at cpsc.gov/Recalls, you can look up reports and submit one at SaferProducts.gov, and the phone line is 800-638-2772. Federal law bans selling recalled products, and that applies to resale and to gifts, not just to shops. It takes about a minute, and it is the single most useful habit for anyone buying used children's equipment.

## Named, handmade, and one-of-one gifts

There is a category that beats both toys and gear, and it is the one that says someone was thinking about this particular child. A book with a note written inside the cover. A photo album. Something made and clearly made for them. A day that is theirs alone, with a name on it.

These cost less than most toys and are kept longer than almost all of them, which is a strange thing for an industry to have missed.

## How to give this without it landing badly

Giving a non-toy gift can feel like a criticism if it is done carelessly, so take the awkwardness out of it in advance.

- **Say something.** "I heard you are drowning in toys, so I went a different way this year" removes the whole problem. Silence leaves the parent wondering whether you disapproved.
- **Give the child something to unwrap.** A book, a box of supplies, and a wrapped promise all open well. An envelope with an experience inside it lands better with an object.
- **Do not make the parent work.** A craft kit that needs supervision, a pet, or a plan that requires a car journey at 9am on a Sunday is a gift with a job attached.
- **Keep a small toy in reserve if the child is very young.** Under three, almost the entire experience of a gift is the opening. A book and one small object usually covers it.
- **If you are close to the family, ask what would actually help.** The answer may be a term of an activity, or it may be replacement winter boots, and both are better than another box.

## The bottom line

Not buying a toy is not the same as not giving a gift. Consumables get used, books get read, experiences get remembered when the adults can deliver them, and a better version of something already in use improves a child's week. Choose the category by asking what the household actually lacks rather than what you would enjoy buying, keep a small wrapped object in the parcel so there is something to open, and say your reasoning out loud so nobody has to guess. And if a wheeled item is involved, sort the certification and check the recall list before you wrap it.
