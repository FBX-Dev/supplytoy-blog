A parent searches the name of a toy on the recall site, finds nothing, and concludes the toy is fine. That check has a coin-flip quality to it, because the search that actually matters is not the product name. It is the model number and the date code stamped on the toy or the packaging.

Recall notices are written to describe a specific product produced in a specific window. They are narrow on purpose. A toy with the same name, the same photo and the same colour scheme can be outside the scope of the recall because it came off a different line, and a toy that looks slightly different can be squarely inside it.

## A recall is a lookup, not a feeling

There is a temptation to treat recall information as background noise, the way most people treat a car recall notice that arrives three years late. With children's products it is worth more attention than that, because the failure modes are the ones you cannot undo. The high-frequency reasons a toy gets recalled are a short list: choking, button batteries, magnets, strangulation, lead, and mechanical failure.

It is also worth knowing the scale. The CPSC announces roughly 300 recalls a year across all product categories, not only toys. That is a number small enough to be worth checking against and large enough that you will not remember them.

## Where the information actually lives

Four addresses cover nearly everything a parent needs.

- Recalls are published at **cpsc.gov/Recalls**. This is the searchable database and the first place to look.
- Unsafe products and injuries can be reported at **SaferProducts.gov**. It was created by the 2008 CPSIA, and it works in both directions: you can search incident reports other people have filed, and you can file your own. Companies are given the chance to respond, and their response is published alongside the report.
- The CPSC hotline is **800-638-2772**, with a TTY line at **800-638-8270**.
- Recall alerts by email can be set up at **cpsc.gov/Newsroom/Subscribe**, where you can select the children and infant products category rather than receiving everything.

That last one is the highest-leverage minute in this article. A recall most often fails to reach a family because nobody knew it existed, not because the family decided to ignore it.

## The model number and the date code decide it

Every recall notice names the specific identifier for the affected product, and it is usually one of these.

- A model number or item number, often printed on the base of the toy, inside a battery compartment, or on a sewn-in tag.
- A batch or lot code.
- A manufacturing date range, which is what tells you whether your unit was built during the affected window.
- A UPC or product code on the packaging, which helps if you still have the box and not the toy.

Write the information down before you search. Reading a model number off the underside of a toy while holding a phone in the other hand is the reason most searches get abandoned, and a photo of the label takes three seconds and can be taken later. If you keep a simple [document organizer for manuals and receipts](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=receipt+and+warranty+document+organizer&tag=cnxixi-20), the receipt that proves the purchase date can live with the manual, and a remedy claim becomes much easier.

## Why the same-looking toy may not be covered

This is the part that catches people out, and it cuts both ways.

The recall usually applies to a production window, so a toy made before or after that window is not covered even though it is visually identical. Two children can own what looks like the same product with different answers, and the difference is a set of digits nobody reads.

The reverse also happens. A recall can cover several model numbers under one name, or a design that was sold under more than one brand. If the notice lists a family of items, read the whole list rather than the first line.

The practical consequence is that comparing with a friend's toy tells you nothing. The sticker on your own toy tells you everything.

## Second-hand, resale, and what the law says

Federal law prohibits selling a recalled product, and that prohibition applies to second-hand sales as well. A recalled toy cannot be sold at a yard sale, listed online, or passed along in a bin, unless the recall notice's remedy has been completed and the product is no longer in the recalled state.

That makes the recall check a step in accepting a hand-me-down rather than an optional courtesy. It also means the seller's obligation does not disappear because the toy was cheap. If you find out a toy in your house is under recall, the answer is not to move it along quietly. It is to follow the remedy or to dispose of it so it cannot be re-entered circulation.

Second-hand items with batteries or magnets deserve a check first, because those are the two failure modes where a recall tends to happen and where the consequence is worst. A toy with a magnetic component that can come loose is not a repair job. Regulated magnet products must satisfy a flux index below 50 kG²mm² under 16 CFR part 1262, and swallowed magnets can attract across body tissue and cause perforation, obstruction, sepsis or death. If it rattles, it is finished. And if a magnet has already been swallowed, seek medical help immediately rather than waiting for a symptom.

## The registration card is not junk mail

The card tucked into the box of a crib, a stroller or a high chair looks like marketing. It is the opposite.

Durable infant and toddler products can be registered with the manufacturer, which is how the manufacturer contacts you directly if a recall happens. Without that registration the company has no way to reach you, because it does not know who bought the product. The card is the mechanism, and filling it in takes a minute.

Toys generally do not come with this, which is precisely why the recall database and the email alert matter more for toys than for nursery furniture.

## Car seats belong to a different agency

One exception worth knowing so you do not waste the search. Car seat recalls are handled by NHTSA, not the CPSC. Searching the CPSC database for a car seat means searching the wrong list entirely, and the same is true for anything designed for use in a vehicle.

## What to do when a toy you own is recalled

Read the remedy. Recall notices typically offer one of a repair, a replacement, or a refund, and they tell you exactly which applies and how to claim it.

Then take the toy out of circulation in a way that is physical rather than theoretical. Put it in a [lidded storage bin](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=toy+storage+bins+with+lids&tag=cnxixi-20), label the bin with what it is and which recall it belongs to, and put it somewhere a child will not find it, which is not the playroom floor. If you have several children and a few products in question, [adhesive bin labels](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=adhesive+labels+for+toy+storage+bins&tag=cnxixi-20) keep the quarantine bin from being emptied back into general circulation by someone trying to be helpful.

If the recall is for a serious hazard, such as a battery or magnet that can come loose, do not attempt a home fix while you wait. A repaired toy is a new, untested product.

## The five-minute routine

- Photograph the model number, lot code and date code on any new toy, especially anything with batteries or magnets.
- Check those identifiers at cpsc.gov/Recalls rather than searching the product name.
- Report a problem you have seen at SaferProducts.gov, and read other reports while you are there.
- Subscribe to recall alerts and tick the children and infant products category.
- Register durable items such as cribs, strollers and high chairs.
- Quarantine and label anything recalled; never resell it.

## The bottom line

A recall check takes five minutes and fails for one reason: the wrong search. Look up the model number and the date code, not the name on the box. Say yes to the email alerts, fill in the registration card, and check hand-me-downs before they enter the house rather than after a child has been playing with them for a month.

Recalls are rare and they are public. The only real risk is not knowing that one applies to you.
