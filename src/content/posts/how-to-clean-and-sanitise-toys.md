The plastic dinosaur has been in a mouth, on a kitchen floor, down the side of a car seat and back into the mouth. This is normal, and it is why the question of how to clean toys comes up at least once a week in most houses with small children.

Most of what you will find online reaches for bleach immediately. The public health guidance does not, and the difference matters, because the wrong approach is both harsher on the toys and more effort than the job needs.

## Why most toy cleaning stops at soap and water

The CDC's position on household cleaning is simple and widely ignored. Soap and water remove most germs from most surfaces, and for most situations that is the entire job. Cleaning and disinfecting are separate actions: cleaning removes dirt and germs mechanically, and disinfecting kills germs on a surface that has already been cleaned. Disinfectant applied to a grubby surface delivers far less than its label implies.

The line worth remembering for a house full of toys is that when nobody in the household is sick, and nobody who is sick has visited recently, disinfection is usually not necessary. Routine cleaning is enough. A toy that lives in a mouth is being rinsed by saliva and by the sink anyway, and a bin of bleach is not the answer to a normal week.

## When disinfection is actually worth doing

There are real situations, and they are narrower than the fear suggests.

- Someone in the household is ill, or has been ill in the last few days.
- A shared toy has been handled by a child who was sick at the time.
- A toy has been in contact with bodily fluids.
- Toys are shared between children in a childcare setting during an outbreak.

Even then, soap and water plus thorough drying is a reasonable default for most objects. Reserve disinfection for the things that actually get passed around: hard plastic toys, handles, table edges, and the high-traffic surfaces around them.

## The dilution, and the habits that waste it

If you are going to disinfect with bleach, use plain unscented household bleach with 5 per cent to 9 per cent available chlorine. Do not use splashless bleach or laundry bleach, and do not use anything whose label does not state a concentration in that range.

If the bottle does not give a dilution for surface disinfection, the CDC ratio is 5 tablespoons, which is one third of a cup, of bleach in one gallon of room-temperature water, or 4 teaspoons in one quart. A [small spray bottle kept for cleaning only](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=small+spray+bottle+for+cleaning&tag=cnxixi-20) makes the mixing easier to do accurately and stops the solution being stored by accident.

Two habits quietly ruin the result. The first is using hot water, which is not what the instruction says; use room-temperature water. The second is eyeballing the bleach. The ratios are small, and more is not safer, it is simply a stronger irritant left on a surface a child will touch.

## Contact time, and the mixing mistake

The surface has to stay visibly wet for at least one minute. Spraying and wiping straight away does not disinfect anything, it just moves the germs around the room.

Never mix bleach with another cleaner or disinfectant. Not with an ammonia-based glass cleaner, not with vinegar, not with anything. The combinations can release gases that are dangerous to breathe, and this rule has no exceptions in a house with children in it.

Mix the solution fresh on the day you use it. It loses strength over time, and a bottle mixed last week is no longer a disinfectant. Wipe off visible dirt with soap first, because bleach on a surface still carrying food or grime is mostly wasted.

## Working through the materials

The material of the toy decides the method, and getting this wrong is where most damage happens.

### Hard plastic toys

Plain hard plastic is the easy category. If the manufacturer says the toy is dishwasher safe, the dishwasher is a legitimate option and reaches temperatures a sink cannot. Otherwise use warm soapy water, a [soft brush for cleaning toys](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=soft+brush+for+cleaning+toys&tag=cnxixi-20), a clean-water rinse, and air drying. Check for holes and seams before soaking anything, because a toy with an opening, or a battery compartment, will take on water and stay wet inside.

### Wood, board books and anything absorbent

Wooden toys, board books and anything made of paper or unsealed wood should not be soaked in bleach. Wood absorbs the solution, holds it, and is damaged by it, and a wet board book is simply a ruined board book. A wipe with a damp soapy cloth, then a clean damp cloth, then proper drying, is the appropriate level of effort here.

### Fabric and plush

Soft toys can usually go into a washing machine on a gentle cycle, following the care label, then air dry or tumble on low. A [mesh laundry bag for plush toys](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=mesh+laundry+bag+for+plush+toys&tag=cnxixi-20) keeps small ones out of the machine's workings and keeps any attached parts together. If a soft toy cannot be washed, it can be surface cleaned, but fabric should never go near a bleach bucket.

### Electronics and battery toys

Electronic toys should never be soaked. Take the batteries out if the compartment opens, wipe the surfaces with a cloth or wipe intended for electronics, and make sure everything is completely dry before the batteries go back in. Water and battery compartments are a bad combination, and a toy that still feels damp inside is not ready to be used.

## The hand sanitiser blind spot

One specific gap is worth knowing, because it is a very common assumption. Hand sanitiser does not work well against norovirus. For norovirus, the recommendation is to wash hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, which matters a great deal in a house where a stomach bug is going round and everyone is reaching for the bottle instead of the tap.

If you are disinfecting after a norovirus illness, the concentration is much stronger than the everyday solution. The CDC gives 1,000 to 5,000 parts per million of available chlorine, which is 5 to 25 tablespoons of 5.25 per cent household bleach per gallon of water, with a contact time of at least five minutes. This is a different and more serious job than routine toy cleaning, and it is not a mixture to make up casually around children.

## What we would not do

- Do not soak wooden toys, board books or fabric toys in bleach.
- Do not put electronic or battery toys in water, ever.
- Do not mix bleach with any other cleaning product.
- Do not keep a mixed solution for a second day.
- Do not disinfect the whole playroom because one child had a cold.
- Do not rely on hand sanitiser to stop a stomach bug.
- Do not assume a stronger solution is a better one.

## The bottom line

For most households, most of the time, the answer is soap, water, a rinse and letting things dry properly. Disinfection has a place, it is smaller than the internet suggests, and the details are what make it work or fail: plain unscented bleach at 5 per cent to 9 per cent available chlorine, 5 tablespoons in a gallon of room-temperature water, at least one minute of contact time, mixed fresh, and never combined with another cleaner. Keep bleach away from wood, fabric and electronics. Keep soap and water for your hands, for at least 20 seconds when it counts.
