Two toys, nearly identical, both carry a "3+" on the corner of the box. One was tested to the current standard and carries a traceable label and a certificate that nobody at the store will show you. The other was never assessed at all. The number on the front looks the same in both cases, which is why the label by itself is not the whole story.

Reading a toy label well means understanding three separate things: what the age grade is derived from, what the standard behind it covers, and what documentation exists that is not printed on the front of the box.

## The age grade is a safety statement

The most common mistake is reading the age number as a development prediction. It is not. The age grade is primarily a **safety statement**, derived from things like the size of the smallest part the toy contains and how the toy behaves under stress and abuse.

The single most important threshold is **36 months**, because that is where the small-parts requirement lives. A toy may contain parts too small for a child under three. That is what the standard "not suitable for children under 36 months" phrasing is communicating.

Two consequences follow, and both are routinely misunderstood.

First, a label of "3+" does not mean a two-year-old cannot play with the toy. It means the toy was not assessed for a two-year-old. If you decide to allow it, that is your risk assessment, and it needs active supervision rather than a single judgement made once.

Second, a toy with no age marking at all is not automatically unsafe, but it has not been assessed. Toys from informal markets, unregulated online sellers, and handmade sources may never have been tested to any standard.

## What the current standard is

In the United States, the mandatory toy safety standard is **ASTM F963**. The current version is **ASTM F963-23**. The CPSC published the final rule incorporating it on **January 18, 2024**, and it **took effect on April 20, 2024**, replacing **F963-17**. It is codified as a mandatory standard in **16 CFR part 1250** under the CPSIA.

The scope is worth knowing: **ASTM F963 covers toys intended for children under 14**. It does **not** cover bicycles, non-powder guns, kites, or playground equipment. Those are governed elsewhere, and if you are reading a label on one of those products, you are reading a different set of rules.

## What the standard actually covers

When a toy has genuinely been tested to F963, a specific list of things has been checked.

- **Paint lead limit of 90 ppm.** This is the number most people have heard, and it applies to surface coatings.
- **Eight soluble heavy metals** are controlled, alongside restrictions on **phthalates** in certain plastics.
- **Small parts requirements for toys intended for children under 36 months.** Anything that fits entirely inside the small-parts cylinder is a hazard at that age.
- **Sharp edges and sharp points**, which are assessed both as manufactured and after abuse testing.
- **Battery accessibility**, covered in **section 4.25** of the standard.
- **Sound pressure limits.** For toys held close to the ear, the limit is **65 dB LAeq and 110 dB LCpeak**. For handheld toys it is **85 / 115**. Rattles have an **LCpeak limit of 115**, and push-pull toys are limited to **85 LAeq, 94 LAFmax, and 115 LCpeak**.

The sound limits explain something parents notice by ear: a toy can be entirely compliant and still be unpleasant at close range. The standard is written around specific measurements, not around whether a particular noise annoys you in a car.

## The certificate you never see

Under the CPSIA, a children's product carries obligations that are not visible on the shelf.

- **A tracking label** on the product and its packaging, so a specific unit can be traced back to a batch and a date.
- **A Children's Product Certificate (CPC)**, in which the manufacturer or importer certifies compliance.
- **Testing by a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory** for the relevant requirements.

A toy that has been properly certified is traceable. That is what the tracking information on the back of the box is for. If you cannot find any manufacturer identification, batch information, or contact details on a toy, that absence is meaningful — not proof of harm, but a sign that the certification chain you are relying on may not exist.

## Reading a label in the store

A short practical pass, in order.

- **Find the age grade** and read it as a safety statement, not a preference.
- **Look for the small-parts phrasing** if the intended child is under three. "Not suitable for children under 36 months" is the sentence that matters.
- **Look for the warnings block.** Choking warnings, battery warnings, and cord warnings are all signals about which hazard the manufacturer identified.
- **Check for a tracking label and manufacturer details.** This is your handle for a recall check later.
- **Match the toy to the child, not the other way around.** If it is a gift, the label is the recipient's constraint.

One more habit: treat a toy that parts ways with its hardware as no longer compliant. A missing screw on a battery cover moves a compliant toy out of compliance in your house, whatever the box said.

## What labels will not tell you

- **They will not tell you whether the toy is fun.** That is a separate question and, honestly, the harder one.
- **They will not tell you the toy is right for a specific child.** Age grades are population-level judgements.
- **They will not survive wear.** A label reflects the toy as manufactured, not the toy after two years of hard use or a trip through a washing machine.
- **They will not stop a younger sibling.** The most common serious incident in a multi-age household involves a younger child and an older child's toy, and no label on the box prevents that.

## Buying against the label

Most of the value of knowing the label is in what you buy next. For a child under three, [toys for babies and toddlers under 36 months](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=toys+for+babies+under+36+months&tag=cnxixi-20) are the ones you should be filtering to, precisely because the small-parts assessment has been done for that age band. If you are buying for a child in the twelve-to-eighteen-month range, [toddler toys for 12 to 18 months](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=toddler+toys+12+to+18+months&tag=cnxixi-20) are graded for the phase where mouthing is still constant.

For families who would rather sidestep the small-parts question on some items entirely, solid [wooden toys for toddlers](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=wooden+toys+for+toddlers&tag=cnxixi-20) tend to have fewer separable components, though they are not automatically compliant and still benefit from the same hardware check.

## What we would not buy

- **Anything sold as a toy with no age marking at all**, for a child under three.
- **Products marketed to babies that are not toys and were never tested as toys.** Fidgets, clips, and novelties are common offenders.
- **Toys where the age grade is being argued down by the seller.** If the box says 3+, the seller's opinion does not change the assessment that was done.
- **Any toy whose warning block has been covered by a price sticker or a promotional label.** Those warnings are the part of the packaging you were supposed to read.

## The bottom line

Read the age number as a safety rating, not a preview of what a child will like. The current mandatory standard is **ASTM F963-23**, in force since **April 20, 2024** and replacing **F963-17**, codified at **16 CFR part 1250** for children under 14. It controls paint lead at **90 ppm**, eight soluble heavy metals, phthalates, small parts for the under-36-month group, sharp edges and points, battery accessibility, and specific sound pressure limits.

Behind the label, what you actually want is traceability: a tracking label, a certificate, and a third-party laboratory in the chain. Buy to the age grade, then check the hardware yourself, because a compliant toy stops being compliant the moment its battery cover loses its screw.
