By the second month of first grade, the dining table has stopped being a dining table. There is a pencil case at one end, worksheets under a bowl, and an adult-sized chair being climbed into and out of forty times an evening. So you shop for a small table and a small chair, and you find that the two pieces of furniture nobody thinks about come with the most confusing paperwork.

Here is the reframe that makes the aisle readable. A child's table and chair are not toys. They are furniture, and furniture in the United States is regulated by product type rather than by one rule covering the category.

## Your child's table is not a toy, and that is a paperwork fact

The toy safety standard, ASTM F963, defines a toy in a way that explicitly excludes furniture. The CPSC's business guidance says a toy does not include bicycles, scooters, sporting goods, musical instruments, juvenile products, and furniture, with one exception: the toy counterparts of those things. A dollhouse-sized table belongs to the toy standard. The table your child actually sits at does not.

The version of that sentence repeated online is that furniture is not tested. That is not what the exclusion says. It says children's furniture sits outside the toy standard's scope and is governed by the standards written for furniture instead.

## Furniture standards are written one product at a time

Children's furniture has no single umbrella rule. Instead there is a set, each defined by the shape of the object and the age of the child.

- Children's chairs and stools: ASTM F2613, a voluntary consumer safety specification.
- Children's folding chairs and folding stools: a CPSC mandatory standard, 16 CFR part 1232.
- High chairs: a separate CPSC mandatory standard, 16 CFR part 1231, which incorporates ASTM F404.
- Clothing storage units: a CPSC mandatory standard, 16 CFR part 1261, covered further down.

Same child, same room, four rulebooks, depending on whether the thing folds, whether it lifts the child to table height, or whether it is tall enough to tip.

## The folding rule, and the line it does not cross

The CPSC's mandatory standard for children's folding chairs and folding stools is 16 CFR part 1232. It requires every children's folding chair and folding stool to meet all applicable clauses of ASTM F2613-21, which the rule incorporates by reference. The folding mechanism is why the rule exists, and the injuries it targets are pinch and shear points, finger amputations, degloving injuries and compound fractures. A product that does not meet the hinge clearance requirements has to carry an "Amputation Hazard" warning label.

Now the part that is usually reported wrong. The federal mandatory standard covers folding chairs and folding stools. A non-folding child's chair or stool has no matching CPSC mandatory standard; it is covered by the voluntary ASTM standard instead.

That is a statement about where the line is drawn, not a statement that nobody is watching. Buy a folding chair and there is a federal rule behind it. Buy a non-folding one and the safety case rests on the voluntary standard plus your own inspection.

## What the voluntary standard actually covers

ASTM F2613-22, the Standard Consumer Safety Specification for Children's Chairs and Stools, defines its own territory precisely. Its scope is a chair or stool intended to be used by a single child who can get in and get out of the product unassisted, with a seat height of 15 inches or less, with or without a rocking base. One child, climbing in alone, seat at or below 15 inches.

The exclusions are just as informative. The standard does not apply to products used in a commercial setting or to products without a rigid frame such as bean bag chairs or foam chairs. It also does not apply to seats with restraint systems, infant or infant/toddler rockers, children's step stools, or children's potty chairs. A foam chair is not a chair in the regulatory sense, and a seat with a restraint system is a different product class — which is why the high chair is governed separately.

## High chairs are a third rulebook

A high chair is not a bigger child's chair. The mandatory standard is 16 CFR part 1231, which incorporates ASTM F404, and its definition is narrow: a freestanding seat for a child under three years old, with a seating surface more than 15 inches above the floor.

So the line between a child's chair and a high chair is neither age nor price. It is 15 inches of seat height, plus whether the child can get in and out alone. The size range built for a child who climbs in alone is where to shop — a plain [child-sized table and chair set](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=child+sized+table+and+chair+set&tag=cnxixi-20) is the honest version of the idea.

## Measure three heights before you buy anything

- Seat height. The child's feet should be flat on the floor or on a footrest, with knees at roughly a right angle. A seat that leaves the feet dangling is a seat the child slides out of.
- Table height. The gap between the seat surface and the underside of the table has to clear the child's thighs. If it does not, the child sits sideways, which is the posture every parent recognises.
- Cabinet height, or the height of every tall piece in the room. Anything a child could climb gets measured for anchoring, not for style.

Children's furniture is sold by age band, and the bands overlap so much that two products labelled for the same child can differ by several inches.

## The tip-over risk is bigger than the chair

CPSC's February 2024 report found about 17,800 people a year treated in emergency departments for injuries from furniture, televisions and appliances tipping over, averaged across 2020 to 2022. Children under 18 accounted for 44 per cent of those injuries, which works out to one child every 53 minutes. Of the total, 82 per cent involved furniture, and 22 per cent of the injured were children aged one to three.

The advice that follows is the one parents do not expect: do not store toys, remotes or anything else a child wants where they have to climb to reach it. Heavy things low, interesting things at the child's own height, and tall furniture fixed to the wall. A set of [furniture tip-over anchor straps](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=furniture+tip+over+anchor+straps&tag=cnxixi-20) usually costs under 20 dollars and takes under 20 minutes to install.

The anchor itself is a product, and it has been recalled. On January 12, 2024, CPSC announced a recall of millions of plastic furniture tip-over restraint straps made by New Age Furniture, because the plastic becomes brittle and weak over time. ## The rule for clothing storage units, and the advice that goes with it

The STURDY Act was signed into law on December 23, 2022, and codified at 15 U.S.C. § 2056f. The CPSC's final rule at 88 FR 28403 adopted ASTM F2057 and placed it at 16 CFR part 1261. It applies to clothing storage units manufactured after September 1, 2023.

The scope is narrow. A covered unit is freestanding bedroom clothing storage furniture with a height of 27 inches or more, a mass of 30 pounds or more, and an enclosed storage volume of 3.2 cubic feet or more. What falls outside matters just as much: bookcases, entertainment furniture, office furniture, dining room furniture, jewellery cabinets, under-bed drawers, furniture not intended for the bedroom, and permanently fixed built-in furniture.

Then the sentence that matters more than the standard. CPSC says all clothing storage furniture, regardless of when it was manufactured or purchased, should be anchored and secured. The standard governs new units; the advice covers the dresser already in the spare room.

## The battery compartment on a light-up desk

A desk with a music panel, a chair with a light-up canopy, a table with an illuminated drawing surface: these are furniture with electronics, and the electronics usually run on button cells. Under 16 CFR part 1263, the rule implementing Reese's Law, a battery compartment must open either with a tool or with at least two independent and simultaneous hand actions.

The check takes ten seconds. Turn the piece over, find the compartment, and see whether it needs a screwdriver or a deliberate two-handed pinch. If it pops open with one thumb, leave that one on the shelf. A visible screw on a [kids study desk with storage](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+study+desk+with+storage&tag=cnxixi-20) is a feature to look for.

## The bottom line

Children's tables and chairs are furniture, not toys. ASTM F963's definition of a toy excludes furniture and covers only the toy counterparts, so the toy standard is not the one governing the table your child does homework on. That is a statement about which rulebook applies, not about whether one does.

The furniture rulebooks are split by product type. Folding children's chairs and stools have a federal mandatory standard at 16 CFR part 1232. Non-folding children's chairs and stools sit under the voluntary ASTM F2613 standard instead, whose scope stops at a seat height of 15 inches and a single child who can climb in unassisted. High chairs are a separate mandatory standard at 16 CFR part 1231. Clothing storage units are governed by 16 CFR part 1261 under the STURDY Act, and the CPSC's advice is to anchor every one of them, whatever year it was bought.

So the routine is short. Measure the seat height, the table height and the height of every tall piece in the room before you order. Anchor before you fill. And turn the piece over to look at the battery compartment before the first child sits down.
