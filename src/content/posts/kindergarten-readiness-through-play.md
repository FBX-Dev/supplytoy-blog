The question parents ask in the summer before kindergarten is almost always about letters. Should we be doing more of them? Is the child behind because they cannot write their name yet? The thing that actually goes wrong in the first week of school is almost never a letter. It is a coat, a toilet, a lunchbox, and a room with twenty other children in it making noise.

None of those are academic problems, and none of them are solved by a workbook. They are solved by the skills children build while playing, which is the least efficient-sounding and most reliable answer available.

## What readiness is not

Readiness is not the ability to produce letters on demand. By five, most children can print some letters or numbers and copy a triangle and other shapes, and a good number can do none of that yet on the first day of school without it predicting much.

It is also not sitting still for long stretches. Nothing in the milestone lists asks a five-year-old to hold a chair for half an hour. What the lists do describe, repeatedly, is a child who can do things with other people.

## The three thresholds that actually matter

Strip away the academics and three abilities decide how the first term goes.

The first is joining. Can the child walk up to two children who are already playing and find a way in? That is a social skill, and it is practised in every pretend game a four-year-old runs with a friend.

The second is holding a plan when something interrupts it. Can the child decide what they are doing, get interrupted, and return to it? This is the machinery behind getting through a school day that is full of interruptions.

The third is following instructions that have more than one part. Understanding a three-step instruction is something most four-year-olds can do, and the CDC puts not following three-part commands on the list of reasons to talk to a doctor at four. At school, almost every instruction arrives with three parts in it: put your bag on the peg, wash your hands, sit on the carpet.

## Executive function is a play skill

The American Academy of Pediatrics report on the power of play is unusually blunt about all this. Play, it says, builds executive function along with language, social and maths skills, and it buffers the effects of toxic stress. The report's own summary of the evidence is that the importance of play for learning cannot be overstated.

That sentence is worth reading twice, because the way many families prepare a child for school is to remove exactly the thing the evidence points at. Executive function is not a subject. It is the set of habits that lets a child wait, switch, plan and recover, and it is built during games with rules, pretend scenarios that go wrong, and construction projects that fall over.

## The fifteen minutes that changed the morning

The most useful finding in that report for anyone worried about a nervous four-year-old is small and specific. For three and four-year-olds who were anxious about starting school, being allowed to play with a teacher or with other children for fifteen minutes reduced their stress about twice as much as being read a story by the teacher did.

Twice as much, from a quarter of an hour of play. If your child is anxious about a new setting, that is the intervention with the best evidence, and it costs nothing except the willingness to let the child lead.

## What happens when play gets squeezed out

This is not a hypothetical worry about a generation of children. Between 1981 and 1997, children's play time fell by 25 percent. Thirty percent of American kindergarten children no longer have recess at all, because the time was given to academic instruction.

That is the pressure working in one direction, and screens working in another. The same report cites research finding that the typical preschooler watches four and a half hours of television a day. Between the two, the very activity that builds school readiness is the one being trimmed.

## Getting outside is part of the plan

A national survey of 8,950 preschool children and their parents found that only 51 percent of the children went outside with a parent for a walk or play once a day. Ninety-four percent of parents reported safety concerns about outdoor play.

Those two numbers sit awkwardly together, and the practical version of them is modest. The child does not need an expedition. A walk that the child half-leads, in which they choose the route and stop to look at things, is doing the same work as the indoor play version of the story: following a plan, noticing, talking, and moving.

## Screens during the transition

Screens get their own section because they behave differently from everything else here, and because the official guidance is clear enough to act on.

For two to five-year-olds, the recommendation is no more than an hour a day of high-quality programming, watched together with an adult, avoiding fast-paced content, and no screens during meals or in the hour before bed. The AAP's Family Media Use Plan exists to put a household's own rules on paper. Excessive screen time is associated with irregular sleep, language delay in children under three, lower maths scores, less physical activity, social problems and higher body mass index.

Notice that the association list is almost a mirror of the readiness list. Sleep, language, movement and getting along with people are the four things a child needs in the first term of school.

## Practising the parts of the day that are hard

If you want to prepare a child for kindergarten, rehearse the logistics rather than the alphabet, and rehearse them as play.

- Turn the coat on and off thirty times, laughing. Fastening and unfastening clothes is a five-year-old skill, and the coat hook is the least forgiving part of the morning.
- Practise opening the lunchbox and the drink bottle, and packing them back up. A child who can open their own lunch eats more of it.
- Play at being a teacher: give the child three-step instructions and let them give you three-step instructions back.
- Set up a pretend shop, a pretend doctor's waiting room, or a pretend bus. The report's own recommendation for four to six-year-olds is singing and dancing, storytelling, and acting out imagined roles and scenarios.
- Practise the goodbye. Short, cheerful and repeated, not long and solemn.

## When to ask someone else

Most children settle in a few weeks, some take a term, and a few get worse rather than better.

There are specific things that are worth a conversation rather than a wait. At four, those include not following three-part commands, no interest in interactive games or make-believe, not understanding same and different, and being unable to retell a favourite story. Routine developmental screening is recommended at 9, 18 and 30 months, and the milestone lists are monitoring tools rather than a diagnosis, so treat this as the prompt for a conversation with a pediatrician or the school rather than a verdict.

## What does not help

- Flashcards and letter drills for a four-year-old. Copying a few capital letters is a milestone, and drilling it is not how it arrives.
- Cutting play to make room for preparation. That removes the mechanism and keeps the goal.
- Sitting practice. Nothing in the guidance asks a four-year-old to practise stillness.
- A busy schedule of classes. Twenty minutes of led activity is not the same thing as an hour of the child choosing what happens.
- Treating the first bad morning as evidence. The second week is often worse than the first, and it usually turns.

## The bottom line

The children who settle fastest into kindergarten are not the ones who arrived knowing their letters. They are the ones who can join a game, keep a plan, and follow three steps in a row, and every one of those is built by play rather than by instruction.

So spend the summer that way. Let the child lead pretend scenarios, give instructions with three parts in them, get outside daily, rehearse the coat and the lunchbox, and keep the screens inside the guidance. Then, if the first week is rough, remember that fifteen minutes of play moves a nervous four-year-old more than anything else we know how to do.
