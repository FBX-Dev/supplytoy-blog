The electric ride-on car is parked in the hallway overnight, plugged into the charger it came with, next to the coat rack and the pram. The remote-control truck is on charge in a child's bedroom while everyone sleeps. A spare battery sits in a kitchen drawer at full charge, waiting to be swapped in.

Every one of those arrangements is ordinary, and every one of them is the sort of detail that appears in the incident reports. Lithium batteries store a great deal of energy in a small package, and when they fail they fail with flame rather than with smoke and a smell. The reason this matters more than it should is that the rules governing them have not caught up with the products.

## What five years of incident reports add up to

In the proposed rule it published in March 2025, the Consumer Product Safety Commission set out what it had counted over five years, from 2019 to 2023, for lithium-ion batteries and electrical systems in micromobility products: 227 separate incidents, covering fires, explosions, gas release, burns, overheating and smoke inhalation.

Of those 227 incidents, 90 were linked to 39 deaths and 181 injuries. Within the total, 39 of the 227 events involved more than one person being killed or hurt. In other words, a battery fire that reaches the point of an incident report is rarely a single-person event, which matches what a fire in a hallway or a stairwell physically does.

Those figures come from the regulator's own rulemaking file, and they are the numbers to keep in mind when deciding where a charger is allowed to live. The products in the reports are electric bikes and scooters rather than toys, but the chemistry and the charging habits are identical, and a ride-on car gets plugged into the same socket in the same hallway by the same family.

## The rule that was proposed and then withdrawn

The sequence is worth setting out, because it explains why this article has no federal standard to point at.

The commission published the proposed rule on 26 March 2025, and on 30 April 2025 it voted to move the proposal into the rulemaking process. The proposal was then withdrawn. As of September 2026 there is no mandatory federal standard in the United States covering these batteries and electrical systems.

That is not the same as saying these products are fine. It means that the protective layer that exists for most children's products is missing here, and that the standard-setting that has happened has been done by certification bodies and by city governments rather than by federal rule. San Francisco, for instance, has required micromobility devices to be certified to UL or a European standard since 2024, and limits how many devices a single household may store. The voluntary standards that exist are UL 2271 for battery packs, UL 2849 for a complete vehicle electrical system including battery, motor, charger and wiring, and UL 2272 for self-balancing scooters.

## What the city data and the product warnings show

Two other bodies of public information round out the picture.

New York City's own count, published in July 2024, recorded 733 fires caused by lithium-ion batteries in micromobility devices since 2019, with 29 deaths and 442 injuries in the city. The trend behind those totals is what made it a policy problem: these battery fires rose from 44 in 2020 to 220 in 2022.

Alongside that, the regulator has issued product-level warnings. On 24 November 2025 it warned about certain batteries used in Rad Power Bikes products, identifying two model numbers and reporting 31 known battery fires and 12 reports of property damage; the company disputes the finding. In July 2025 it warned about a FENGQS F7 Pro electric bike, saying the battery could overheat and catch fire, with nine known fire reports, and the firm declined to agree to an acceptable recall.

The pattern in that pair of warnings is the useful part for a parent. By the time a regulator issues a warning about a specific product, the failures have usually been happening for a while, which means the household habits below are doing more work than any list of models to avoid.

## Thing one: where the charger lives

Charging position is the change with the largest effect, and it costs nothing to get right.

- Do not charge in a doorway, a hallway, a stairwell or anywhere that is part of the only route out of the building.
- Keep the charger away from anything that burns quickly: coats, curtains, soft furnishings, cardboard, piles of laundry, the pram.
- Do not charge in a child's bedroom, and do not charge anything in a bedroom overnight.
- Do not leave a battery charging while everyone in the house is asleep, or while nobody is home.
- Charge on a hard, non-flammable surface rather than on carpet or a wooden shelf, where the heat has nowhere to go.
- Keep an alarm that will wake you in the room or corridor where charging happens, and test it.

## Thing two: the charger and the timing

The failures that start during charging usually involve the wrong charger or the wrong timing.

- Use only the charger that came with the product. A charger from a different device, or a cheap replacement bought online, is the single most common way a battery is pushed outside its design.
- Unplug when the battery is full, and do not leave it permanently on charge. Overnight top-ups and all-day trickle charging are the habits worth breaking.
- Do not store a battery at full charge for long periods, and do not leave batteries or chargers in a hot car or in direct summer sun.
- Never modify, dismantle or "upgrade" a battery to get more speed or range out of a ride-on toy. This is the one intervention that turns a product failure into a foreseeable one.
- Let a hot battery cool before charging it, and stop charging immediately if the pack or the charger is unusually warm, swollen, hissing or smelling sweet and chemical.
- Watch for a battery that takes longer to charge, runs down faster, or has a cable end that is discoloured. Retire the whole pack rather than continuing to use it.

## Thing three: what to do with a dead battery

Disposal is the part almost every household gets wrong, and the mistake is genuinely dangerous rather than merely untidy.

A lithium-ion battery must not go into household rubbish, into a general or kerbside recycling bin, or into the battery drop-off box in a shop. A damaged or partly-charged pack can ignite inside a collection vehicle or a recycling facility, and shop collection boxes are not built for lithium chemistry. The correct destination is a municipal household hazardous waste collection point, and the practical step before driving there is to telephone and confirm that they accept lithium-ion batteries, because not all of them do.

Until it is handed over, keep the pack away from heat and out of the reach of children, and do not tape over or cover the terminals to "make it safe" by guesswork. A swollen battery should be moved to a cool place outdoors away from anything flammable, and treated as an urgent disposal job rather than a future errand.

## Choosing a ride-on or remote-control toy

The certifications above are the only useful filter available at the point of purchase, because there is no federal standard to fall back on.

Look for the UL mark on the product or its packaging, and where the details are given, look for the standard that belongs to that class of product: UL 2271 for a battery pack, UL 2849 for a complete electrical system, UL 2272 for a self-balancing board. A [ride-on toy that carries a recognised electrical certification](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=ride+on+toy+ul+certified+battery&tag=cnxixi-20) is not a guarantee, but it means someone outside the seller has tested the charging system and the wiring rather than only the paint and the decals.

At home, the cheap additions are worth as much as the certification. A [fire-resistant charging bag for the battery and charger](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=fireproof+charging+bag+for+li+po+batteries&tag=cnxixi-20) gives a failing pack somewhere to fail, and a [smoke alarm](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=smoke+alarm&tag=cnxixi-20) in the room where charging happens is the thing that turns an incident into a wake-up rather than a surprise.

One more point about gifts, since toys with lithium cells cluster around the end of the year. A toy that arrives as a present comes without the household having chosen it, and the charger that goes with it is often the only one anyone has. Read the label, keep the charger with the toy, and do not substitute one from the drawer.

## What we would not do

- Charge anything overnight in a bedroom or in a hallway that is a route out of the house.
- Use a charger that did not come with the product, however well it seems to fit.
- Leave a battery on permanent charge, or at full charge for weeks on end.
- Modify or rebuild a battery pack for more performance.
- Keep using a pack that is swollen, hot, hissing or discoloured, or a charger with a burnt cable end.
- Put a lithium battery in household rubbish, a kerbside recycling bin or a shop collection box.

## The bottom line

The regulator counted 227 battery incidents over five years, 90 of them linked to 39 deaths and 181 injuries, then proposed a mandatory standard in March 2025 and withdrew it. As of September 2026 there is no federal rule for these batteries, so the household is the standard. Charge in a place you would be willing to have a fire, and not while you sleep; use only the charger that came in the box and unplug when the pack is full; and take a dead or damaged lithium battery to a municipal household hazardous waste collection point after telephoning to confirm they accept it. When you buy, look for the UL mark.
