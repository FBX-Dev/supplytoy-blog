A four-year-old swallows one small magnetic ball. Nothing happens. He is fine. Hours later, he swallows a second. That second swallow is when an ordinary afternoon becomes a surgical emergency, and there may be no warning sign at all in between.

Magnet hazards are the rare case where the danger does not scale with how much was swallowed. It scales with how many, and where they end up relative to each other.

## Why one magnet is not the problem and two are

A single small magnet often passes through the digestive tract without incident. Two magnets are a completely different situation. Once they are in the body, they can attract each other through the walls of the intestine and clamp the tissue between them. Whatever is caught in that pinch loses its blood supply, and the consequences can include perforation, twisting or obstruction, infection, and sepsis. A swallowed magnet can also attract other iron-containing objects already in the body.

The clinical pattern that makes this so treacherous is the delay. There may be no symptoms at first. The child seems entirely well. Tissue damage then becomes apparent later, sometimes days later, by which point the injury is substantial. That is why a suspected magnet ingestion is not a wait-and-check-the-diaper situation. It needs prompt medical assessment even when the child looks fine.

## The 50 kG²mm² threshold

Loose magnet hazards in the United States are governed by **16 CFR part 1262**. The CPSC adopted the magnet mandatory standard on **September 7, 2022**, and it was published in the Federal Register on **September 21, 2022 (87 FR 57756)**. It applies to regulated magnet products **manufactured after October 21, 2022**.

The technical requirement is the one number worth carrying around in your head. Any loose or separable magnet that fits entirely inside the CPSC small parts cylinder (**16 CFR 1501.4**) must have a **flux index below 50 kG²mm²**. Flux index is a measure of magnetic strength in a defined geometry. Below 50 is the line. Above it, the magnet is strong enough to clamp tissue across the wall of an intestine rather than pass through.

Here is the part that trips people up. There is an exemption: **toys subject to 16 CFR part 1250** — that is, **ASTM F963** — are **excluded**, and **toy magnet risk is covered by ASTM F963** instead. A compliant magnetic toy is not unregulated. It is regulated under the toy standard. There is also a separate **ASTM F3458-21**, which covers packaging, labeling, and storage case requirements for magnet sets intended for users **14 and older**.

## How real the risk is

The CPSC estimated **about 26,600 magnet ingestions treated in emergency departments between 2010 and 2021**, with cases **rising every year since 2018**. There have been **7 known deaths associated with dangerous magnet ingestion**, two of them outside the United States.

The most typical scenario is not a toddler at all. It is an older child or teenager using small but powerful magnets to imitate a lip piercing or tongue piercing, holding them in the mouth, and swallowing them. That detail changes how you store the things: a set marketed to a fourteen-year-old is still a set of high-strength magnets sitting in a house that may also contain younger children.

## What a well-made magnetic toy looks like

Magnets that are fully enclosed and cannot come loose are the entire ballgame.

- **Sealed internal magnets.** In magnetic tiles, the magnet sits inside a welded or ultrasonically sealed plastic shell, with no exposed disc anywhere.
- **A toy that survives a pull test.** Pull hard on every magnetic element. If anything shifts, rattles, or hints at separation, retire it.
- **No rattle when shaken.** A magnet that has broken free inside a hollow toy will announce itself.
- **No exposed disc magnets on products for young children.** Decorative closures and novelty items with visible discs are the ones to keep away from a child who still mouths everything.

Small magnetic balls sold as desk toys, fidgets, or building sets are the highest-risk category in an ordinary home. They are small enough to be swallowed easily, strong enough to clamp tissue, and sold in packs of dozens, which makes a missing one genuinely hard to notice.

## If a magnet is swallowed

Treat it as urgent, and treat it as urgent even if the child seems completely fine. Make contact with a professional rather than waiting: **Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222**, free and available 24 hours a day, or **CPSC at 800-638-2772** for product-related reports. Injuries can be filed at **SaferProducts.gov**.

Two absolutes apply. Do not wait for symptoms, and do not attempt to make the child vomit. If you know or suspect that **more than one magnet**, or a magnet plus another metal object, was swallowed, say so explicitly when you make contact, because that detail changes the urgency of the response.

One more point is worth repeating, because it is the reason families delay: **symptoms are not a reliable guide to how serious this is.** A child can have a pair of magnets pinching tissue and still eat, play, and sleep normally for a time. The absence of pain is not evidence that nothing is happening. If you know or suspect a magnet was swallowed, the decision to seek care should be based on the swallowing event itself, not on how well the child appears an hour later.

## Storage in a house with more than one child

Most magnet incidents in a home do not involve a toddler who found a loose ball on the floor. They involve an older child's toy left within reach, or a set put away with a piece missing. Three habits cover most of that gap.

- **Store high-strength magnet sets above the reach of the youngest child, in a closed container rather than an open bin.** A drawer at floor level is functionally an open invitation.
- **Count the pieces when you tidy up.** Sets of small balls and rods are sold in fixed quantities, which makes a missing piece detectable if you are actually looking.
- **Keep the storage case with the toy it came with.** A set designed with a case and a lid stays contained; the same set tipped into a general toy bin loses pieces quietly.

## Buying for a household with mixed ages

If children under six live in the house, the simplest and most defensible position is to keep strong loose magnets out of it entirely.

For building play at younger ages, [sealed magnetic tiles](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=sealed+magnetic+tiles&tag=cnxixi-20) are built so the magnet cannot be reached at all. For the same open-ended construction play with no magnetic hazard whatsoever, [wooden building blocks](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=wooden+building+blocks+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) do the job with nothing to swallow and nothing to clamp. And for the teenager who genuinely wants the desk-toy set, a [locked storage case](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=locked+storage+case&tag=cnxixi-20) keeps it contained — and contained is the only version of that purchase I would make in a house with a toddler.

## What we would not buy

- **Loose neodymium ball sets** in a home with children under six. No exceptions.
- **Magnetic building sets with visibly loose discs** or rattling interior components.
- **Novelty jewellery and fake piercings with small magnets.** These are the actual source of a large share of the serious cases.
- **Any magnetic toy with a crack in the shell.** A cracked shell means the magnet is one impact away from being loose.

## The bottom line

One small swallowed magnet is usually an inconvenience. Two are an emergency, because they can attract through intestinal walls and clamp tissue between them, often before any symptom appears. The regulatory line is a **flux index below 50 kG²mm²** for any loose magnet that fits inside the small parts cylinder, and toy magnets are covered by **ASTM F963** rather than by part 1262.

At home, the rule is short: enclosed magnets only, pulled and shaken before you trust them, and no loose high-strength magnet sets in a house with young children. If you suspect a swallow, call **1-800-222-1222** or **800-638-2772**, and do not wait for symptoms to appear.
