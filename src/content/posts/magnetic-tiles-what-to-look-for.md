Magnetic tiles are the rare toy that a two-year-old and a seven-year-old will fight over, which is why they have become a default gift. They are also a category where the difference between a set that lasts five years and a set that ends up in the bin after a month is invisible from the product photo.

Almost everything that matters on a magnetic tile happens where you cannot see it: inside the plastic, along the seam, and in the metal that holds the whole thing together.

## The one question that matters most: is the magnet sealed in

A magnetic tile is a plastic shell with magnets enclosed inside it. That enclosure is the entire safety case for the toy.

If the shell is welded or glued shut well enough that the magnet cannot be shaken, chewed, or pried out, the tile is a safe building toy even for a child who mouths everything. If it is not, the tile is a container holding a small powerful magnet, and it will fail at the exact age when children put everything in their mouths.

So the first test is the simplest one. Pick up a tile and shake it. If you hear a rattle, put it down. A magnet that moves freely inside the shell is a magnet that is working its way toward the seam.

The second test is the seam itself. Run a fingernail along the edge. A well-made tile has a consistent, flat join with no lip and no gap. A cheap tile has a visible line where two halves were pressed together and sometimes a fine ridge of plastic that can be picked at. On a toy that will be chewed, that ridge is a starting point.

The third test is the corners and the rivets. Some designs use a metal pin or rivet at each corner to hold the two halves together. If those pins can be gripped and pulled, or if they show any play, treat the whole set as suspect.

## Magnetic tiles are not magnetic beads, and the difference is not cosmetic

This is the point where buyers get confused, so it is worth stating plainly.

A magnetic tile contains magnets that are sealed inside a plastic shell and are, in normal use, not accessible. A set of small magnetic balls or cubes is the opposite: the magnets are the toy, they are handled loose, they are small enough to swallow, and they are strong enough to attract each other through the walls of the intestine.

That difference in form is what creates the difference in risk. Swallowed magnets do not pass through quietly. They clamp onto each other across layers of tissue and hold the bowel in a loop, which can lead to perforation, twisting, obstruction, and infection. CPSC has estimated roughly 26,600 magnet ingestions treated in emergency departments between 2010 and 2021, with the numbers rising since 2018, and seven known deaths associated with dangerous magnet ingestion.

None of that describes a properly made magnetic tile. All of it describes a loose high-powered magnet set. If you are buying tiles, you are buying the first thing. If a relative is buying magnetic balls for a teenager in a house with a toddler, that is a different conversation, and the toddler is the one at risk.

## What the rules actually cover

In the United States, the CPSC's mandatory magnet standard is 16 CFR part 1262, adopted in 2022. It applies to regulated magnet products and requires that any loose or separable magnet capable of fitting entirely inside the small-parts cylinder have a flux index below 50 kG²mm².

There is a specific exemption worth knowing about. Toys that are subject to 16 CFR part 1250, which is how the toy safety standard ASTM F963 is made mandatory under CPSIA, are exempt from that magnet section. In plain terms, the magnetic risk in toys is covered by ASTM F963 rather than by the standalone magnet rule.

The current version of that standard is ASTM F963-23, which took effect on 20 April 2024 and replaced F963-17. It applies to toys for children under 14, sets a lead limit of 90 ppm in paint, restricts eight soluble heavy metals, limits phthalates, and covers small parts, sharp edges, and battery accessibility. A compliant tile set should be tested by a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory, carry a tracking label, and be covered by a Children's Product Certificate.

Why does this matter to a shopper? Because it tells you what to look for and what to ignore. The relevant question is not whether a listing mentions magnets. It is whether the brand can tell you which standard the set was tested to, and whether the enclosure survived the use and abuse testing that standard requires.

## What to look at on the box

- The age label. If a set is labelled for three years and up, it was designed with the assumption that mouthing has mostly stopped. That is a signal about the enclosure, and about small parts generally.
- Piece count versus usable shapes. A large number of pieces is not automatically better, but there is a floor below which you cannot build anything with a roof. Count the squares and triangles separately, since triangles are what make the interesting shapes possible.
- Whether the listing says anything about the magnets at all. A brand that explains how the magnets are enclosed is usually a brand that thought about it.
- Whether the pieces are sold as compatible with the major tile brands. Compatibility is a genuine practical feature, and a set that only works with itself is a closed system.

## What to check when the box arrives

Do these four things before the toy goes into the playroom.

1. Shake every tile. Any rattle is a return.
2. Inspect all four edges of a sample of tiles under a lamp. Look for gaps, flash, and inconsistent joins.
3. Try the drop test yourself. Drop one tile onto a hard floor from counter height and check whether the seam has opened. If it has, send the set back.
4. Check the strength. Two tiles should hold together firmly with a slight pull. If they slide apart with almost no resistance, the magnetic strength is too low for the size of the pieces, and towers will fall constantly.

## Size and how many pieces are enough

If the child is under three, the practical question is the size of the smallest piece rather than the size of the set. Large tiles are easier to grip, harder to swallow, and much easier to build with for a child who is still developing a pincer grip. Look at [large magnetic tiles for toddlers](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=large+magnetic+tiles+toddlers&tag=cnxixi-20) and check the dimensions in the listing rather than trusting the words on the box.

For a first set, twenty to thirty pieces is usually more useful than sixty, because a child who can build a complete shape with every piece has a better experience than a child who has a pile of parts. Add a set of [magnetic tiles with wheels](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=magnetic+tile+set+with+wheels&tag=cnxixi-20) later if the play has moved toward vehicles; the wheels keep a build in use for another year.

When you do expand, buy more of the same brand rather than a second brand with different magnet polarity, because mismatched polarity is the most common reason two sets will not stick together.

## What we would not buy

- Any tile that rattles, or any set where a tile has already cracked in the box.
- Small magnetic cubes, balls, or rods sold as building toys for children. These are not the same product as tiles and should not be in a home with young children.
- Tile sets with no age label at all.
- Sets with fabric, foam, or sticker components glued on, which lift off and become the small part.
- Budget sets with glossy photos of complex builds but fewer than a dozen triangles, which cannot actually make the pictures shown.

## Supervision, and the rule that solves most of it

Nothing here means a magnetic tile set is dangerous. It means the safe version is the one where the magnets stay where they were put, and it means the check is cheap to do: shake the tile.

Keep loose high-powered magnets and magnetic ball sets out of the house entirely while there is a child under six, including teenagers' desk toys. If you ever suspect a child has swallowed one or more magnets, treat it as an emergency and get medical care immediately rather than waiting for symptoms.

## The bottom line

Buy the tile, not the count. Check the age label, shake every tile, inspect the seams, and confirm the brand can tell you what standard the set was tested for. Then buy fewer pieces than you think you need, in a large size, and add more of the same brand later.
