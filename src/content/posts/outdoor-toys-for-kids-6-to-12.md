Every outdoor toy is sold as though the only question is what it does. The question that actually decides whether it is still being used next summer is where it lives on a wet Tuesday in November, and how long it takes to set up on the one afternoon everyone is free.

That reframes the whole category. Instead of sorting outdoor toys by function, sort them by whether they can stay outside overnight, and three tiers fall out of it. Each tier has its own failure mode, and almost every disappointing purchase is a toy bought from one tier while living in another.

## Tier one: it never comes inside

These are the swings, climbing frames, sandpits, and playhouses. They are bought once, installed once, and left out, and that is the whole point of them.

The problem is that the standards you rely on elsewhere do not fully apply. The current mandatory toy safety standard, ASTM F963-23, covers toys for children under fourteen, but it explicitly excludes playground equipment, bicycles, and kites, each of which has its own rules or none. That does not mean a climbing frame is unsafe. It means that the inspection is your job rather than a box tick, so check the fixings, the anchors, and the joints yourself.

For this tier, pay for the frame and not the trim. A wooden climbing structure with a wide footprint and thick timber will outlast a plastic one with a slide and a telescope. Look at how it is anchored rather than how it looks, because a structure that tips is worse than a structure that is boring. And check the fall surface underneath, since a climbing frame over hard paving is a different object from the same frame over grass or bark.

If the space is limited, [backyard play equipment](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=backyard+play+equipment+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) in a compact footprint is usually a better buy than a large set that has to be squeezed into a corner, because the child needs clear room around the structure, not just underneath it.

## Tier two: out and back every day

These are the balls, bats, rackets, chalk, buckets, and sidewalk props that come out after school and go back in at dinner. This tier has almost no design problems. It has one logistics problem.

A toy in this tier gets used if the distance between the child and the toy is short and the effort of putting it away is small. If the bats live in a shed at the bottom of the garden, they will be used less than the ones in a bin by the back door, even if the ones in the shed are better. The honest constraint is the ninety seconds it takes to tidy up, multiplied by how tired the adult is at seven in the evening.

So buy a bin, a hook rail, or a waterproof box, and put it where the play actually happens. Then buy fewer of this tier than you think you need, because loose outdoor toys are the ones that end up wet, mud-covered, and lost behind a shed.

## Tier three: an event, not a toy

These are the inflatable slides, the paddling pools, the big water tables, and anything needing a pump. None of them are bad. They are simply not toys in the sense the word is usually meant, because using them requires a decision, a forecast, and a block of adult time.

The arithmetic is worth doing honestly before buying. If inflating takes fifteen minutes and drying takes thirty, the toy will be used a handful of times a season and the adult will resent every one of them. If you buy something in this tier, buy it knowing that it is an occasion rather than a habit, and prefer versions that fold flat and drain without you having to lift them.

## Space is the real cost, and it is counted in steps

Nobody measures their garden before buying. Do it anyway, and measure it in adult strides rather than metres, because strides are what you will actually notice when the toy is in place.

Most wheeled toys need a hard, smooth surface and about ten strides of clear run to be fun. Most ball games need a wall or a fence and at least fifteen strides. A climbing frame needs clear air around it, not just a footprint. If your outdoor space is a shared courtyard or a balcony, the honest answer is that tier one is unavailable to you, and the money is better spent on wheeled toys and a good local park.

This is also where the injury numbers become relevant, in a practical way rather than a frightening one. Non-motorised scooters are the single largest identified category for toy-related injuries, accounting for 23% of estimated toy-related injuries in 2023, roughly 53,000 injuries, and more than one in five toy-related injuries among children under fifteen. But five of the recently reported deaths from 2022 and 2023 involving non-motorised scooters were collisions with motor vehicles, which tells you the risk lives at the road rather than in the driveway. Where a child rides matters more than what they ride.

## The helmet rule parents get backwards

Most parents know a helmet is a good idea for a scooter or a bike and stop thinking there. Two details are less obvious.

The first is which helmet. There is no separate United States standard for scooter or tricycle helmets, and CPSC points to the bicycle helmet standard, 16 CFR Part 1203, as the one to use. A skateboard helmet is a genuinely different product, tested to ASTM F1492, which is a multiple-impact standard, and there is no federal standard for skateboard helmets at all in the United States. This means a helmet that shows only the CPSC 1203 mark is a bicycle helmet even if it is styled to look like a skate helmet, and the sticker inside the shell is the only reliable way to tell. Replace any helmet after a crash, even if it looks perfect, because the foam does not recover once it has been crushed and the damage is usually invisible. Never use a second-hand helmet of unknown history.

The second detail is when to take it off, which is the part that catches people out. Helmets come off before a child climbs on playground equipment or climbs a tree. The United States recorded its first death from a bicycle helmet snagging on playground equipment in 1999, and there have been near-misses since. The gap between a platform and a rail is roughly the size of a helmet strap. This is not a case for more protection. It is a case for removing it.

## Water, and the number parents underestimate

Water play is where outdoor toys and genuine risk overlap, and it is worth being plain about it without being dramatic. Among children aged five to fourteen, drowning is the second leading cause of unintentional injury death, behind motor vehicle collisions. Among children aged one to four it is the leading cause of death from any single cause, which is a different order of magnitude from every other hazard discussed in this article.

The settings matter. For younger children the common location is a pool. For teenagers, the most common setting is natural water, which is exactly where a lot of summer outdoor play happens. If your children are in the older half of this age band, the useful conversation is about open water rather than about the paddling pool.

None of this is a reason to skip water play. It is a reason to buy [water toys for the garden](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=water+toys+for+the+garden+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) rather than anything that implies a child can be left alone with it, and to empty anything that holds water at the end of the session rather than leaving it to stand. If you want wheels as well as water, a bicycle helmet is the right one to add for the scooter or trike, and [kids bike helmets](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+bike+helmet&tag=cnxixi-20) are the standard to look for on the label.

## What we would not buy

- Inflatables that need a pump, a dry day, and two adults, because the setup cost will beat the play value.
- Anything that has to be anchored into ground you cannot dig, which includes most of the tier one category in a rented house.
- Large plastic climbing frames with integrated slides at a low price, because the joints and the anchors are where the money was saved.
- Cheap bats, wickets, and rackets sets that crack in a month, since the replacement cost ends up higher than one decent purchase.
- Sets with thirty small loose parts, because collecting them at dusk is the task that ends outdoor play.
- Toys advertised for three-year-olds that an eight-year-old will use once as something to jump off.

## The bottom line

Decide the tier first. If a toy cannot stay outside permanently, work out where it will live and how long it takes to put away before deciding whether it is worth buying. Measure the space in strides, prefer fewer and better items in each tier, and put the storage where the play happens.

Then buy the helmet that matches the activity, check which standard is on the sticker inside it, and take it off before anyone climbs anything.
