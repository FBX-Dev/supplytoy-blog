In the teething aisle there is a gel, a necklace, and a gel-filled ring, all sitting at eye level, all marketed for the same week of a baby's life. Two of the three are products a regulator has told parents to stop using. The shelf does not say so.

This is a buying guide for a category where the rules are unusually specific. Almost none of them are about the part of the product you notice.

## The pacifier standard, in four checkable requirements

Pacifiers in the United States are governed by 16 CFR Part 1511, a mandatory standard dating from 1977. It is short, and every clause answers a documented failure.

- The shield must not be completely pulled through the test template under a force of not more than 2.0 pounds, which is 8.9 newtons. The shield is the part that stops the whole pacifier going into a mouth, and the point of the test is that it must not fold away under a determined pull.
- The shield must have at least two symmetrically placed holes, each with a minimum dimension of at least 0.20 inches, which is 5 millimetres. Those holes are not decorative. They are the airway if the shield does end up in a mouth.
- The protruding part must be no more than 0.63 inches, which is 16 millimetres, so that the part outside the mouth cannot be levered deep into it.
- After six boil cycles, the pacifier must still pass the small parts test, meaning nothing has come off that a young child could choke on.

The practical summary is that a compliant pacifier is a soft, holey shield with a short protrusion and no loose parts, and it stays that way after being sterilised repeatedly. A hard plastic disc with a nipple and no ventilation is not that product.

## The clause parents never think about: no cord, no ribbon, no chain

The strangulation clause is the one that changes behaviour most, and it exists because the accessory is the hazard rather than the pacifier.

Under 16 CFR 1511.6, a pacifier cannot be sold with any ribbon, cord, string, chain, twine, leather, yarn or similar attachment. Alongside it, the standard requires a mandatory warning, worded exactly like this: Warning—Do Not Tie Pacifier Around Child's Neck as it Presents a Strangulation Danger.

Read together, those two clauses say something simple. The dangerous configuration is not the pacifier in a mouth, it is the pacifier hanging from a neck. So a separate clip or holder is a decision a parent makes, and it should be a short one, and it comes off before sleep. A free strap long enough to slip over a head is the thing the rule was written about.

## Amber teething necklaces: the clearest no in the category

Amber teething necklaces are jewellery placed on an infant's neck, and the Food and Drug Administration's position is a warning rather than a caution.

The agency has received reports of death and serious injury from teething jewellery such as amber necklaces, including strangulation and choking. Its safety communication of 20 December 2018 documents cases including an 18 month old who was strangled by an amber necklace during a nap.

The action that follows is straightforward. Do not buy them, do not accept them as gifts, and if one is already in the house, put it away rather than saving it for an occasion. Nothing on an infant's neck belongs there while they sleep, and the product does not do the job it is sold for.

## Benzocaine teething gels

The second product to walk past is the anaesthetic gel.

The FDA's drug safety communication of 23 May 2018 states that these products should not be used for teething pain in children because they can be dangerous. Benzocaine products can cause a condition called methemoglobinemia, which can be serious and sometimes fatal.

That leaves the question of what does help, and the answer is unglamorous: pressure on the gums from something firm, at room temperature, plus time. If a baby is distressed enough that you are considering a medicated gel, that is a conversation with a pharmacist or a paediatrician rather than a shelf decision. Pain relief is a medical question, and the answers that work are prescribed rather than picked up at a supermarket.

## Liquid-filled teethers, and what to buy instead

The third item is the gel-filled ring, and here the instruction is about material rather than risk category.

FDA guidance for teethers is that a child should be given a firm rubber teether and not a liquid-filled one, and that teething rings should not be frozen. The reason to care is what happens when the filling escapes: a cracked seam on a gel-filled ring puts the contents into a mouth. In 2022, Health Canada ordered a recall of Nûby liquid-filled teethers over a Bacillus cereus risk, which is a concrete example of the failure mode rather than a hypothetical one.

The check at home takes seconds. Squeeze the ring, look at the seams, and retire anything that weeps, smells unusual, or has been through a dishwasher cycle it was not designed for. If a filled teether does split in a child's mouth, treat it as an exposure and call your local poison control centre rather than waiting to see.

## The one thing that does belong in the cot

It is worth ending the teething discussion with a positive, because there is one product in this category that professional guidance actively recommends rather than merely tolerates.

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends offering a pacifier at nap time and at night to reduce the risk of sudden infant death syndrome. That recommendation is about a plain pacifier. It is not about a pacifier on a clip, a strap or a chain, and the standard above is the reason.

## Phthalates: eight substances, one limit

The plastic side of this category is covered by a permanent restriction that is worth knowing about because it is one of the few in the area that applies automatically.

Under the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act, eight phthalates are permanently prohibited in children's toys and child care articles at levels above 0.1 percent. The list grew from the original three to eight, the vote took place on 18 October 2017, the rule was published on 27 October 2017 at 82 FR 49938, and it took effect on 25 April 2018, codified at 16 CFR 1307.

Pacifiers and teethers fall inside that coverage, which means a compliant product cannot be made with those plasticisers above the limit. It is a reason to prefer products from makers who state their compliance over unbranded soft plastic with no paperwork at all.

## What we would not buy

- Amber teething necklaces, in any form, for any age.
- Anaesthetic teething gels containing benzocaine, for any child.
- Liquid-filled teethers, and anything intended to be frozen before use.
- A pacifier with fewer than two ventilation holes, or with a rigid shield that does not flex.
- Pacifier holders with a long or elastic strap, which stretch back into the loop they were meant to avoid.
- Unbranded soft plastic teethers with no compliance statement, given that phthalate limits are the one area where paperwork is the product.

## The bottom line

16 CFR Part 1511 sets the pacifier rules, and they are all about preventing the product from disappearing into a mouth or tightening around a neck: a shield that resists a pull of not more than 2.0 pounds or 8.9 newtons, at least two symmetrical holes each of at least 0.20 inches or 5 millimetres, a protrusion of no more than 0.63 inches or 16 millimetres, and no separable small parts after six boil cycles. A pacifier may not be sold with any cord, ribbon or chain, and the mandatory warning on the packaging says not to tie one around a child's neck.

Three products in the same aisle are worth leaving there. Amber necklaces, because the FDA has received reports of death and serious injury including strangulation; benzocaine gels, which the FDA says should not be used for teething pain because they can cause serious and sometimes fatal methemoglobinemia; and liquid-filled teethers, where firm rubber is the alternative and freezing is not. A plain pacifier at sleep is the one thing here that the guidance recommends.
