A child arrives at the playground on a scooter, wearing a helmet, and does the reasonable thing with it, which is to keep wearing it while climbing the frame. Every instinct says that is the safer choice. It is the one habit in this whole area that consistently runs the wrong way.

The rest of playground and outdoor safety is less counterintuitive and more administrative. Children are injured outdoors for boring reasons, and the biggest single category of toy-related injury in the country is not a toy most parents think of as dangerous at all.

## The rule that runs backwards: helmets come off

Helmets come off before playground equipment and before climbing trees. The first US death from a bicycle helmet snagging on playground equipment was in 1999, and there have been further close calls since.

The mechanism is simple once you see it. A helmet is wider than a head, and a wide object can pass through a gap that a head cannot. On a climbing frame, a slide rail, or between the rungs of a ladder, the helmet catches, and the child's body keeps going. A helmet that is doing its job on a bike becomes a hook on a climbing frame.

The handover point is the gate. Helmet on for wheels, helmet off at the equipment.

## Playground equipment is covered by a different standard

This is worth knowing before you assume that a certification mark on a toy means something about a climbing frame.

ASTM F963-23 is the mandatory US toy safety standard and it covers toys intended for children under 14. It explicitly does not cover bicycles, non-powder guns, kites or playground equipment. That last exclusion is not an oversight: public playground equipment falls under its own category of rules, and home play structures are treated as a different product class again.

In practice, this means you cannot point to one certification and consider the question closed. What you can do is inspect the things that actually fail. Look for the gap sizes and the places where a small body can get through but a head cannot. Check that the hardware is tight and that nothing has a protruding bolt thread at child height. Look at the base of the structure, because loose footings and lifted edges are where falls begin.

## Scooters are the largest single injury category

If you had to name the most dangerous thing in the average garage, the answer from the national data is the non-motorised scooter.

Of an estimated 231,700 toy-related injuries treated in US emergency departments in calendar year 2023, non-motorised scooters accounted for 23%, about 53,000 cases, and for more than one in five toy-related injuries in children under 15. That makes it the single largest identified category by a wide margin.

The rest of the 2023 picture is worth carrying around, because it explains what you are actually protecting. Of those injuries, 57% were male. Some 72% of the injured were 14 or under, 67% were 12 or under, and 36% were 4 or under. Head and face injuries made up 45% of the total, lacerations 21%, and 92% were treated and released. Ten toy-related deaths of children 14 and under were reported for 2023, with ages from 7 months to 6 years, and over 2021 to 2023 there were 40 reported toy-related deaths of children 14 and under.

The trend is mildly reassuring rather than alarming. From 2016 to 2023, injuries fell about 5% for under-15s, from 175,600 to 167,500, and about 8% for under-13s, from 167,800 to 154,700. The category is getting slightly safer and the head-and-face share is still the number that drives helmet behaviour.

Two things follow from the scooter numbers. A helmet belongs on a scooter every single time, including the two-minute ride to the corner, because the worst scooter outcome in the data is a child struck by a car rather than a child who fell. And a scooter with a stiff or vague brake, or a handlebar that folds under load, is not a scooter to keep.

## Bikes, skateboards and the sticker inside the helmet

Every bicycle helmet sold in the United States must meet CPSC 16 CFR Part 1203, which has been mandatory since 1999. A CPSC sticker therefore means the helmet passed a bicycle standard, and nothing more than that.

The distinction matters as soon as a child moves from wheels to a board. ASTM F1492 is the skateboard standard, and it tests for repeated impacts rather than a single high-energy one. There is no US federal standard for skateboard helmets at all, and an American helmet can be sold for skateboarding with no certification whatsoever as long as nobody markets it for cycling. The word skate on the packaging is not a claim about testing.

Two useful pieces of straightforward guidance sit here. The CPSC has said that tricycles and scooters have no separate helmet standard, so a bicycle helmet is the correct choice for both. And a helmet must be replaced after any impact, even if it looks completely undamaged, because the foam crushes and stops absorbing energy in a way the outside shell does not reveal. A [kids bike helmet meeting CPSC 1203](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+bike+helmet+cpsc+1203&tag=cnxixi-20) is the baseline for everything with wheels, and if the child skates, look for [a skate helmet with ASTM F1492](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=skate+helmet+astm+f1492+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) printed on the certification label inside the shell.

Skateboarding follows an age line rather than a skill line. The AAP advises that children under 5 should not skateboard, and that children aged 6 to 10 need supervision while they do. Pads are the difference between a scrape and a stop to the afternoon, so [knee and elbow pads](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+knee+and+elbow+pads+set&tag=cnxixi-20) are worth having before the board is, not after the first fall.

## Water: the numbers worth knowing

Drowning is the outdoor hazard where the difference between a good outcome and the worst one is measured in seconds, and the scale of it is larger than most parents assume.

The CDC reports about 4,000 fatal unintentional drownings a year in the United States, an average of 11 a day, plus roughly 8,000 non-fatal drownings a year. More children aged 1 to 4 die from drowning than from any other cause of death. For ages 5 to 14, drowning is the second leading cause of unintentional injury death, after motor vehicle crashes. Death rates for ages 0 to 17 fell 38% between 1999 and 2019, from 1.6 to 1.0 per 100,000, with the 1 to 4 age group consistently highest, falling from 3.2 to 2.4.

Where it happens changes with age, and that shapes what supervision has to look like. In 2018 and 2019 the most common setting was bathtubs for children under 1, swimming pools for ages 1 to 4 and 5 to 13, and natural water for ages 14 to 17.

The practical consequences are dull and effective. Empty anything that holds water when it is not in use, including buckets and paddling pools. Stay within arm's reach of a child in or near water rather than watching from a chair. And understand what flotation toys are: they are toys, not rescue equipment, and two of the toy-related deaths reported for 2023 involved children drowning while using flotation toys. A proper [US Coast Guard approved life jacket](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=us+coast+guard+approved+kids+life+jacket&tag=cnxixi-20) is a different product with a different purpose than a swimming aid shaped like an animal.

## Motorised rides and e-scooters

At the motorised end of the category, the guidance is unusually blunt. The AAP advises that children under 16 should not ride or operate a motorised e-scooter. That is not a recommendation about helmets or supervision; it is an age line.

The reason it exists is that these devices reach speeds and share space with vehicles, and the injury pattern when a child comes off one at speed is different from anything a push scooter produces. If a child in the 9 to 12 range is asking for one, the honest answer is that the category is the wrong category, and a good push scooter plus a helmet is where that energy should go.

## Surfaces, heights and the boring checks

Most of the remaining risk is installation rather than play.

- Check the ground under the equipment. Grass wears away, and the fall height a child is climbing to has to match what is underneath.
- Look for entrapment gaps, especially anywhere a helmet-width opening exists at head height.
- Tighten hardware at the start of the season and again a month later, because new structures settle.
- Remove drawstrings and cord necklaces before climbing, and take off loose clothing that can catch.
- Look up during heat waves, because metal slides and dark plastic surfaces stay hot well into the evening.

## What we would not buy

- A skateboard or scooter helmet with no readable certification label inside it.
- A second-hand helmet of unknown history, however good it looks.
- A scooter whose handlebar stem folds rather than locks firmly.
- Any flotation device marketed as making a child safe in water.
- A trampoline bought before a conversation about netting, ground clearance and who will be watching.

## The bottom line

The two rules that matter most are both simple. Helmet on for wheels, helmet off for climbing. And the scooter, not the skateboard, is the biggest single source of toy-related injuries in the national data, which is the reason a helmet belongs on every short ride.

Everything else is maintenance. Check what the equipment is standing on, tighten the hardware, know where water is, and keep the age lines for skateboarding and e-scooters where the AAP put them. Outdoors is where children take the risks they need. The job is not to remove the risk, it is to keep it in a place where the landing is survivable.
