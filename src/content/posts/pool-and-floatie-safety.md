A float is a toy that lets a child do something they cannot do on their own, in the one environment where getting it wrong is final. That is the whole problem, and it is why the flotation sections of the federal toy death report read differently from the rest of it.

Three of the six toy-related deaths recorded for children aged 14 and under in 2024 were drownings involving flotation toys. Three children, three pools, three decisions that looked sensible at the time. Each one is worth walking through slowly, because the failure in each case was not stupidity or neglect. It was the ordinary assumption that the thing holding the child up would keep holding the child up.

## The label said 33 pounds. The child weighed 47.

The first case is a four-year-old girl who weighed 47 pounds. She was in a seated swim ring, the kind sold for very small children, and it carried a label stating that the ring was for infants weighing no more than 33 pounds. The aunt who was watching her turned her attention away for about a minute. When she looked back, the ring and the child had flipped over together, and the girl was underneath it. She was taken to hospital and died of drowning.

A weight limit on a buoyancy device is not a suggestion. A ring rated for 33 pounds carries a 47-pound child lower in the water, at a worse angle, with less of the ring above the surface, and it has nothing in reserve to right itself when the child tips. Past that number you are asking the product to do something it was never built to do.

The practical version is short. If a child is in or near water, what they wear should be [a life jacket sized by weight and marked as US Coast Guard approved](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=toddler+life+jacket+us+coast+guard&tag=cnxixi-20), fastened and buckled, rather than a ring they sit inside.

## Falling off a float is not the same as swimming

The second case is a four-year-old boy who fell off a flotation toy in a swimming pool. He tried to tread water. He tried to climb out. He managed neither, and he sank.

This is the assumption floaties quietly build in a child: that being in deep water is a thing you do rather than a skill you have. A child who has spent two summers in armbands has been in deep water hundreds of times without ever once having to hold themselves at the surface. Take the plastic away and what is left is a child with no floating position, no route to the wall, and no experience of the second when the water closes over their head.

Falling off a float is not a problem a child can solve by having spent time on floats. What solves it is either a device that keeps the head up by design, or an adult close enough to reach them before the sinking starts. Those are the only two options, and floaties are neither of them.

## The toy in the water is the reason to go in

The third case is a three-year-old boy who fell into an above-ground pool at an apartment building while reaching for a flotation toy floating in it.

An empty pool is boring. A pool with something bobbing in the middle of it is a target, and a three-year-old cannot reason about the depth of water between the edge and the toy. The fix costs nothing at all: when the pool is not in use, nothing floats in it. No rings, no noodles, no inflatables left where a child can see them from the deck. Take them out, and let them dry somewhere out of reach.

## What the report says, and what it does not

The figures come from the Consumer Product Safety Commission's annual report on toy-related deaths and injuries, which named flotation toys, non-motorised scooters and bouncy balls as the products most often involved across the three years from 2022 to 2024.

The report is careful about what that means, and so should you be. A toy being associated with an incident is not the same as the toy being the cause of it, and death certificate reporting runs late, so the most recent years in the data are still incomplete. What the three cases do show is a pattern: in each one, the child was in the water in something that was not a life jacket, or was trying to reach one.

## Why armbands and rings are not life-saving devices

Water wings, inflatable armbands, swimming rings, kickboards and inflatable pool toys are not safety equipment. The American Academy of Pediatrics names inflatable armbands, neck rings and "floaties" directly in its 2026 policy statement on drowning prevention and advises against them, on the grounds that they were not designed to protect a child.

The reasons are mechanical rather than moral.

- They leak slowly and invisibly, so a device that held a child on Tuesday can be noticeably soft by Friday.
- They slip off, particularly when a child raises both arms or twists away from an adult's grip.
- They can flip a child face down and hold them there, which is what happened in the first case above.
- They teach a child that deep water is normal before they can swim, which is the false confidence behind the second case.

## What to look for instead

A life jacket is the only item in this article that is built to keep a child's head above the surface, and the checks on it are short.

- Look for the words "US Coast Guard approved" on the label, and treat that as the entry requirement rather than a bonus feature.
- Size it by weight, not by age and not by height, and re-check the sizing every time the child grows.
- Fasten every strap and buckle, then lift the jacket at the shoulders; it should not ride up over the ears.
- Retire anything torn, faded or missing a buckle, or with a strap that has lost its elasticity. A worn-out jacket is not a spare.

The same applies on any boat, motorised or not: the jacket goes on before the child gets in.

## Portable and inflatable pools count as pools

The fencing advice does not weaken because the pool arrived in a box and took twenty minutes to fill. The American Academy of Pediatrics is explicit that portable and inflatable pools carry drowning risk and should be fenced in the same way as a built-in one.

The specification for a pool fence is specific. It needs four sides, so that the pool is separated from the house as well as from the rest of the yard, and it needs no gap a child can use as a ladder. Height matters, and four feet is the floor rather than the ideal. The gate is the part that fails in real life, so it has to close and latch by itself every single time, with no help from anyone. A [four-sided pool fence with a self-closing, self-latching gate](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=pool+fence+with+self+closing+gate&tag=cnxixi-20) is the single change that moves the numbers most, and a [gate alarm that sounds when the latch opens](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=pool+gate+alarm&tag=cnxixi-20) is worth adding wherever the gate cannot be trusted to stay shut.

Four-sided fencing can reduce toddler drownings by more than half, which is a larger effect than almost any other change a household can make.

## One arm's length, and not one step further

The supervision standard for a child who cannot swim is closer than most parents picture. The academy's 2026 guidance calls for touch supervision, which means an adult within arm's length, for infants, toddlers and weak swimmers whenever they are in or near water.

Arm's length is not "keeping an eye on them". It is close enough to grab a wrist without moving your feet, and it applies at the paddling pool on the patio and in the bath as much as it does at a public pool. The academy describes drowning as quiet and fast: it does not look like the shouting in films, it is silent, and it takes about a minute.

The numbers behind the standard are not small. The CDC records roughly 4,000 fatal drownings a year in the United States along with about 8,000 non-fatal ones, and drowning is the leading cause of injury death for children aged 1 to 4. The highest-risk group is 12 to 36 months: children who are mobile and curious and have no judgement about water at all. Most infants who drown do so in bathtubs and buckets, while preschoolers drown in pools, and in 69 per cent of cases involving children under five, nobody expected the child to be anywhere near the water.

## What to skip

- A seated swim ring, armband, water wing or inflatable used in place of a life jacket.
- Leaving floats, rings or noodles in the pool when it is not in use.
- Assuming a portable or inflatable pool is exempt from fencing because it looks temporary.
- Treating an older child as a substitute for an adult within arm's length.
- Keeping a device in service after a child has grown past its weight limit.

## The bottom line

Three of the six toy-related deaths recorded for 2024 were flotation toy drownings, and none of them needed a negligent adult. They needed an over-weight child in an under-rated ring, a child who had been in deep water but could not stay at the surface, and a toy left floating where a small child could see it and reach for it. The response is small and boring: a life jacket sized by weight and marked US Coast Guard approved, floats out of the pool between sessions, a fence on four sides with a gate that closes itself, and an adult within arm's length of anyone who cannot yet swim.
