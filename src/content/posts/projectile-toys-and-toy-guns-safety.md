A dart blaster gets handed to a four-year-old at a birthday party. The box says eight and up, nobody reads it, and the toy is genuinely good fun, which is exactly the problem. Projectile toys are the category where the age band on the box is doing more safety work than the design is, and where buying the wrong thing for the right child is the most common mistake in the aisle.

This is a buying piece with a narrow thesis. Buy by age band first, and by style second. Style is what children ask for. The age band is what tells you whether the thing will hurt someone before they have the judgement to stop.

## The orange plug is a regulation, not a decoration

Toy guns that look like real firearms are marked for a reason, and the rule is specific. The federal marking requirement for toy, look-alike and imitation firearms now sits at 16 CFR Part 1272, which is where the provision moved in 88 FR 30228.

Two details are worth knowing because they are checkable in a shop in about five seconds.

- The muzzle must carry a permanently fixed, solid blaze orange plug, in the specified colour, and it has to be fixed in place rather than merely fitted.
- That plug must be recessed no more than 6 millimetres from the muzzle end, which means it should be plainly visible at the front of the barrel rather than buried inside it.

Put those together and you get a decent test. If the orange is missing, painted over, or comes off in your hand, you are not looking at a decorated toy, you are looking at a product that does not meet the marking requirement. That matters most with secondhand items and no-name marketplace listings, where the plug is the first thing to disappear.

An orange tip is a marking rule, not a safety feature. It tells you the product was designed to be read as a toy at a glance. It does nothing about what happens when something leaves the barrel.

## What the toy standard asks of the projectile itself

The performance side is handled by the toy safety standard, and the Consumer Product Safety Commission summarises it plainly: certain toys that can fire projectiles must meet certain performance requirements, such as ensuring the projectiles are not small parts or that they cannot exert excessive force when impacting a surface.

Read that as two separate promises. The projectile has to be too big to be a choking hazard, because a projectile is a small object that children put in their mouths, and it has to be soft or light enough that hitting a face at close range is survivable rather than injuring. Both of those are design properties, and both are easy to break.

They get broken in predictable ways. A blaster whose spring has been replaced or whose listing advertises more power is no longer the product that was tested. A cheap refill pack of hard darts used in a blaster that came with soft ones undoes the whole arrangement. So the honest buying rule is not only about the blaster. It is about the darts that go in it, now and in six months when the originals are lost behind the sofa.

## The eye injury numbers, and why they are quoted in two different ways

This is where the category earns its age bands.

- In 2012, about 3,160 children were treated in United States emergency departments for eye injuries caused by non-powder firearms, which is the BB gun, paintball, airsoft and air gun family, according to a study published in the Journal of AAPOS in 2015.
- Between 2003 and 2022, there were 131,094 toy-related eye injuries, an average of roughly 6,550 a year, and projectile toy guns accounted for 9.2 percent of them, per a 2026 analysis in the same journal based on national injury data.

Those two figures measure different things and should not be added together. The first counts a group of products that are not toys at all. The second counts toys. What they share is the tissue. An eye has almost no capacity to absorb a mistake, and a dart that a child would shrug off on an arm is a different event a few centimetres higher.

That is the argument for taking the label seriously rather than treating it as a suggestion, and it is also the argument for eye protection wherever the projectile moves faster than a foam dart.

## The age bands, stated plainly

The official age guidance for this category has two lines in it, and both are worth repeating without softening.

- Arrows and darts are not for children under five. The Consumer Product Safety Commission's own wording is that if you are buying arrows or darts for children, not under five years, you should make sure they have soft tips, rubber suction cups or other protective tips. In other words, the protected tip is not an upgrade, it is the baseline for the age group that is allowed to use them.
- For anything under eight, the toy must not have dangerous sharp edges or sharp points. A blaster with a hard moulded fin, a metal barrel end, or a rigid stem on the arrow fails that test before anyone has fired it.

Read together, the bands mean something practical. Under five, this category is essentially closed, and the substitute is throwing games that cannot hurt anyone. From five, soft tips and suction cups are the entry ticket. From eight, you are still choosing the gentler end of the range, and still watching where the barrel points.

## Airsoft, BB guns and paintball markers are not toys

These get shelved near toys and get bought as gifts, and they are a separate product family with a separate regulatory story. They are not children's products, they do not carry the same age labelling, and eye protection is part of using them rather than an accessory for them.

The practical consequence for a household with mixed ages is storage. Non-powder firearms and their ammunition belong in a place a child cannot reach, separate from the toy bin, and that separation is the point of having a category distinction at all.

## What we would not buy

- A blaster with no manufacturer name and no age statement, whatever the listing photo shows.
- Anything whose muzzle marking is missing, retouched or removable, since the marking is what the rule is about.
- Hard plastic-tipped darts, metal-tipped arrows, and rigid-shaft projectiles for a child under five.
- Refill dart packs chosen on quantity alone, which is how a soft blaster turns into a hard one.
- Any blaster sold with an advertised power upgrade, a modified spring, or a claim about higher muzzle velocity.
- Secondhand projectile toys with no way to check the tip condition and the barrel markings.

## Buying choices that reduce the risk

Most of the value here is in the accessories, which is where the money and the risk usually are.

Buying outside the age band means the matching gear does not exist, so the honest option is a different toy. ## Range rules that cost nothing

The rules that prevent most of the injuries here are free and take one conversation.

- Nobody is a target, including pets, and that rule is set before the box is opened rather than after the first hit.
- Darts are checked from time to time: a soft tip that has hardened, cracked or gone missing is a hard tip. Bin it.
- Eye protection goes on for everyone in the room, not only the child firing.
- Darts and blasters live together and out of reach, so the ammunition does not end up as a loose small object on the floor next to a younger sibling.
- The age on the label is set by the youngest person in the house, not the child holding the toy.

## The bottom line

Buy the age band, then the style. The marking rule for toy guns sits at 16 CFR Part 1272, and the check takes seconds: a permanent blaze orange plug, recessed no more than 6 millimetres from the muzzle end, is the minimum a look-alike toy should show you. Projectiles themselves have to be non-small-parts and unable to hit with excessive force, which is why home-modified blasters and cheap hard-tipped refills undo the design.

Arrows and darts are not for children under five; from five, soft tips and rubber suction cups are the baseline; and under eight, a toy must not have dangerous sharp edges or sharp points. Different numbers, same conclusion: the age band is the feature that matters.
