Most of a backyard budget goes into falling. You buy the loose fill, you measure the depth, you check the clearance under the swing set, and then you buy a sandbox, the one piece of playground equipment in the yard that the Consumer Product Safety Commission says needs no protective surfacing at all.

That splits the purchase in two. You are buying a material to dig in, not a soft landing, and the sand is the part of the project with the fewest rules behind it.

## The sandbox is the one thing that needs no safety surface

The line comes from the Consumer Product Safety Commission's Outdoor Home Playground Safety Handbook, publication 324: "Ground level equipment—such as a sandbox, activity wall, playhouse or other equipment that has no elevated play surface—does not need any protective surfacing."

That is practical rather than generous, because sand is a weak cushion. Loose-fill surfacing is specified at 9 inches deep, and at that depth sand only protects a fall from 5 feet; wood chips or shredded wood at the same depth covers a fall from 10 feet. Sand is roughly half the protection of the cheap alternative, which is why nobody sensible counts a sandbox as fall protection.

Spend the surfacing budget on the climbing frame and treat the sandbox as what it is: a digging pit. If you are planning one for next spring, settle this part now, because the decision you will agonise over is not the box. It is the bags.

## The bag of sand is where the rules run out

Here is the part almost nobody knows. In 1986 there were concerns about tremolite, a fibrous mineral found in some crushed limestone and crushed marble, turning up in commercial play sand, and about whether long-term exposure carried the same risk as asbestos. That produced a petition asking the CPSC to ban the sale of play sand containing significant tremolite. The CPSC denied it, and today the agency has no standard and no labelling requirement covering the source or composition of sand.

Read that in the right direction. It does not mean every bag is fine, and it does not mean sand is dangerous. It means the label will not answer your question, so you have to.

What is available instead is the American Academy of Pediatrics' advice for parents. Buy natural river sand or beach sand. Avoid products made from crushed limestone, crushed marble or crushed crystalline silica, which is also called quartz, and avoid anything else that is obviously dusty.

Natural river and beach sand is rounded grain that pours and settles. The crushed products are angular, and their fine fraction is dust, which is why a dry bag throws a cloud when you tip it into the box. If a bag dusts up on a dry afternoon, it is not what the pediatric guidance points you toward.

If you want a definite answer, you can send a sample to a laboratory to test for tremolite or crystalline silica, and the EPA's state or regional Asbestos Coordinators can point you toward a reliable lab. For most families, [bags of natural play sand](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=natural+play+sand&tag=cnxixi-20) sold clearly as river or beach sand are the simpler route.

## The lid is the cheapest piece of equipment you will buy

If you take one thing from this article and ignore the rest, take the lid.

The pediatric guidance is blunt: cover the box when it is not in use, and let the sand dry completely before you cover it, because wet sand can grow bacteria. That second half is the part people miss. A lid laid over damp sand seals in the moisture that causes the problem, so the routine is rake, dry, cover.

A fitted lid also does more than a thrown-over sheet: it keeps rain and leaves out, and it survives a windy week. If you are buying the box new, [a sandbox with a fitted lid](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=covered+sandbox+with+lid&tag=cnxixi-20) solves the storage problem and the covering problem in one purchase.

## What dogs and cats leave behind

A sandbox is an outdoor floor, and outdoor floors collect what animals leave, which is why sandboxes come up in the parasite literature.

Toxocariasis is caused by a Toxocara parasite, the dog roundworm and cat roundworm, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention describes the route plainly: eggs are passed in the feces of infected animals and can enter a person's mouth through contaminated soil or unwashed hands. A review published in 2009 in PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases put the vulnerable group in one sentence: "Children are particularly prone to infection because they are exposed to the eggs on sandboxes and playgrounds contaminated with dog and cat feces."

The CDC is equally direct about who is hit hardest: "Severe cases are more likely to be in young children who have been playing in or eating contaminated dirt." In its severe form the parasite can reach the eye, causing inflammation and retinal damage that usually affects only one eye, or travel to the liver or the central nervous system.

The reason to know that is not to abandon the sandbox. The CDC's prevention list is short and almost entirely in your hands: have a veterinarian check and deworm your pets, especially puppies and kittens and animals that spend time outdoors; wash hands with soap and water after playing with animals, after time outside and before handling food; pick up pet feces every time, bury or bag it, then wash your hands; and teach children that eating dirt or mud is dangerous, because that is the behaviour the parasite is waiting for. Many infected people never have symptoms, which is another reason the response here is hygiene rather than alarm.

## Raccoons, and why prompt cleanup is the whole story

Raccoon roundworm, Baylisascaris procyonis, is the harder parasite and the rarer one. The CDC notes that most infections are in children and others more likely to put dirt or animal waste in their mouth by mistake, and that human infection is rare but can be serious, because the larvae can invade the eye, the organs or the brain.

You need one fact, and the CDC provides it: the eggs are not infectious when they are passed. They need two to four weeks in the environment to become infective.

Feces removed promptly, before the eggs have had time to mature, never becomes infective, so the litter tray habit, the rake and the bin, is the response. If raccoons have made a latrine of a corner of the yard, the CDC's instruction is to remove the contaminated material carefully and burn it, bury it or send it to a landfill, keeping your hands and clothes clean; hard surfaces such as a deck or patio can be treated with boiling water or a propane torch used carefully. Do not keep, feed or adopt a wild raccoon, and wash your hands after outdoor work or play. If you think a child has eaten raccoon feces, treat it as a same-day medical question rather than a wait-and-see.

## The frame, and where you put it

The pediatric guidance is clear that cheap railroad ties are the wrong frame. They splinter, and they may be soaked with creosote, a carcinogen. Use non-toxic landscape wood or a non-wood container instead. The CPSC's handbook agrees: do not use creosote-treated wood or wood with pesticide coatings, and CCA pressure-treated wood, which contains arsenic, has not been produced for residential use since 31 December 2003, so an old frame of unknown provenance is the one to replace.

Placement is the part people skip. The CPSC's rule is to separate active and quiet activities, which means keeping the sandbox out of the swing's range of motion, or putting a barrier between them. A sandbox in the arc of a swing ends up full of feet, and the swing ends up full of sand.

If you do not have a yard, the same rules shrink rather than disappear, and [a sand table for a balcony or a small yard](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=sand+table+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) keeps the sand contained, easier to dry out and simple to cover.

## What we would not buy

- A frame built from railroad ties, or any old timber of unknown treatment.
- Bags sold as crushed limestone, crushed marble or crushed crystalline silica, also called quartz.
- Builder's sand, paver sand and sandblasting sand, which are graded for jobs rather than for children.
- Any bag that throws a visible cloud of dust on a dry day.
- A sandbox with no lid, or a lid that does not fit properly.
- A box sited inside the swing's arc.

## The bottom line

Sand is the one playground material the CPSC says needs no protective surfacing at all, which frees you to buy it for digging and to spend your fall-protection budget on the climbing frame. The real decision is the sand itself, and it is yours to make, because the CPSC has no standard and no labelling requirement for its source or composition. The pediatric advice fills that gap: natural river or beach sand, and avoid crushed limestone, crushed marble and crushed crystalline silica, along with anything obviously dusty.

Then the maintenance list, which is short: fit a lid that works, let the sand dry before you cover it, rake it regularly, and clear animal waste before the eggs can mature, because raccoon roundworm eggs need two to four weeks to become infective. Wash hands after outdoor play, keep the frame non-toxic and keep the box out of the swing's reach. Ten minutes of choosing and a two-minute habit afterwards is the whole article.
