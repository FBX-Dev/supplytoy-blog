A scooter is the first thing a child owns that goes faster than they can run, and it is often bought on the way home from school in about four minutes. That is roughly three minutes less than the decision deserves.

CPSC's most recent toy injury data puts non-motorised scooters as the single largest identified product category, at 23% of estimated toy-related injuries in 2023, about 53,000 injuries, and more than one in five toy-related injuries among children under fifteen. That is a striking concentration for one product, and it is worth understanding before the money is spent.

## The category that leads the numbers

The wrong reading of that figure is that scooters should be avoided. The better reading is that this is the one toy where details you would otherwise ignore decide how often a child goes down.

Of toy-related emergency visits in 2023, 45% involved the head and face and 21% were lacerations, and most were treated and released. Head and face is exactly the profile you would expect from a wheeled toy with a low deck and a hard surface.

The deaths happen somewhere less expected. Five of the deaths reported in the 2022 to 2023 period involving non-motorised scooters were collisions with motor vehicles. The driveway is not the hazard. The road at the end of it is.

## The brake, and where it sits

If you check one thing, check the brake, with your hand rather than by reading the description.

A rear fender brake, pressed down with a foot on the back of the deck, is the simplest and most reliable system for a young rider. It fails gradually rather than suddenly and does not depend on hand strength. The drawback is that it needs the child to shift weight backwards, which is a skill in itself.

A handlebar hand brake is faster but depends on grip strength and on remembering which lever does what, and on a cheap scooter the lever is often stiff and the cable stretches within a month. If you buy one, test the lever and make sure it returns cleanly.

What you do not want is a scooter whose only slow-down mechanism is a plastic block rubbing the rear wheel. That is a wear item, not a brake, and it is the first thing to disappear.

## Wheel diameter, and the surface it will meet

Wheel size is described as a styling choice. It is really a statement about the ground.

Small hard wheels are light and fast on a smooth surface and miserable everywhere else. A larger wheel in a softer polyurethane rolls over the cracks and paving joints that a small hard wheel catches, which is the difference between a child riding for an hour and a child stopping every few metres. Bigger wheels are heavier, so there is a genuine trade-off, but on anything other than a perfectly smooth skate park the larger wheel wins.

Deck height matters just as much. A deck that sits too high is hard to push and hard to step off, and stepping off is the manoeuvre a child actually uses to avoid a fall.

Two-wheeled scooters lean into turns and need more balance, so they suit a child who is already confident on a bike. A three-wheeled scooter with two wheels at the front is stable and self-righting, and [three-wheel scooters for younger riders](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=three+wheel+scooters+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) remain the right first scooter for most children. Once a child rides a bicycle comfortably, [two-wheel scooters for older kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=two+wheel+scooters+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) are the natural upgrade, and wheel size matters more than frame material at that point.

## The handlebar lock nobody checks

This is the detail behind the falls parents never see coming. A folding scooter usually has a hinge in the middle of the steering column, and on a cheap scooter that joint loosens with use. When it lets go, the handlebars rotate independently of the deck while the child is still holding a bar that no longer steers.

Before buying, grip the handlebar and try to twist it against the front wheel. There should be no play at all. The folding mechanism should need a deliberate action, ideally two, and should lock with an audible click rather than friction alone. If a child can fold the scooter while standing on it, the joint is too easy to release.

The stem height adjuster fails in the same way. A telescoping column held by a single quick-release lever is a common weak point, and a stem that drops mid-ride puts the child straight over the front.

## The helmet question, answered

There is no separate United States helmet standard for scooters or tricycles, and CPSC's position is that a bicycle helmet is the one to use. That means the CPSC 16 CFR Part 1203 mark.

Skateboard helmets are a different product. ASTM F1492 is the multiple-impact standard for skate and trick-roller helmets, and there is no federal standard for skateboard helmets at all in the United States, so a helmet sold as a skate helmet may carry no certification. One showing only CPSC 1203 is a bicycle helmet even if it looks like a skate helmet, and the sticker inside the shell is the only way to tell.

Two rules are absolute. Replace any helmet after a crash even if it looks untouched, because crushed foam does not recover and the damage is generally invisible, and never use a second-hand helmet of unknown history. A correctly fitted helmet sits about two fingers above the eyebrows, with one finger between the chin and the strap, and should not move when the child shakes their head with the buckle undone. If you are buying your first, look for the CPSC mark rather than the styling, which is what separates [kids bike helmets](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+bike+helmet&tag=cnxixi-20) built to the standard from the ones that only look the part.

A helmet also comes off before playground equipment or a tree. The first United States death from a bicycle helmet catching on playground equipment was recorded in 1999, because a helmet strap fits neatly into the gap between a platform and a rail.

## Motorised ride-ons, and the reason to wait

Motorised ride-on toys look like the natural next step, and for many families they are a step taken too early.

The American Academy of Pediatrics advises that children under sixteen should not ride or operate motorised e-scooters, a much higher age than most parents assume. The death data points the same way for smaller powered toys: electric ride-on toys appear among the toy types linked to single fatalities in the 2022 to 2024 reporting period, and the ten toy-related deaths recorded in 2023 ranged in age from seven months to six years.

There is also a practical argument. A motorised ride-on removes the effort that made the toy worth having, usually at a size the child outgrows within two years.

## Ride-on toys are a different purchase

A ride-on with a seat and four wheels is not a scooter with a seat bolted on. The two checks that matter are the wheelbase and the seat height.

A short wheelbase tips backwards when a child pushes hard on the pedals, so look at the distance between the axles rather than the overall length. Seat height should let the child reach the pedals with a slight bend in the knee, and since most of these toys have no adjustment, sizing is the whole decision. Pedal-driven ride-ons are also bulky and awkward to carry, so measure the doorway before buying anything that has to come back indoors every evening. If you are comparing options, [ride-on toys for kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=ride+on+toys+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) differ enormously in stability once the wheels and the wheelbase are taken into account.

## Battery compartments, and the two-hour rule

Anything motorised brings button and coin cell batteries into the house, and this is the one hazard here with a hard clock on it. A swallowed button or coin cell can cause internal chemical burns in as little as two hours.

Under 16 CFR part 1263, which references ANSI/UL 4200A-2023, a battery compartment must open either with a tool or with at least two independent and simultaneous hand actions. Check that on the toy itself rather than trusting the packaging. If a child may have swallowed a battery, call the national poison help line at 1-800-222-1222 rather than waiting for symptoms.

## What we would not buy

- Scooters whose only braking is a plastic block rubbing the rear wheel.
- Folding scooters with any twist in the handlebar or any looseness in the stem clamp.
- Scooters bought for a child who has outgrown the deck height, which is the most common reason a scooter is abandoned rather than ridden.
- Motorised ride-ons for a child well below the age on the box.
- A skate-style helmet without an ASTM F1492 sticker, if the child is actually going to skate.
- Any second-hand scooter without a clear history, because the failure points here are the ones you cannot see.

## The bottom line

Buy the brake first, then the wheels, then the deck height, and test the stem joint with your hands before you pay. Add a bicycle helmet carrying the CPSC 1203 mark, wear it every time, and take it off at the playground gate.

A scooter is a genuinely good toy. It is just one where the details you cannot see in a photograph decide whether the child rides it for four years or stops by March.
