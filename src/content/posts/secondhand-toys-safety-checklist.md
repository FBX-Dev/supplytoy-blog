A friend drops off a bin of toys. Some are five years old, some are from a house where nobody kept the small pieces, and one still has a battery cover held on with packing tape. The question is not whether a used toy is acceptable. Plenty of used toys are better than new ones. The question is what changed since the toy was manufactured, because that is where the risk quietly accumulates.

Second-hand is a sensible way to own toys. It is also the category where a quick inspection earns the most.

## The mindset that actually works

Two failure modes are common here. The first is reflexive refusal — treating anything second-hand as suspect, which throws away good toys and teaches children nothing useful. The second is reflexive acceptance, where a bin arrives and every item goes straight into the playroom.

The workable middle is this: assume the toy is fine, then spend ten minutes trying to prove otherwise. You are not auditing the manufacturer. You are looking for the specific things that degrade with use, and the specific things that were legal when the toy was made but are not now.

There are really only five categories worth checking. Everything below is one of them.

## Check for recalls first

Recall information is public, and a recall check is the single highest-value minute you will spend. It catches the case where a toy was manufactured defectively and pulled from the market after you, or the original owner, bought it.

Look the toy up before you hand it over. The **CPSC** publishes recall notices and can be reached at **800-638-2772**, and product injuries can be reported at **SaferProducts.gov**. If a toy is under an active recall, it does not get handed down. Retrieving a recalled toy and manufacturing a fix at home is not a repair, it is a new product with no testing behind it.

Recall checks are especially worth doing for toys that had any reputation for breaking, for anything with small magnets or batteries, and for older plastic toys with a history of cracking.

## Check completeness and hardware

This is where most real incidents live, because almost every serious toy injury involves a toy that came apart rather than a toy that was defective on the shelf.

- **Put every fastener back where it belongs.** Missing screws on battery covers, wheel hubs, and handle mounts are the most common defect in a used bin.
- **Flex the plastic.** A hairline crack in a shell is the beginning of both a sharp edge and an interior part coming loose.
- **Pull on removable parts.** Wheels, eyes, caps, lids, and handles. Anything that comes off in your hand is a part a child can get off.
- **Check the seams on soft toys.** An opening stitch line means an internal filling or component is becoming external.
- **Look for missing pieces.** An incomplete toy is often a toy with a small piece somewhere in the box, and that piece is the hazard.

If a toy fails this pass, the answer is to retire it, not to repair it. A repaired toy reliably fails again, and the second failure is usually harder to see.

## Check whether it predates current standards

This is the part people miss. A toy can be in perfect condition and still not meet what is required today.

The current mandatory toy safety standard is **ASTM F963-23**, which the CPSC finalized on **January 18, 2024**, and which **took effect on April 20, 2024**, replacing **F963-17**. It is incorporated as a mandatory standard in **16 CFR part 1250** under the CPSIA. A toy made before that change was tested against an earlier edition.

What that means in practice, for a used toy:

- **Paint lead limit is 90 ppm.** Very old painted wooden and metal toys can predate this, and old paint that is lifting or flaking should end the conversation.
- **Eight soluble heavy metals are controlled**, and phthalates are restricted. Old soft plastics are the place to be careful.
- **Small-parts requirements apply to toys for children under 36 months.** A toy marked for older children is not a toy for a toddler.
- **Sharp edges and points are governed, and battery accessibility is covered in section 4.25.** Worn edges and loose compartments drift out of compliance with use, even on a compliant toy.
- **CPSIA also requires a tracking label, a Children's Product Certificate, and testing by a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory.** A toy with no traceable labeling was never certified, whatever its condition.

If a toy has no age marking at all, it has not been assessed. That does not make it unsafe, but it means the judgement is yours.

## Check the two hazards that are always urgent

Two categories deserve their own pass, because they are the ones where a ten-minute inspection genuinely prevents an emergency.

**Batteries.** Button and coin battery compartments must be secured by a tool or at least two simultaneous hand actions. On a used toy, the screw is often simply gone. If the cover lifts, the toy is finished. Do not tape it.

**Magnets.** Any toy with a magnet that can come loose gets retired immediately, not repaired. A swallowed magnet is dangerous mainly when there is more than one, because they can attract across intestinal walls. If a magnetic toy rattles, the interior magnet has already broken free.

If either of these has been swallowed, do not wait for symptoms — seek medical help and call **Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222**.

## Check cords, strings, and attachments

Corded and strung toys deserve a dedicated look on anything second-hand, because the cord is exactly the part that wears and exactly the part nobody inspects. Ribbons, drawstrings, elastic, pull cords, pacifier clips, and anything that can form a loop around a neck should be evaluated with the same seriousness as a small part. Short is safer than long, fixed is safer than dangling, and nothing with a cord belongs in a crib or playpen with a child unattended.

## Clean it properly, and look while you clean

The cleaning step doubles as the best inspection you will do.

Wash hard plastic and wood with soap and water, then air dry fully. For plush, use a [mesh laundry bag](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=mesh+laundry+bag+for+washing+stuffed+toys&tag=cnxixi-20) so small eyes, noses, and trim are not lost in the machine, and inspect the seams afterwards, because a wash is a stress test. If a soft toy comes out of the wash with an opening seam, it is done.

For anything you cannot wash, wipe it and look for mould, mildew, or a musty smell, particularly on bath toys and anything with a hole that holds water. Mould is not a safety recall, but it is a reason to say no.

## The ten-minute pass

- Pull every removable part and put every fastener back.
- Flex the shell, looking for cracks.
- Test the battery cover and shake the toy.
- Check for any loose or rattling magnet.
- Look for cords that can form a loop.
- Search the toy for recalls.
- Wash it and reassess the seams.

For a mixed-age household, a [small parts tester](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=small+parts+tester+choking+hazard&tag=cnxixi-20) removes the guesswork, and a set of [toy storage bins](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=toy+storage+bins+with+lids&tag=cnxixi-20) with lids is how the older child's small-part toys stay physically separate from the younger child's reach. Physical separation works where verbal rules do not.

## What we would not accept

- **Any toy with a missing battery-cover screw**, unless a correct replacement fastener is available and fits properly.
- **Any magnetic toy that rattles.**
- **Old painted wooden or metal toys with lifting, chalking, or flaking paint.**
- **Anything under an active recall**, in any condition.
- **Toys with a long cord or a loop** intended for a cot or playpen.
- **Toys with no age marking at all, offered for a child under three.**

## The bottom line

Hand-me-downs are worth accepting. The trick is to accept them conditionally, after a short inspection aimed at five things: recalls, hardware and completeness, the standards the toy was built to, batteries and magnets, and cords.

Most of the risk in a used toy bin is visible once you know what you are looking for. Pull on the parts, check the screws, shake it for a loose magnet, search the recall database, and wash it. Anything that fails gets retired rather than repaired — and if something has already been swallowed, seek medical help rather than waiting to see.
