There is a box in the storage room that nobody wants to deal with. Some of it is outgrown, some of it is broken in ways you cannot quite name, and something at the bottom has had a missing battery cover for two years. The instinct is to split it into two piles, sell and donate, and that instinct is right. What is not obvious is that the two piles obey different rulebooks, and mixing them up is how people either lose a deduction or pass on something that should never have left the house.

## Selling and donating are not the same job

When you sell a toy, you are a seller, and consumer product law treats you like one. When you give a toy to a charity, you are a donor, and what governs you is tax law. The obligations barely overlap.

The selling side is where the hard rules live, because it is the side where money changes hands. The donating side is softer in one sense and stricter in another. Nobody will fine you for donating a wobbly play yard, but if you want a deduction, the condition of what you give away has become a financial question.

So sort first, then decide which rulebook applies to the pile in front of you. Both take about ten minutes a box once you know what you are looking for.

## The one hard line on the selling side

Federal law prohibits the sale of a recalled product. The Consumer Product Safety Act makes it unlawful for any person to sell, offer for sale, manufacture for sale, distribute in commerce, or import any consumer product that is subject to voluntary corrective action taken by the manufacturer in consultation with the Commission, or subject to an order issued under section 12 or 15 of the Act. That is Section 19(a)(2)(B)-(C), at 15 U.S.C. 2068.

Two details matter more than the wording. It covers both a manufacturer's voluntary recall and a recall ordered by the CPSC, so "the company chose to recall it" is not a loophole. And the definition of manufacturer includes importers, while the provision reaches anyone who distributes a recalled product in commerce. The CPSC states that its laws apply to anyone who sells or distributes consumer products, which includes thrift stores, consignment stores, charities, and people running a yard sale.

The penalty provision, Section 20 at 15 U.S.C. 2069, sets a maximum of 100,000 dollars per violation and a cap of 15,150,000 dollars for a related series of violations. Each listing, each sale, and each distribution can count separately. A bin of recalled toys offered one at a time is not one mistake.

Which is why the first step on the selling side is not cleaning and not pricing. It is a recall search, and it takes two minutes.

## No testing required does not mean anything goes

Here is the sentence that gets misread. The CPSC's guide for resellers says plainly that resellers are not required to test products or meet registration card requirements, then adds that resellers should seek reasonable assurance that the products comply with the applicable durable infant or toddler product safety standards.

Read both halves. You do not have to send anything to a lab. You do have to have a reasonable basis for believing the thing is legal to sell, and that is a separate obligation from testing.

For the doubtful case the CPSC's instruction is four words: "When in doubt, throw it out!" The same guidance names the durable infant products that should not be passed on at all, including play yards, infant walkers, bath seats, and bed rails for toddlers, along with anything used in a nursery that is broken, wobbly, or missing parts, even if it was never recalled.

## Cribs have a date, not just a condition

A crib is the clearest case in the category, and it is not about wear. Since June 28, 2011, cribs sold, offered for sale, contracted for sale, resold, manufactured, or used in commerce in the United States must meet the current federal standards, at 16 CFR part 1219 for full-size cribs and 16 CFR part 1220 for non-full-size cribs.

For older cribs the CPSC's business guidance is direct. Retailers should destroy any full-size crib, with or without drop sides, manufactured before June 28, 2011, and should not resell it unless the seller has written proof that the crib meets the applicable requirements.

Written proof is the whole test. A crib that looks solid and spent a decade in a dry attic is still on the wrong side of that line without paperwork, and a clean surface does not substitute for it.

## Four checks before anything goes up for sale

Once the recall search comes back clean, the rest of the CPSC checklist is mechanical.

- Search the product in the recall database at cpsc.gov/recalls, and remember that selling, offering for sale, or distributing a recalled product is unlawful.
- Confirm that the manufacturer's name and contact information, the model name and number, and the manufacture date are permanently marked on the product itself, not only on a sticker that has since fallen off.
- Check that warning labels are present, complete, and legible.
- Print the assembly instructions for that model and pass them along, which is what the CPSC recommends when the original is missing.

A toy that fails the second or third check is not automatically dangerous. It is a toy whose compliance you cannot demonstrate, which usually decides the answer.

## The donating side runs on tax rules

Donating is not selling, and the rulebook changes completely. What you are working with here is IRS Publication 526, IRS Topic No. 506, and the instructions to Form 8283.

The first test is condition. Generally, you cannot claim a deduction for clothing or household items you donate unless they are in good used condition or better. Toys count as household items here. A box of toys with missing parts and cracked shells is not a deduction, it is a disposal problem wearing a receipt.

Then the paperwork thresholds.

- A noncash deduction of more than 500 dollars requires Form 8283, with Section A for amounts above 500 and up to 5,000.
- A single item, or a group of similar items, worth more than 5,000 dollars requires a qualified appraisal and Section B of Form 8283.
- A donation of 250 dollars or more requires a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the charity, describing the property and stating whether you received any goods or services in return.

Two more rules close the gaps. Gifts to individuals are not deductible, so handing the box to a neighbour's child is generous but not a deduction. And the value you report is fair market value, which the Form 8283 instructions define as the price a willing, knowledgeable buyer would pay a willing, knowledgeable seller when neither has to buy or sell.

That definition is the practical warning. Second-hand toys trade at very low prices, so writing down what the toy originally cost usually overstates what it is worth. Value it as a used toy, because that is what it is.

## What we would not pass on

- Anything under an active recall, in any condition, at any price.
- Any crib manufactured before June 28, 2011 without written proof of compliance.
- Broken, wobbly, or incomplete nursery gear, including play yards, infant walkers, bath seats, and bed rails for toddlers.
- A toy with a battery compartment that no longer closes securely, or a magnetic toy that rattles.

None of these need repairing. Repairing a toy that failed a safety check just moves the failure to a child who never saw the first one.

## Tools that make a box go faster

The physical work is not complicated, but a few ordinary things make it far less annoying.

Sort into [clear storage bins with lids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=clear+storage+bins+with+lids&tag=cnxixi-20) rather than one pile, because a closed bin is the difference between a decision made today and the same box still sitting there next year. Label each one with [removable labels](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=removable+labels+for+storage+bins&tag=cnxixi-20) so the category on the outside matches what you actually sorted. Wipe hard plastic and wood down before anything is packed, and keep [packing tape and a dispenser](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=packing+tape+dispenser&tag=cnxixi-20) with the boxes so a sealed donation box stays sealed.

The point is not neatness. A box that is already sorted and labelled is a box that gets dealt with, rather than re-decided three times.

## The bottom line

Run a recall search before anything goes up for sale, and treat it as a fixed step rather than a good intention, because that is the one line federal law draws without much room for judgement. Read the crib date and the condition of the nursery gear before deciding that something is too good to throw away.

On the donation side, classify first and value honestly. Only good used condition or better counts, the paperwork thresholds start at 500 dollars and tighten at 5,000 dollars, and the number you write down is fair market value rather than what you once paid.

For the pile that is neither sellable nor deductible, the honest answer is the least glamorous one. Take it apart so it cannot be mistaken for a working toy, and dispose of it according to your local rules, rather than leaving it intact at the kerb for someone to pick up.
