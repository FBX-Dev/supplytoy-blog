Day three is usually worse than day one. By then your child knows exactly what happens when you reach the cubby, and the arms go around your leg and stay there.

The crying is not the problem. The AAP calls separation anxiety "an entirely normal behavior and a beautiful sign of a meaningful attachment." What you control is not whether your child cries, but how you leave.

## What separation anxiety actually is

The AAP opens its page on this with a line worth keeping: "The trick for surviving separation anxiety demands preparation, brisk transitions, and the evolution of time." None of those is a purchase. Preparation means the child knows the shape of the morning before it arrives. Brisk transitions means the goodbye is short. The evolution of time is the part you cannot hurry.

It is also not a steady state: "Separations are more difficult when children are hungry, tired or sick." A bad drop-off after a short night is evidence about the night, not about your child.

## The age lines, and why they matter more than anything you buy

The timing is not random, and knowing the schedule saves a lot of self-blame.

- Babies. It appears once object permanence does: "Although some babies display object permanence and separation anxiety as early as 4 to 5 months of age, most develop more robust separation anxiety at around 9 months."
- Toddlers. "Many toddlers skip separation anxiety in infancy and start demonstrating challenges at 15 or 18 months of age."
- Preschoolers. "By the time children are 3 years of age, most clearly understand the effect their anxiety or pleas at separation have on us." The AAP adds: "It doesn't mean they aren't stressed."

A child who sailed through infant care and then fell apart at eighteen months has not regressed. The behaviour arrived on its own timetable.

## Your child's age is the biggest variable in when to start

If you have any flexibility about the start date, the AAP's child care guidance is unusually concrete.

- Birth to 7 months: "young infants generally will transition to a consistent child care worker in almost any setting."
- 7 to 12 months: "Stranger anxiety normally occurs at this time." The advice is direct: "If possible, do not start child care during this period, or just ease into it."
- 12 to 24 months: "This is when separation anxiety peaks and your child has the most difficulty with your leaving."

Most families do not get to choose, because work starts when it starts. But even a few weeks can move a start out of the hardest window.

## The goodbye is a routine, not a feeling

Six things help, and they all point the same direction.

1. Create quick goodbye rituals. "keep the goodbye short and sweet. If you linger, the transition time does too. So will the anxiety."
2. Be consistent. Same time, same ritual, same way of leaving.
3. Give your full attention while you are there, then leave cleanly.
4. Keep your promise.
5. Be specific, child style: "I'll be back after nap time and before afternoon snack." For a trip, "I'll be home after 3 sleeps."
6. Practice being apart, with a grandparent, a playdate, or a friend for an hour.

A goodbye that is short and identical every day does more work than one that runs long and changes each time.

## The hardest rule is the one about coming back

The most useful line in the AAP's child care guidance is this: "Once you have left, do not reappear unless you are prepared to stay or walk with your child to the door."

A parent who slips back later to check often finds a child who had settled and is now upset again, and the next morning restarts from the beginning. The child learns that crying long enough brings you back, which is a fair conclusion given the evidence you just supplied.

The fix is not willpower but scheduling. If you cannot stay away, hand the drop-off to someone else for the first stretch.

## What the milestone actually measures at drop-off

Parents grade a drop-off by how loud it was. The CDC measures something else. Among the three-year milestones: "Calms down within 10 minutes after you leave her, like at a childcare drop off."

The official wording is not "does not cry." It is "calms down." The milestone is about recovery, not silence, and a child who is upset at the door and then joins the table is meeting it.

The definition behind the list matters too. "CDC's milestone checklists include milestones most children (75% or more) are expected to reach by a given age." Most, not all. A milestone describes what is typical, not a standard your child has to hit on a particular morning.

## When to ask someone rather than wait

Some of what looks like separation anxiety is separation anxiety; some of it is something else, and the CDC's early identification work exists to make that distinction easier.

Two numbers are worth holding onto: "1 in 6 children (3-17 years of age) have a developmental disability," and "1 in 31 (8-year-olds) are estimated to have autism." Asking the question is ordinary, not alarming.

Monitoring and screening are two different things. "Developmental monitoring complements screening to improve early identification." Monitoring is what you and the teachers do, continuously, using the milestone lists; screening is the formal step, done with validated tools by a professional. The AAP recommends screening at 9 months, 18 months, and 24 or 30 months, with autism screening at 18 and 24 months. If support is needed, IDEA Part C covers 0 to 3 and Part B covers 3 to 22, and each state's programme can be found through cdc.gov/FindEI. The CDC's three steps are to "Talk with the child's healthcare provider," "Ask about developmental screening," and "Consider seeking support through early intervention or public schools." And the line that closes the loop: "You know your child best. Don't wait."

## What to ask the daycare before you enroll

Two sets of numbers are worth knowing before you tour, and one of them is routinely mistaken for law.

The AAP and APHA national standards, published as Caring for Our Children, recommend 1:3 with a class of no more than 6 children for the under-12-month room; 1:4 with a maximum of 8 for 13 to 35 months; 1:7 with a maximum of 14 at age three; and 1:8 with a maximum of 16 for four and five-year-olds. These are recommendations, not requirements, and state licensing minimums are generally looser. So the useful question on a tour is not "are you compliant" but "how many adults and how many children are in this room today."

The second is cribs. The federal mandatory standards for full-size baby cribs sit at 16 CFR part 1219, and for non-full-size baby cribs at 16 CFR part 1220. Cribs that child care facilities provide for use have had to comply since December 28, 2012, and the CPSC presumes that cribs manufactured on or after June 28, 2011 comply. For an older crib, a Children's Product Certificate or a test report from a CPSC-accepted third-party lab is the preferred evidence, and "Receipts alone are not an indicator of compliance." Portable cribs and mesh play yards follow a separate standard, 16 CFR part 1221.

## What to skip

- Anything sold as a way to stop separation anxiety. What resolves a drop-off is a goodbye that is short, predictable, and followed through.
- Cameras and monitors aimed at the door. Watching does not shorten the transition; it usually lengthens your own.
- Sneaking out. Leaving without saying goodbye removes the one part of the morning the child can rely on.
- Long, solemn partings with a second round of reassurance.
- Big changes stacked into the same month.

## The bottom line

A child crying at drop-off is normal, it is expected at particular ages, and it is not a verdict on you, on the caregiver, or on the room. The part you control is the leaving: the same short ritual every morning, the same time, the same promise, and no reappearing once you are gone.

So the strategy is not a product. It is a habit: a goodbye that is brief, identical, and true. Build the morning around that, let the child carry their own bag, and ask the pediatrician rather than the internet if nothing is moving.
