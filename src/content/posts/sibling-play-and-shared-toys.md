Here is the scene: two children, one toy, and a parent kneeling on the carpet explaining the idea of sharing to two people who are both crying and neither of whom is listening. The toddler is holding the truck with both hands. The four-year-old is announcing, at volume, that it is his truck, which it is not, and which does not matter, because the truck was never the actual subject.

That is the counter-intuitive part. A fight over a toy at three or four is not evidence of a character flaw. It is the developmental timeline doing what it says on the box. Which means the useful question is not how to make these two share, but what you have set up in the room that makes sharing possible at all.

## What the milestones actually say

The CDC's "Learn the Signs. Act Early." checklists define a milestone as something 75 per cent or more of children can do by a certain age, a majority, not a deadline every child meets on the same day.

At 30 months, the social and emotional milestone is worded like this: "Plays next to other children and sometimes plays with them." The word to hold onto is sometimes. Parallel play, with brief visits into each other's games, is what is expected at two and a half. At three, the milestone becomes "Notices other children and joins them to play."

So a two-year-old who cannot share is not behind, because sharing is not the milestone. A three-year-old can often take turns and can sometimes trade a toy to end an argument; the AAP's page on preschool social development says children of about three can resolve a dispute by taking turns or swapping. The same page adds the line that matters most in practice: especially in the beginning, a parent needs to be there encouraging the cooperation. Sharing is a skill under construction, not a setting you switch on.

And one note for anyone reading this with a knot in their stomach. These checklists are a starting point, not a test. The CDC is explicit that they do not replace validated screening, and the step to take if you are worried about your child's development is a conversation with your child's doctor.

## Work out which toys are shared and which are personal

Most of the daily friction comes from a category error, where a child treats a toy as personal and a parent treats it as communal.

- Shared by design: loose blocks, a big building set, a pretend kitchen or shop, dress-up clothes, art supplies, board games, a box of cars for a road they build together.
- Personal by design: a comfort object, the one doll or truck a child picked out themselves, a gift given to one child by name, and anything a child has been allowed to sleep with.
- The grey zone, which is where the fights usually live: a second copy of a favourite that nobody has decided is whose.

The AAP's guidance for twins, which works for any two children close in age, is to give a new toy a day or two of solo ownership before it enters the shared pool, and to make gifts genuinely different rather than two versions of the same thing. That rule sounds like the opposite of sharing. It is the opposite of sharing, deliberately, because a toy that has never been scarce stops being worth fighting for.

## Set up the room so sharing has somewhere to happen

The AAP's advice for families with two children the same age is not to make them do everything together, but to set up more than one place to play.

- Two small play stations beat one big one, because they let each child keep a game running without a negotiation every ten minutes.
- Expect different interests rather than matching ones. Two children close in age can want completely different things on the same afternoon.
- Keep the shared toys in one obvious place, so the communal pile is a real object rather than a vague idea.

This is where a [large set of building blocks for kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=large+building+blocks+set+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) earns its floor space. Blocks are unusually good shared toys because a child cannot hold the whole supply, so the easiest way to get what you want is to build alongside the other person. That is cooperation by arithmetic rather than by instruction.

## Five things that make cooperation easier than fighting

The AAP's sibling-play suggestions are refreshingly concrete.

- Give them roles instead of a lecture. You be the teacher, I will be the student; you are the cashier, I am the customer. Then swap the roles after a while, which is where most of the learning happens.
- Put a game on the table that they have to win together. A [cooperative board game for kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=cooperative+board+games+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) removes the winner and the loser entirely, which removes the thing they are currently fighting about.
- Take turns on a shared drawing, or build a story one sentence at a time. Neither child can finish it alone, so both have to hold the other's contribution in mind.
- Settle the toy dispute with a timer rather than a verdict. Let the child choose the length of the turn, five minutes or ten, and have the child hand the toy to the other at the end.
- Play games where the children are on one side and you are on the other, and let them win now and then.

A [visual turn-taking timer](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=visual+timer+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) does the emotional work in that fourth point. The timer, not the parent, ends the turn, which means neither child has to accept that a brother or sister was favoured. This is the same logic as the AAP's egg-timer suggestion: a timer is believable in a way a parent's judgement is not.

## When they argue: who steps in, and when to step back

Siblings argue. The AAP's guidance on sibling relationships says the arguments are inevitable and that the subject changes with age, from toys in the early years to privacy later on.

The most useful part for a parent of small children is dosage. Younger children need more frequent intervention, with the same simple rules repeated, and you gradually step back as they get older. So the family rules need to be few and consistent: no hitting, ask before you go into someone else's room, and only kind words. Those three cover most of what is coming.

For the rest, the sequence is worth memorising. Give a small dispute a chance to resolve itself first. If it cannot be resolved, or if anyone's physical or emotional safety is in play, step in calmly and neutrally, and hear both of them out before you decide anything. Being fair out loud matters more than being quick.

## Two children on two tablets is not a solution

The tempting shortcut when the noise gets loud is one screen each. It works, in the sense that the room goes quiet.

What it does not do is the thing you actually wanted: the practice. The AAP's guidance on the power of play is that pretend play is where children try out social roles, learn cooperation, and build negotiation, communication and language skills, and every one of those skills needs a second child who is arguing back. A child alone with a screen is rehearsing nothing. Two children in two separate worlds are not negotiating, not taking turns, and not practising the words they will need next time they both want the same truck.

Quiet is easy to buy. The hour where two children work out how to share one rug is the hour that pays.

## What a mixed-age pair can actually do together

A two-year-old and a five-year-old cannot play the same game, and trying to force it is how the afternoon ends in tears.

The trick with a big gap is to pick activities where the older child's advantage is the point rather than the problem. Building a track or a tower gives the older child a planning job and the younger one a carrying job, and both are genuinely needed. Pretend play works the same way, because a small child can be the customer, the patient or the dog while the older child runs the shop. Drawing, painting and play dough are level, since neither child is trying to win. Physical games where the children are on one team against an adult let a small child contribute without being a liability, and team games are one of the places children practise empathy.

The one thing to protect in a mixed-age pair is the younger child's turn. It is easy to let the older one direct every game. Reverse the roles often, and let the small one hand out the instructions now and then.

## The bottom line

Fighting over a toy at two, three and four years old is expected, on schedule, and not a sign that anything is wrong with your children or with you. The CDC's milestones show the timeline plainly: at 30 months the goal is playing next to another child and sometimes with them, and at three it is noticing other children and joining in. Sharing arrives on the back of that, with an adult standing nearby encouraging it for longer than feels necessary.

The things that help are more about setup than about discipline. Separate shared toys from personal ones, give each new thing a day or two of private ownership, set up two places to play, keep the rules to three short sentences, and put a timer between the children and the argument. Then step in less each year, and let them do the negotiating, because that is the part they are actually learning.
