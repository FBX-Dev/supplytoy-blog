A tub of slime arrives in a party bag, and on the back there is a word that sounds reassuring and means almost nothing. Non-toxic is a marketing phrase, not a limit. The information that would actually help is a single figure, and it is usually absent.

That figure is the boron migration limit. Slime, putty and modelling clay are the products where it applies most often, and it is the one part of this category that most parents have never been told about.

## The two numbers to know

The enforceable limits that exist for boron in toy materials come from the European Union. They are set out in the Toy Safety Directive 2009/48/EC, Annex II, Part III, point 13, as amended, and they come in three bands.

- 300 mg/kg for liquid or sticky toy materials, which is where slime sits.
- 1200 mg/kg for dry, brittle, powder-like or pliable toy materials, which is where putty and modelling clay sit.
- 15000 mg/kg for scraped-off toy material, which is the category used when the material is tested by scraping it off a surface.

Milligrams per kilogram is parts per million, so 300 mg/kg is three hundred parts per million. It is a migration limit, not a total-content figure: the test asks how much boron can come out of the product under defined conditions, because that is closer to what a child actually takes in.

## Why putty sits in the higher band, not the powder band

This is the detail that gets misread. The 1200 mg/kg band is often described as the dry or powder limit, and it is easy to assume that a mouldable tub of putty is covered by the softer 300 mg/kg figure instead.

It is not. The wording of the 1200 mg/kg band covers dry, brittle, powder-like or pliable material. Pliable is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and it is what pulls putty, modelling clay and dough into the 1200 band rather than the 300 band. The 300 band is for the liquid and sticky materials, which is slime and anything with a similar texture.

Get those two the wrong way round and every comparison you make afterwards is wrong.

## What the testing has found in real products

Both bands are exceeded in practice, and not occasionally.

- In July 2018, Which? tested 11 slimes and found 8 of them above the 300 mg/kg limit, with the highest reading at 1400 mg/kg.
- The European Union's Safety Gate alert system recorded alerts in 2025 with measured boron migration as high as 2870 mg/kg.
- A German alert in 2024, logged as A12/02532/24, measured 956 mg/kg.

Three sources, three years, one pattern: these products reach shelves with readings well above the limit, and the highest figures are not from obscure hobby materials.

What that means is not that slime is dangerous in the abstract. It is that the label on the tub is the only evidence you are likely to get, and a large share of the market provides none.

## Where the American legal line actually sits

Here the honest answer is an uncomfortable one. The boron limits above are European. There is no matching federal boron limit for toy materials in the United States, so a product sold in America is not being checked against 300 or 1200 mg/kg by any American authority.

That is a statement about the absence of a rule, not a statement about the product. It does not mean a tub of slime sold in the United States is fine, and it does not mean it is not. It means the specific protection that exists in the European market has no American equivalent, and the buyer retains the whole of the decision.

The other American standard worth naming is ASTM F963, which does cover modelling compounds, but it limits soluble heavy metal migration rather than boron. A clay that complies with the toy standard has not therefore been checked against a boron figure.

## Buying: what a useful label looks like

Since the legal limit is European, the useful signal on a listing is a European one. A tub that states its boron migration figure, or that names the Toy Safety Directive and the EN 71 series it was tested against, is giving you something to compare. A tub that says non-toxic, natural or safe for children, and nothing else, is not. That is not a guarantee of anything. It is a way of sorting the market into makers who have looked at the question and makers who have not.

## Homemade slime and the borax question

Making slime at the kitchen table has one real advantage, which is that you know exactly what went into it, and one real problem, which is that what goes into most recipes is borax.

The useful fact about borax is narrow and specific. It is readily absorbed from the stomach, but it does not easily cross healthy skin. Mixing slime with your hands is not the part to worry about. Eating or swallowing any of the material is.

The handling rules that follow are simple enough.

- An adult supervises and handles the powder, because the powder is the concentrated part.
- Nobody eats, drinks, or snacks at the table where the slime is being made.
- Hands are washed after mixing and after play, before anything goes in a mouth.
- Finished slime is stored in a sealed container with a label, not in an unmarked cup in the fridge.
- If a child does eat some of it, call your local poison control centre and tell them what was in the batch.

If you want the material without the borax question, the commercial alternatives are not automatically better. Choose one from a maker who publishes the number.

## Magnetic putty, briefly

Magnetic putty and magnetic clay belong to a different problem and a different standard. The mandatory federal standard at 16 CFR 1262 covers products styled around mental stimulation and stress relief, and it requires that any loose or separable magnet that fits entirely inside the small parts cylinder has a flux index below 50 kG²mm².

That is the swallowed-magnet hazard rather than the boron hazard, and it is covered properly elsewhere. The point here is only that a tub of putty with magnets in it has two separate things to get right, and the magnet is the sharper of the two.

## Buying choices that reduce the risk

The sorting rule is the same everywhere in this category: buy from the part of the market that publishes numbers, and make the rest of the play session boring in a useful way.

## What we would not buy

- Any slime or putty with no maker name, no age statement and no material information at all.
- Shop-bought slime aimed at very young children, where the taste-it reflex and the product overlap.
- Unlabelled party-bag slime bought in bulk, which is where several of the highest readings in testing have come from.
- Homemade slime kits given to a child to use without adult supervision, because the powder is the part that needs an adult.
- Magnetic putty for any child still putting things in their mouth, regardless of what the boron figure says.

## Playing rules that do most of the work

Most of the risk reduction in this category costs nothing and takes place after the purchase.

- Play at a table rather than on a bed, a carpet or a sofa, where slime gets ground into fabric and then into hands.
- Wash hands after play, every time, and before food.
- Keep the tub sealed and labelled, and throw out anything that has dried out, gone crumbly or grown something.
- Keep slime away from siblings under three, who are the ones most likely to eat it.
- Treat a swallowed amount as a phone call rather than a wait-and-see.

## The bottom line

The number worth looking for on a tub of slime or putty is a boron migration limit. The enforceable figures come from the European Union: 300 mg/kg for liquid or sticky materials such as slime, and 1200 mg/kg for dry, brittle, powder-like or pliable materials, a band that includes putty and modelling clay rather than only powders. Testing has found readings well above both, up to 1400 mg/kg in a 2018 Which? sample of 11 slimes, up to 2870 mg/kg in 2025 Safety Gate alerts, and 956 mg/kg in a 2024 German alert.

The United States has no matching federal boron limit, so the European figure is a comparison point rather than a local guarantee. Buy from makers who publish the number, supervise the homemade version because borax is absorbed from the stomach rather than through skin, and keep the tub off the carpet and away from the toddler.
