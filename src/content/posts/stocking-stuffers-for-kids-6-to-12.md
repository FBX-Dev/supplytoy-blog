By the first week of January the stocking contents have sorted themselves into three groups on a bedroom floor: the things that broke, the things that were eaten or used up, and the two or three objects the child still reaches for. The wrapping is long gone. The plastic film is long gone. What remains is the honest answer to whether the stocking was worth filling.

## Why the stocking is where gift money quietly disappears

A stocking is a container for impulse buys. That is its function and also its problem. Everything inside is small, everything inside is cheap relative to the main present, and none of it gets the same scrutiny as the box under the tree.

For a child between six and twelve this is worse than it was a few years earlier, because the toddler classics have stopped working. A nine-year-old does not want a plastic rattle, a chunky board book, or a squeezy animal, and yet those are exactly the shapes that most stocking-filler aisles are built from. Buy four of them and you have spent real money on four objects that will be in a bin bag by February.

## The one question that sorts the whole stocking

Ask where the object will be at the end of January. Not whether the child likes it on Christmas morning, because almost anything is liked on Christmas morning. Where will it be five weeks later?

That question produces three rough tiers, and once you know which tier a small gift belongs to, the stocking fills itself. It also does something useful for your budget, because it makes the cheap-looking options and the throwaway options look very different from one another.

## Tier one: still in the house at the end of January

These are the small gifts that behave like real tools. They get used, they have a place to live, and they survive the clean-up that happens sometime in the new year.

- A good set of drawing pens or pencils, with a case. A nine-year-old with real pens draws differently from a nine-year-old with a chewed crayon, and [drawing pens that come with their own case](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=drawing+pens+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) get put away rather than lost.
- A card game that fits in a stocking and seats two to four players. These get played on rainy afternoons for years, and [a small card game in a tin](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=card+games+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) is one of the highest-value things you can drop into a stocking.
- A small building or model set that can be built in one sitting. Scale matters more than piece count here. A set that finishes before bedtime gets rebuilt; a set that takes a month gets abandoned in a bag.
- Consumables with a purpose: good tape, a decent notebook, decent glue, or a roll of paper wide enough to draw a map on.
- A torch, a compass, or a small practical item, provided the child is old enough to have somewhere to keep it.

## Tier two: gone by the end of the week, and that is allowed

Some stocking gifts are supposed to disappear, and there is nothing wrong with them. Sweets, stickers, bath things, a treat the household does not normally buy. The only rule is to know you are buying an experience rather than an object, and to not pay object money for it.

If you want a middle category that is both consumable and absorbing, [craft materials a child can ruin without guilt](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=craft+supplies+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) sit between the two tiers. Clay, tape, sticker sheets, origami paper. They get used up, which is the point, and a child who makes something with them has had a real afternoon rather than a sugar rush.

## Tier three: the objects that get thrown away with the wrapping

This is the tier to avoid, and it is the largest one in most shops.

- Novelty keyrings, mini gadgets, and anything that lights up once.
- Figures and vehicles sized to a collection the child does not actually keep.
- Plastic toys that do one thing and then sit in a drawer until someone else throws them out.
- Character merchandise bought because the logo is familiar, with no play attached to it.
- Anything that needs batteries the house does not have on Christmas morning.

## The packaging rule worth repeating every year

Here is the piece of official advice that applies to stockings more than to any other part of Christmas: once the presents are open, throw the plastic packaging away immediately. Films, bags, twist ties, and the small bags that parts arrive in are exactly the shapes that end up near a mouth or a throat. Packaging is not a toy, and it should not spend a week in a stocking or on a bedroom floor.

The same logic explains why the injury figures are not confined to toddlers. Of an estimated 267,100 toy-related emergency department visits in calendar year 2024, 175,400 of them, roughly two thirds, involved children twelve and under. Small objects, small cells, and small parts that work loose all live in a six-to-twelve-year-old's stocking just as easily as in a toddler's.

## The balloon line, and the button cell line

Two specific hazards deserve a sentence each for the younger end of this age band.

Children under eight should not have uninflated balloons within reach, and a balloon that bursts has to be cleared away immediately, because the fragments behave like the flat latex rather than like the inflated balloon. Party packs that include flat balloons are a stocking trap at the six and seven-year-old end of the range.

Button batteries are the other one. They power the small devices that stockings love, and a loose or spare cell is a serious item if it is swallowed. If a gift runs on a button cell, keep the spare out of the stocking, and treat a swallowed battery as an emergency rather than something to watch.

## A word on the stocking itself

Most of the drama in this article lives in the contents, but the container is worth a thought too. A cheap fabric stocking is fine. A hard plastic stocking that stands up on its own and gets stored in a loft for eleven months is also fine. What is not worth buying is a stocking that is really a toy box with a strap, because a house that already owns a toy box does not need a second one that rattles.

## What to skip

Skip anything sized for a child two years younger than the one you are buying for. That is the single most common stocking mistake, and it is why a ten-year-old's stocking ends up full of objects that read as baby toys. Then skip the rest of the tier-three list above, and accept that a slightly smaller stocking with better contents beats a big one filled with noise.

## The bottom line

Fill a stocking for a child between six and twelve with things that have a place to live, and accept that part of it is meant to vanish. Good pens, a card game, one small buildable set, and a few consumables will still be around in February. Novelty and packaging will not. Clear the plastic film and any balloon fragments on the same morning they appear, because that is the hour they are most likely to be picked up, and check that any spare battery has a home that is not the stocking.
