A pull toy with a long string is one of the oldest designs in the catalog, and it is a genuinely good toy on a hard floor in a living room with an adult sitting there. The same toy, dropped into a crib, is a different object entirely. Same product, different room, different level of danger — and the room is usually what determines it.

Cords and strings do not get the attention that small parts and batteries get. They should. Strangulation is quiet, it can happen in a very short window, and the mechanism is almost always a loop.

## How the mechanism works

A strangulation hazard requires two things: something that can encircle a neck, and something that can tighten or catch. A cord, ribbon, strap, or elastic band can supply both. A loop that slips over a head and then tightens under the weight of a body is the classic pattern, and it is why cords that dangle from a standing height are treated so seriously for young children.

The reason this hazard is underrated is that it does not announce itself. There is no cry and no visible injury in the earliest moments. A child can become entangled and trapped without making a sound, which is why the crib and playpen setting has a stricter rule than the living-room setting.

## Where the risk concentrates

Corded products are not uniformly dangerous. The hazard is concentrated in a few places.

**Crib and playpen toys with cords or dangling elements.** This is the highest-risk category, because a child in a cot can be left unattended, can move but cannot reliably free themselves, and may be wrapped in bedding. Anything with a cord, strap, ribbon, or loop that is reachable from inside a cot deserves to be treated as unsuitable.

**Pacifier clips, teether straps, and clothing attachments.** These are designed to keep an object from falling to the floor, which means they are designed to be attached to a child. A strap long enough to reach around a neck is the risk. The clip is not the problem; the length and the loop are.

**Pull toys and toys on strings.** Fine on a floor with supervision, wrong in a cot or playpen, and wrong when the string is long enough to reach from a standing child's hand to the floor. Coiling or shortening a pull string is a reasonable adjustment; leaving a long free length is not.

**Ribbons, drawstrings, and badges on soft toys.** These are often decorative and often overlooked. A decorative ribbon that can be pulled into a loop is a cord.

**Cords on push-along and ride-on toys**, which are usually fine but become relevant when the child is unsteady on their feet and the cord can tangle around a limb or a neck during a fall.

**Adjacent, not toy, but in the same room:** curtain and blind cords. These are not toys at all, but a room that is safe for a cot is a room where the window cords are anchored out of reach. It is worth mentioning precisely because parents secure the toys and forget the window.

## Length and attachment are the two variables

If you want a single mental model, use two questions rather than a ruler.

First: **can this form a loop big enough to pass over a head?** A free cord end attached at one end only is the dangerous configuration, because it can wrap and then tighten. A cord fixed at both ends, short enough that it cannot be pulled into a loop, is a different object.

Second: **is the attachment point fixed or free?** A strap sewn at both ends to a short tether behaves differently from a clipped strap with a free loop. The clip is convenient and comes off; the loop that results is what to look at.

Where you cannot answer either question confidently, the practical answer is to remove the cord. A toy without its cord is usually still a usable toy.

## The crib and playpen rule

The rule for cots and playpens is simpler than the rule for the rest of the house, and it is worth stating plainly: nothing with a cord, string, ribbon, strap, or loop that a child can reach belongs in a cot or playpen with the child unattended.

That includes mobiles and crib gyms with dangling elements once the child can reach up and grab them, and it includes anything that was marketed as a cot toy. A product being sold for cots is not the same as a product being safe to leave in the cot unsupervised.

For the floor with an adult present, a pull toy is fine. For the cot, the floor version does not transfer.

## What to check at home

Fifteen minutes with a basket and you will find more than you expect.

- **Take everything out of the cot and playpen and look at each item for cords.** Mobiles, clips, straps, decorations, and anything tied or clipped to the rails.
- **Measure by asking the loop question**, not by trusting the packaging. Can it form a loop over a head?
- **Check soft toys for decorative ribbons and tags.** If a ribbon is a pull-and-return loop, cut the cord.
- **Look at pull strings on wheeled toys.** Knot or coil the cord shorter rather than leaving a long free end.
- **Check the child's clothing.** Drawstrings on hoods and waists, and long cords on jackets, are part of the same family of risk.
- **Anchor window blind cords** out of reach anywhere a child sleeps or plays.
- **Re-check after washing.** A laundered plush toy often comes out with its ribbon end loose and its strap longer than before.

## What we would not buy

- **Any cot or playpen toy with a reachable cord, strap, or loop.** Regardless of how it is marketed.
- **Decorative soft toys with long ribbons** intended for a child who sleeps with them.
- **Pull toys with an unshortened long string** in a home with a cot.
- **Pacifier clips with a long or elastic strap**, which stretch and re-form the loop you were avoiding.
- **Headphones, necklaces, and lanyards** for young children, which are cord products with a different label.

## If a child is entangled

If a child is caught in a cord or appears to be struggling, act immediately — remove the cord and seek emergency medical help right away rather than waiting to see whether anything develops. Delayed breathing and airway symptoms after entanglement are the reason this is an emergency call and not an observation. If you are unsure whether something has been swallowed rather than caught, Poison Help at **1-800-222-1222** is free and available 24 hours a day.

## The bottom line

Cords are the hazard parents most consistently underestimate, because the product looks harmless and the danger depends entirely on the room it is used in. The mechanism is a loop that tightens, and the highest-risk setting is a cot or playpen where a child is unattended and cannot free themselves.

Keep corded toys for supervised floor play. Take everything with a cord, strap, or ribbon out of the cot, ask the loop question rather than trusting a label, and anchor window cords out of reach. When in doubt, the cord comes off — and if a child is ever caught, treat it as an emergency rather than something to watch.
