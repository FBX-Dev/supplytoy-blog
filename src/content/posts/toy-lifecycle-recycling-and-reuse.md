The clock on a toy starts the day it comes through the door. Not the day it breaks, and not the day your child outgrows it. From the moment the wrapping comes off, the object is being dropped, chewed, left in the sun and quietly turning into waste, and almost none of the usual conversation about "green toys" is about that part at all.

## The environmental cost lands after the purchase

The Environmental Protection Agency's figures on plastics are from 2018, and the agency states that this is the most recent data it has. In 2018, plastics generation was 35.7 million tons in the United States, which was 12.2 percent of municipal solid waste generation. Three million tons of that plastic was recycled, an 8.7 percent recycling rate in 2018. Another 5.6 million tons was combusted, which was 16.3 percent of all municipal solid waste combusted with energy recovery that year, and 27 million tons went to landfill, 18.5 percent of all the waste landfilled. Containers and packaging were the largest plastic category, at over 14.5 million tons in 2018.

One number you will not find in that report is toys. The EPA does not break out a toy category, so any figure claiming that toys are some percentage of household waste is invented, however confident it sounds. What the report does tell you is the shape of the problem: plastic is a modest share of what we throw away and a very small share of what we recycle.

## The bin a battery toy must never go into

A toy with a lithium-ion battery is not ordinary waste, and this is the part of the lifecycle where a mistake can actually start a fire. The EPA's rule is blunt: lithium-ion batteries and devices containing these batteries should not go in household garbage or recycling bins, and they should be taken to separate recycling or household hazardous waste collection points instead. The reason is mechanical. If the battery or the device around it is disposed of in the trash or placed in the municipal recycling bin with household recyclables, it can be damaged or crushed in transport or by sorting equipment, and that creates a fire hazard. Even used batteries can have enough energy to injure someone or start a fire.

This is not a niche category for a parent. The EPA's own list of products commonly containing lithium-ion batteries includes children's toys, alongside cellphones, laptops and power tools. Before anything leaves the house, tape the terminals with non-conductive tape such as electrical tape, or place each battery or device in a separate plastic bag, or do both. To find somewhere to take it, the EPA points to two locators, Earth911 and Call2Recycle.

Charging and in-use safety is a separate subject with its own rules.

## What "recyclable" is allowed to mean

The word on the box is governed by the Federal Trade Commission's Green Guides, and the rule is stricter than most labels suggest. It is deceptive to misrepresent, directly or by implication, that a product or package is recyclable. An unqualified recyclable claim is only allowed where recycling facilities are available to at least 60 percent of the consumers or communities where the item is sold, the FTC's definition of a substantial majority. Below that threshold, the claim has to carry a qualifier, something like "This product may not be recyclable in your area."

Read the 60 percent figure for what it is. It is the threshold for making an unqualified claim, not a legal requirement that anyone recycle anything.

There is a second test that toys fail more often than bottles do. If any component significantly limits the ability to recycle the item, any recyclable claim would be deceptive. A toy is a mixed-material object by design: a plastic shell, metal springs, a battery, fabric, often a small circuit board. That is why "this toy is recyclable" is far harder to defend than the same claim about a drink bottle.

## The chemicals inside the plastic

The Consumer Product Safety Commission restricts eight phthalates at more than 0.1 percent, or 1000 ppm, in an accessible component part of a children's toy or child care article. DEHP, DBP and BBP are permanently prohibited under section 108(a) of the CPSIA. DINP, DIBP, DPENP, DHEXP and DCHP are prohibited by 16 CFR part 1307, a final rule that took effect on 25 April 2018. What counts as an accessible component part is set out separately, in 16 CFR part 1199.

Manufacturers may use alternative plasticizers that are not on the prohibited list, but that is not the same as a clean bill of health. Manufacturers remain responsible for ensuring that children's products do not contain hazardous substances. Swapping one plasticizer for another does not automatically make a toy safe, and a shell that has since cracked or lost its coating is not the object that was tested.

## Making a toy last longer

The strongest environmental decision here is not a recycling decision at all. It is buying less and keeping what you own in play longer. A toy that survives two children never has to be sorted and never has to be recycled, which means the useful filter at the point of purchase is durability and repairability rather than a green label. Solid joinery, replaceable batteries and parts a child can use in more than one way all extend the same lifespan.

Storage is part of the same question, because a toy nobody can see is a toy nobody plays with, and a toy nobody plays with gets replaced. Clear bins on open shelves keep the inventory visible and make it obvious when the shelf is full. Sorting by type turns tidying into a job a child can do, which is also how a toy gets found again instead of quietly duplicated. ## When a toy is finished

Repair comes before disposal. A loose screw, a wobbly joint, a missing knob or a battery compartment that no longer latches is usually fixable rather than fatal, and a repaired toy never needs to be replaced. A [small precision screwdriver set](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=precision+screwdriver+set+for+small+repairs&tag=cnxixi-20) and the right adhesive cover most of the cases that get a toy thrown out. For the batteries that run the toys worth keeping, moving from disposable alkaline cells to [rechargeable batteries and a charger](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=rechargeable+batteries+and+charger&tag=cnxixi-20) removes the same handful of dead cells from the bin every few weeks.

Whatever cannot be repaired or safely passed on has to be dealt with honestly. The rules for selling or giving away a used product are stricter than most people expect: resellers are not required to test used products, but they still cannot sell or donate products that do not meet the law.

## What we would not buy

- Disposable toys designed around a single afternoon, whether that is a party-favour bag or a novelty that does one trick and then sits in a drawer.
- Bulk cheap plastic toys bought by volume, because the price per item is the reason they get lost, stepped on and thrown away rather than repaired.
- Toys with a sealed-in, non-replaceable battery, which turns a working toy into electronic waste the moment the cell stops holding charge.
- Anything sold mainly on the strength of an environmental adjective. "Eco" and "green" are marketing words, not test results, and the FTC's own rule on recyclable claims is the reason to be sceptical.

## The bottom line

Buy less, buy tougher, repair what you can, and take the battery somewhere separate when the toy is done. Those four habits do more than any label on a box. The plastic figures are from 2018 because that is the most recent data the EPA has, and the recycling rate in them is under one in ten. A battery toy never goes in household rubbish or a kerbside bin, because a crushed pack can start a fire even when it is flat. A recyclable claim means something narrower than it sounds, and a toy is a hard object to make that claim about honestly. None of this is complicated. It is just the part of a toy's life nobody photographs.
