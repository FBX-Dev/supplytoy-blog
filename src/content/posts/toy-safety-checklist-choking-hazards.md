Toy injuries are common, largely preventable, and heavily concentrated in a handful of mechanisms. The most frequent serious risk for children under three is choking on small parts — either from a toy that was too small, or from a toy that came apart.

This checklist covers what age labels mean, how to test a toy yourself, and the failure patterns that are consistently involved.

## What the age label actually means

An age label is primarily a **safety statement**, derived from the size of the smallest part the toy contains and the way it behaves under stress. It is not a statement about what a child can understand or enjoy.

The practical reading is roughly this:

- **0+ / Birth and up.** No small parts, no sharp edges, intended for very limited force.
- **3+.** May contain parts too small for a child under three. This is the significant threshold.
- **6+ / 8+.** Contains small parts *and* components that require judgement — functional sharp edges, heating elements, chemistry sets, projectiles.
- **Not suitable for children under 36 months.** Standard phrasing on anything passing through the small-parts threshold.

Two consequences follow, and both are routinely misunderstood.

**A label of "3+" does not mean a two-year-old cannot play with it. It means the toy was not assessed for a two-year-old.** If you choose to allow it, that is your risk assessment, and it requires active supervision rather than a one-off judgement.

**A toy with no age label at all is not automatically unsafe, but it has not been assessed.** Toys from markets, unregulated online sellers, and handmade sources may not have been tested at all.

## The small-parts test

The relevant threshold is a cylinder approximately 32 millimetres in diameter and 57 millimetres deep — roughly the size of a small toilet roll tube.

**Anything that fits entirely inside it is a choking hazard for a child under three.** Not "looks small". Fits entirely inside, with room to spare.

If you do not have a small-parts tester, a toilet roll tube is a reasonable approximation and better than nothing. Bear in mind that the cylinder is a proxy for the width of a young child's airway, not a guarantee.

Test this at home on: removable parts, caps and lids, wheels and axles, toy food items, marbles and balls, buttons on soft toys, and anything that falls off a larger toy.

## Where small parts actually come from

The toy that arrives off the shelf intact is rarely the problem. Most choking incidents involve a toy that came apart.

**Screw-fixed parts that loosen.** Wheels, handles, and battery covers. Check tightness on a new toy, and check again monthly on anything used hard.

**Press-fit parts held by friction.** Plastic wheels on axles, removable eyes and noses on soft toys, snap-together components. These loosen with use — testing on the day of purchase does not predict the state of the toy in three months.

**Glued and stitched attachments.** Heads on soft toys, decorative parts on plastic play sets, glued trim on wooden pieces. A favourite failure mode, and slow — it starts with a loose corner.

**Long-fibre plush.** Pile that pulls out in tufts can be swallowed or inhaled, and is a distinct concern from small hard parts.

**Magnets.** Small high-strength magnets are a specific and severe hazard if more than one is swallowed, because they can attract each other across loops of intestine. Any toy with small magnets that can come loose should be retired immediately, not repaired.

**Button and coin batteries.** If swallowed, these can cause serious chemical burns within hours and are a genuine emergency. Battery compartments must be screw-secured, not taped or press-fit.

## A practical monthly check

Five minutes, once a month, on the toys in active rotation:

1. **Pull on every removable part.** Wheels, handles, eyes, caps, lids. Anything that comes off in your hand is a part your child can get off.
2. **Twist and flex plastic components.** Listen for cracking. A hairline crack in a plastic shell is the start of a sharp edge and a loose interior part.
3. **Run a finger along every edge and seam.** Wood should be smooth with no lifting grain. Plastic should have no flash or burrs.
4. **Inspect seams on soft toys.** A stitch line that is opening is the beginning of an internal part becoming external.
5. **Check the small-parts cylinder on anything questionable.**
6. **Check battery compartments.** The screw should be tight and the cover flush.

Anything that fails: retire it. A repaired toy reliably fails again, and the second failure is usually less visible than the first.

## Other risks that deserve attention

### Strangulation

Cords, ribbons, drawstrings, and loops. Anything longer than about 22 centimetres can form a loop around a neck, and toys with cords should not be left with a child unattended in a cot or pram. This is the reason cot toys with dangling cords are a poor idea.

### Projectiles and blunt-force

Darts, arrows, slingshots, and hard balls. These are 6+ for a reason: they require the child to understand that direction matters and that other people are in front of them. A dense foam ball is a reasonable compromise at younger ages.

### Loud toys

The concern here is noise close to the ear rather than general volume. A toy held against the head at 100 decibels is a hearing risk. Test by holding it at arm's length — if it is uncomfortably loud to you at that distance, it is too loud for a child.

### Ride-ons and falls

Discussed at greater length elsewhere, but the essentials: age-appropriate sizing, a correctly fitted helmet from the point a child moves under their own power, supervision near steps and water, and monthly checks of axle nuts.

### Heated and electrical toys

No toy with a heating element, a mains plug, or a heating wire belongs with a child under eight. Batteries are the safer option, and battery compartments must be secured.

## Second-hand and hand-me-down toys

Hand-me-downs are a sensible way to own toys, with three checks.

**Check for recall.** Regulations change, and older toys may predate current standards. Recall information is published publicly and is worth a search before handing anything on.

**Check for completeness and condition.** Missing parts, cracked housings, loose joints. A toy that is nearly intact is a toy that is nearly safe.

**Check for age-appropriate paint.** Very old painted wooden toys may use finishes that would not meet current standards, and old painted metal can flake. If the paint is lifting, do not use it.

## Storage and the multi-age household

The most common serious incident in a home with more than one child involves a younger child and an older child's toy. Verbal rules do not prevent this.

Physical separation is what works: older children's small-part toys stored above the younger child's reach in closed containers, and play with small parts happening at a table rather than on the floor. A toy the older child can reach and the younger cannot is the actual safeguard.

## If something is swallowed

If you suspect a child has swallowed or inhaled a toy, a part, or a battery, seek medical help immediately. Do not wait for symptoms, and do not attempt to induce vomiting. Button batteries and magnets are time-critical.

## The bottom line

Read the age label as a safety rating. Keep a small-parts cylinder and test anything questionable — the test is "does it fit inside entirely". Pull on removable parts monthly, and retire anything that fails rather than repairing it.

Separate older and younger children's toys physically. And treat button batteries and small magnets as immediate medical emergencies rather than as things to watch.
