Ask a parent what to buy a ten-year-old for a birthday and you get the same three answers every year: money, a gift card, or a shrug. The shrug is the honest one. At this age the useful present is rarely a toy, and the useful conversation is rarely about the present.

Ten to twelve is the back half of middle childhood. What is growing in that window is not a taste for better toys. It is the machinery of planning, waiting, and aiming at something you picked yourself. A toy is one of the cheapest ways to practise those three, if you treat it as practice and not as the point.

## Three stages, and this one is the middle

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau laid out a developmental map in Building Blocks to Help Youth Achieve Financial Capability, published in September 2016. It splits financial capability into three stages: early childhood, ages 3 to 5, where the work is executive function; middle childhood, ages 6 to 12, where the work is financial habits and norms; and adolescence and young adulthood, ages 13 to 21, where the work shifts to financial knowledge and decision-making.

That decides how you spend the next two years. A ten- to twelve-year-old is in the second stage, not the third. The target is not product knowledge, and not a clever answer about interest rates. The target is habit and normal: that money gets set aside, that purchases get compared, that a plan survives contact with a Saturday. The CFPB adds that children at this stage pick up habits largely by watching parents and peers, and that those habits harden into shortcuts they will use long after they forget the source.

## What the middle childhood milestones actually claim

Six of the CFPB's milestones for ages 6 to 12 are worth putting in front of you word for word. By the end of this stage, at best, a child "Shows the ability to plan ahead and delay gratification." He "Shows future orientation." He "Has begun to develop a positive attitude toward planning, saving, frugality, and self-control." He "Can make spending and saving decisions aligned with his or her goals and values." He "Understands core basic financial concepts and processes." And he "Has successfully managed money or other resources to reach his or her own goals."

First, that is what a child can do at his best, not what every child at this age does. The CFPB is explicit that this is an aspirational profile rather than a developmental norm, so a twelve-year-old who spends everything the day it lands is not a diagnosis.

Second, those milestones run across 6 to 12 as one block, and your child is in the back half of it. The early half is about establishing the habit at all. The late half is about using the habit to reach something the child chose. "Has successfully managed money or other resources to reach his or her own goals" describes a ten- to twelve-year-old, and it is the line that makes a toy useful rather than decorative.

## The six money conversations, and where a toy fits

The CFPB's Money as You Grow material for ages 6 to 12 offers six topics, each with a line you can say out loud. Earning: "You can earn money through an allowance or by doing jobs for the family or others." Saving: "You can start a habit of putting money aside for things you want." Planning: "You need to make choices about how to spend your money." Shopping: "Try shopping around and comparing prices and features before you buy." Borrowing: "Taking out a loan means you pay back what you borrow, plus more—because of charges called interest." Protecting: "You need to keep important personal information private."

Four of those six run straight through a toy without anyone calling it a lesson. The advice that makes them stick is the CFPB's other suggestion: think out loud. Say the decision out loud as you make it. Why you are waiting until next month for the better version, what you gave up to afford the other thing. A child who only sees the purchase learns that money appears and objects get bought. A child who hears the reasoning learns it.

## One wanted thing, tracked

The useful version of this is not a jar of loose coins. It is a specific object with a name, a number, and a place to watch it move.

Pick the thing the child actually wants, a particular set, a bike part, a piece of equipment for a hobby he chose, and write it down with him. Then give him something he can see. [A savings jar he can open and count](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=savings+jar+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) does this better than an app, because the physical wait is the point. Add [a notebook he keeps his own numbers in](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=kids+notebook+journal&tag=cnxixi-20) if he likes tracking for its own sake.

Two rules keep this from becoming a chore. The goal has to be his, because a target you picked is one he can drop without losing anything. And the timeline needs a middle, so weeks, not days.

## Two similar toys, one decision

Comparison shopping is the easiest of the six to run with a toy, and it is worth doing, because telling two similar products apart is the skill adults get wrong most often.

Put two candidates side by side and make the child argue it. Which one does more of what you actually want? Which costs more, and what do you lose by taking the cheaper option? Then let him live with the answer. A child who talked himself into the cheaper option and was right learned more than one who was handed the better thing.

The same exercise works on anything with a long build. [A building set that runs for weeks](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=building+sets+for+kids+9+to+12&tag=cnxixi-20) is a good candidate, because piece count, instruction stages and storage are all things a child can compare before buying, and because the set either gets finished or it does not. [A family board game](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=family+board+games&tag=cnxixi-20) does a different job: it puts a fixed budget of play against a group and makes losing cheap.

## The old toys are the planning lesson

The other half of the money conversation happens on the way out. Every child at this age has a shelf of things he has outgrown, and that shelf is the best planning and protecting exercise in the house.

Selling is planning: photograph it, describe it honestly, decide what it is worth, decide what the money is for, then let go. Give the child the job rather than doing it for him. Protecting is the other half, and the CFPB line "You need to keep important personal information private" stays abstract until a child is filling in a listing and has to understand what should never be typed into one.

## What is changing in the child

Money is only half of what is going on. The CDC's parenting guidance for middle childhood, 9 to 11 years, describes the other half: growing independence from the family, a rising interest in friends, and peer pressure that "can become strong during this time." Friendships get more complex. Awareness of pubertal body changes increases, and body image and eating problems sometimes begin in this window. Attention span lengthens, and children get better at seeing someone else's view.

That explains a lot of the shopping behaviour you will see. Peer pressure is why the wanted thing suddenly has to be the version everyone else has. The longer attention span is why a project spanning several weeks becomes possible. The improved perspective-taking is why an honest conversation about why you are not buying it can land.

The CDC's advice for this stage is short. "Talk with your child about saving and spending money wisely." And "Help your child set his own goals," which is this whole article in five words. On praise, the CDC suggests telling the child "you must be proud of yourself" rather than "I'm proud of you," so the standard ends up inside him rather than in your approval.

## Safety, briefly

None of this is a reason to loosen the ordinary rules. A child under 12 still belongs in the back seat, per the CDC, because it is safer there, and motor vehicle crashes remain the most common cause of unintentional injury death for children this age. Helmets stay on for bikes, scooters, skateboards and skates, and get replaced after an impact. If the child is home alone after school, the rules need writing down.

## What we would not buy

- A plastic electronic toy that lights up for two weeks and then lives in a drawer. It teaches a child that new things stop being interesting.
- A screen-dependent product whose app will be discontinued before the child is finished with it.
- A toy sized for a much younger child. It reads as being talked down to, and he will not say so.
- Anything with a subscription the child cannot cancel himself.

## The bottom line

Three questions are enough for almost any purchase at this age. Will it still be in use in two years? Does it connect to something the child wants to achieve for himself? And is the only evidence that it is good for him the word on the box?

If the answers are yes, yes and no, buy it, and make it the object of a plan rather than a surprise. The CFPB's own framing is the honest one: those milestones are what a child can do at his best, not a standard he owes you. The job is not to produce a ten-year-old who manages money well. It is to make planning, waiting and choosing normal enough in your house that he does it without thinking, a few years from now.
