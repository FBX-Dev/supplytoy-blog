The week a child starts walking, the toys change. Not because you buy new ones, but because the old ones start getting carried. A baby who could once reach only what sat within arm's length now crosses a room with a stuffed animal under one arm and a plastic cup in the other hand. The toys that survive that trip are the ones that matter.

That is the real shift of this age band. It is not that children suddenly need harder toys. It is that their hands are free, their range has tripled, and the whole room is now in scope.

## What actually changes between twelve and eighteen months

Six things, roughly in this order.

1. Walking, first with arms out for balance and then with both hands full.
2. Dumping and filling, deliberately and repeatedly, with no interest in the result.
3. Following a simple one-step instruction, especially when it comes with a gesture.
4. Using objects the way they are meant to be used, such as a cup to the mouth or a brush to the hair.
5. Pointing to share attention with you, rather than only to demand something.
6. A first word or two beyond the names of the parents.

A toy that does not touch one of those six is a toy for a different stage of childhood.

## What the CDC milestones say, and what they do not

In 2022 the CDC revised its developmental milestone checklists, and two changes matter for parents shopping for toys.

First, the milestones are now written as what most children can do by a given age, meaning at least 75 percent of children, rather than what the average child does. A milestone is a floor, not a race.

Second, the revision added checklists at 15 months and 30 months, which had previously been missing. The 15-month list is the one that overlaps this age band, and it reads more like a description of a person than a checklist.

The 15-month items include copying what other children are doing, such as taking a toy out of a container; showing you something they like; clapping when excited; hugging a doll; showing you affection; saying at least one word other than a parent name; looking at a familiar object when you name it; following an instruction that combines a gesture with words; pointing to ask for something; using objects correctly, such as a phone, a cup, or a book; stacking two blocks; walking a few steps on their own; and feeding themselves with their fingers.

One caveat the CDC states clearly: these checklists are a monitoring tool, not a screening or diagnostic tool. If something seems off, the guidance is not to wait and see. Ask for a developmental evaluation and an early intervention referral at the same time.

## Turning the 15-month list into a shopping list

### Something to stack exactly two of

"Stacks two blocks" is on the list because it requires fine motor control plus the abstract idea of putting one thing on top of another. You do not need a tower set for this. You need pieces large enough to grip easily, flat enough to balance, and heavy enough to stay put when a small hand brushes them. Look at [large wooden blocks for toddlers](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=large+wooden+blocks+toddlers&tag=cnxixi-20) and check the piece sizes against the small-parts gauge before buying.

### Something to push, and something to carry

Two different toys, often confused. A push toy gives a new walker something to lean on while moving forward, which means stability and weight matter more than features. A carry toy is whatever the child chooses to haul around, and it should be light enough not to tip them over. A push wagon does both jobs if the handle height is right.

### A container, and something to put in it

Filling and emptying is the dominant activity of this half-year, and it costs almost nothing to support. A basket, a bucket, or a box plus a handful of safe objects is a complete toy. Resist the urge to buy a dedicated "sorting" gadget for this; the container is the toy.

### One shape sorter, not three

Shape sorters work when there is exactly one to master. Start with the circle, which fits in any orientation, and let the square and triangle arrive later. A wooden sorter with large painted shapes is easier for small hands than a plastic box with tight slots, but either works if the pieces are big and there are only a few of them.

### Objects that pretend

A cup, a spoon, a phone, a hairbrush. At fifteen months a child begins using objects the way they are meant to be used, and the toys that support this are small, light, and realistic rather than elaborate. This is the seed of pretend play, and it grows for the next two years.

### Books they can carry and point at

A book at this age is a physical object with pages to turn, and it is also the best available tool for the milestone about looking at a familiar object when it is named. Books with photographs of real objects rather than illustrations do this work better. See [board books with photos of real things](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=board+books+real+photos+toddlers&tag=cnxixi-20) for the format.

## What to skip at this age

- Sit-in walkers with wheels. They move faster than a child can control, and an elevated seat teaches a gait that does not transfer to walking.
- Toys with long cords, ribbons, or loops. Anything that can wrap around a neck should not be left with a toddler unattended.
- Anything with a screen. National guidance advises no screen media under 18 months other than video calls, and that is the simplest rule to keep.
- Battery-heavy "learning" toys with a dozen buttons. At this age one button that does something predictable teaches more than six that do not.
- Small-part sets aimed at older children. They end up in the same toy box and then in a mouth.

## Three safety checks that matter most now

Walking widens the search radius, so the safety checks change too.

- Choking. Test anything removable against a small-parts cylinder. Under three years old, any piece that fits entirely inside is a hazard, no matter what the box says.
- Button batteries. Insist on a compartment that needs a tool, or at least two independent and simultaneous hand movements, to open. Official warnings state that a swallowed coin battery can cause internal chemical burns in as little as two hours. If you suspect a swallowing, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 or the National Battery Ingestion Hotline at 800-498-8666, and seek medical care.
- Magnets. Two small swallowed magnets can attract each other through body tissue and cause serious internal injury. If you suspect a child has swallowed magnets, treat it as an emergency rather than a wait-and-see.

## When to ask for an evaluation

The CDC's red flags for this period are specific: no babbling by 12 months, no gestures such as pointing or waving by 12 months, no response to their name, and no single words by 16 months.

One more, which applies at any age: a loss of a skill the child already had. If a child who was walking stops walking, or a child who had words stops using them, seek medical help immediately rather than monitoring it.

Two notes that come up constantly. Formal developmental screening is recommended at 9, 18, and 30 months, with autism screening at 18 and 24 months. And in bilingual households, count vocabulary across both languages together. Learning two languages does not cause a language delay, and measuring only one of them will make a typical child look behind.

## The bottom line

For twelve to eighteen months, buy less than you think you need: blocks big enough to stack, something to push that will not tip, a container and a handful of safe objects to put in it, one shape sorter, a few small realistic pretend objects, and board books with photographs. If you want a single larger purchase, look at a [sturdy push toy for new walkers](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=push+toy+walker+toddlers&tag=cnxixi-20) and check the handle height against your child before you commit.

The toys that get used in this window are the ones that fit in a hand that is also holding something else.
