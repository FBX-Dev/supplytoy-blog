The invitation arrives in the school bag: a fifth birthday party at a trampoline park, and a note saying younger siblings are welcome to jump. Your child is four. The venue has a net around every trampoline, a smiling waiver, and a wall of grip socks. It looks about as safe as a soft play centre, and it is not.

This is not a piece about a rare accident. Trampolines and bounce houses are a large, ordinary, well-documented source of childhood injury, and the professional guidance on preschool children is unusually direct.

## The official position is clearer than most parents expect

The American Academy of Pediatrics strongly recommends against children younger than six years jumping on any trampolines. Not with supervision, not with a net, not one at a time. The age line is six, and it is a threshold rather than a suggestion.

The same body goes further: it advises against home trampolines at all ages. That is worth reading twice, because it means the question of "what age can we get one" does not have a comfortable answer even for an eight-year-old.

## What the injury numbers say

The scale is the reason the guidance is so blunt.

- The Consumer Product Safety Commission records more than 100,000 trampoline-related injuries going to emergency departments every year.
- A 2020 meta-analysis found that people injured at trampoline parks were twice as likely to need surgery as those injured on home trampolines.
- The same analysis found that people injured on full-size trampolines were six times as likely to need surgery as those injured on mini trampolines.
- A 2023 study in Pediatrics found that 11% of injuries at trampoline parks were major injuries, at a rate of 1.14 injuries per 1,000 trampoline hours.

Two things stand out for a parent of a four-year-old. The first is the surgery rate, which tells you these are not mostly sprains that resolve with a bag of frozen peas. The second is the park-versus-home comparison, which undercuts the assumption that the commercial venue is the safer version.

## Why the net does not fix it

The net is the reason most parents feel fine about the whole thing, so it is worth being precise about what it does.

The AAP notes that padding and nets may give a false sense of security, and that they do not prevent many injuries. The reason is where the injuries happen. Most occur during the jumping itself, not from falling off the edge. Two children on a mat with different weights become a collision and a bad landing before any adult can react, and the net does nothing about that.

The net stops one specific event: falling out. It does not stop the thing that sends most children to hospital.

## Why the park is not the safer option

Parents often reason that a trampoline park is supervised, professionally maintained and designed for the purpose, so it must be better than a garden trampoline. The data above says the opposite about the seriousness of the injuries.

There is also the arithmetic of the crowd. A park offers many trampolines, many children, and staff whose job is rules rather than one-to-one supervision of a four-year-old. For an age group that is highly mobile and can get into a dangerous position in seconds, a busy park floor is a difficult place to keep a preschooler safe.

## If there is already a trampoline at home

Plenty of households inherit one. If a trampoline is already there, the guidance about risk still stands, but there are ways to make it less bad, and every one of them is about rules rather than equipment.

- Install it at ground level, so there is no fall from a height onto the mat.
- Keep it away from trees, walls, fences and other play areas.
- Allow one person on it at a time, every time, no exceptions for cousins.
- Ban somersaults and flips outright, since the landings are where the serious injuries live.
- Check the pads and the net before each use, and repair or replace anything damaged.
- Store the ladder out of reach when the trampoline is not in use, so small children cannot climb on unsupervised.

None of this makes it safe for a four-year-old. It reduces a risk that the official advice says should not be taken at all.

## Inflatable castles and bouncy houses

The same logic runs through inflatables. They need an adult actively watching, and they need a limit on how many children are inside at once. Crowding is where the collisions and the awkward landings come from, and the children least able to protect themselves in a bouncing pile are the smallest ones in it.

If a venue cannot tell you how many children are allowed inside a castle at a time, that is useful information about the venue.

## The birthday party problem

Now the practical part, because the invitation is already on the fridge.

You can ask the host a few ordinary questions without making it a debate. Is there a separate area for younger children? Are staff supervising the trampolines, or are parents? Is the party grouped by age? Does the venue require an adult to stay with each child? If there is no answer that leaves your four-year-old off the trampolines, the honest option is to go and not jump, or to decline and send a card and a gift.

If the child is old enough to be upset about it, give them the reason plainly: this one is for bigger kids, and the rule is six. Framing it as a rule they will grow into works better than framing it as danger, and it keeps the day about the birthday rather than about what they were not allowed to do.

Do not turn it into a lecture for the host. They made a normal choice, based on the same information most parents have.

## Balloons, the other bounce-adjacent hazard

Balloons are usually in the same room as the cake and the party bags, and they deserve their own paragraph, because the risk here is different and larger.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission states that, of all children's products, balloons are the leading cause of suffocation deaths. The mandatory warning on the packet reads, in part, that children under eight years can choke or suffocate on uninflated or broken balloons, that adult supervision is required, that uninflated balloons must be kept from children, and that broken balloons should be discarded at once.

So the age line is eight, not four. Under eight, a child must not have an uninflated balloon, and any balloon that bursts has to be cleared away immediately rather than left on the floor. Fully inflated balloons are a different case: the CPSC does not consider them hazardous to young children. An inflated balloon on a string is fine. The same balloon, deflated or shredded, is not.

The reason is mechanical. A piece of latex presses into the airway and seals it, which makes it extremely hard to clear, and that is why balloons behave differently from other small objects. Round, oval and cylindrical shapes in general are the highest-risk shapes when it comes to choking.

## The insurance detail nobody mentions

One more thing worth knowing before anyone buys a trampoline. Many home insurance policies do not cover injuries related to trampolines. That is a policy detail, not a safety rule, but it tells you how the insurance industry reads the risk.

## What to do instead

None of this leaves a four-year-old with nothing to do, and the substitution is easy.

Falls are the leading cause of injury for preschool children, and this age group is strong, fast and able to find trouble in seconds, which is why constant supervision stays necessary even in a safe playground. The answer is not to remove movement, it is to keep the movement low to the ground.

- Run, climb and swing at a playground with a proper surface, with an adult in range.
- Kick a ball, chase bubbles, or ride a balance bike on flat ground.
- Spend the energy on [outdoor play equipment built for preschoolers](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=outdoor+toys+for+4+year+olds&tag=cnxixi-20), which keeps everything close to the ground.
- Use a [playground ball](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=playground+balls+for+preschoolers&tag=cnxixi-20) for throwing and kicking games, which burn the same energy with no landing from height.
- Draw on the pavement with [sidewalk chalk](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=sidewalk+chalk+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20), which is a favourite at this age and costs nothing to abandon mid-drawing.

The point is not to wrap a child in cotton wool. It is that the bounce is the one activity where the official advice is this clear, and where a five-minute substitution removes a documented, serious risk.

## The bottom line

The American Academy of Pediatrics strongly recommends against trampoline use for children under six, and advises against home trampolines at every age. More than 100,000 trampoline injuries reach emergency departments each year, the nets mostly prevent the wrong accident, and parks produce more surgical injuries than home trampolines rather than fewer. Balloons carry a separate rule: no uninflated balloon under eight, and clear up the fragments the moment one bursts.

If your four-year-old is invited to bounce, ask the questions, plan for them not to jump, and know the rule is six. Nothing about that conversation needs to be frightening. It only needs to be firm.
