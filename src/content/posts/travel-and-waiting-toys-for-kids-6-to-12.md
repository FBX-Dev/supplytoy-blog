The car is somewhere past the second exit when the question arrives for the eleventh time: how much longer? You packed a bag of toys. The bag is already open, the toys have already been looked at, and there are still three hours of motorway to go.

By six or seven, the thing that used to work quietly stops working. A four-year-old can be handed something new and be absorbed for twenty minutes. A nine-year-old has already decided that being handed a toy is faintly insulting. What this age needs on a long trip is not more stuff. It is a job, a rule, and something to do with their hands that does not involve staring at something close up.

## The thing that actually changes between six and twelve

Six to eight is the band where the CDC describes children who show more independence from parents and family, start to think about the future, pay more attention to friendships and teamwork, and want to be liked and accepted by friends. Read that list again and it turns into a packing list. A child who wants independence does not want to be managed through a journey.

On a trip, the useful question is not which toy will keep them busy. It is which part of this journey can belong to them.

## Why the car breaks the usual rules

Here is the part that catches most parents out. Everything you would normally reach for on a long journey, a tablet, a video player, a book, a puzzle that needs concentration, is exactly what the AAP advises against in a moving car, because close-up attention is one of the things that makes carsickness worse. Their guidance is to keep a child's attention outside the car.

So the quietest child on the sofa is often the queasiest child on the motorway. The screen that buys you silence at home can buy you a very different twenty minutes on a winding road.

## What actually works in a moving car

The AAP's list of practical fixes is short and unglamorous, and it is worth running through before you worry about toys at all.

- Seat the child as far forward in the car as is still safe for their size rather than in the very back row, ideally by a window where they can see out and get some air.
- Feed them small amounts of plain snacks, crackers and pretzels rather than anything rich.
- Keep them drinking water, and skip fizzy drinks and anything with caffeine.
- Point their attention out of the window rather than down into their lap.
- Lean on distraction: singing, I Spy, counting games, anything that keeps their eyes up.
- Keep the car cool and dress them in fewer layers, because being hot makes carsickness worse.
- Where you can, schedule the long drive for a time when the child would normally be asleep.

The AAP also suggests carrying clothes that are easy to change in and out of, plus spare clothes, and keeping plastic bags, wipes and a towel in the car as a matter of habit. On a bad stretch of road the fix is usually the seat, the temperature and the snack, not the entertainment.

If it keeps happening, it is worth knowing that there are medicines that sometimes help, though they can also make a child very drowsy or backfire entirely, and the advice is to talk to your pediatrician before trying any of them.

Games that need no looking down are the real car toys. ## The waiting room, the restaurant and the airport queue

Waiting is a different problem from travelling. Nobody is going to be carsick in a dentist's waiting room, but the same principle applies: the child who is given something to hold is calmer than the child who is given something to watch.

The CDC's advice for this age includes asking a child to help with household tasks such as setting the table, which sounds irrelevant until you move it into a journey. Give a child a real job. Let them carry their own bag, hold the boarding passes, or check the departure board. A task that is genuinely theirs does more for a long wait than a bag of novelties, because it answers the thing a child this age actually wants, which is to be trusted with something.

[Travel organizer bags made for kids](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=travel+organizer+bag+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) work here for the same reason. The point is not the bag. It is that the bag and everything in it are the child's to manage.

## What hands do while they wait

There is a quiet argument for giving this age something small to fiddle with, and it is not distraction for its own sake. Fidgeting is how a lot of children keep the rest of themselves still. A squeeze ball, a twistable, a set of tiny magnetic pieces in a tin, a smooth stone in a pocket. The point is that it lives in a pocket and comes out in queues.

Drawing is the other reliable option, and it scales from six to twelve better than most toys. A child of six draws a house and a dog. A child of twelve draws a comic. Both are passing a wait with a pencil and their own head, and neither needs a flat surface, a charged battery, or an adult's help.

A [sketch pad and pencils kept just for travel](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=drawing+pad+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) and a [small tin of fidget toys](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=fidget+toys+for+kids&tag=cnxixi-20) are the two things we would actually put in a bag for this age. Neither is exciting. Both get used on every trip.

## The screen rule you agree on before you leave

The CDC's guidance for six to eight is to limit screen time to no more than one to two hours a day of quality programming, at home, at school or in after-school care. A long car journey is the single easiest place for that number to quietly disappear, because the screen is the thing that works and everyone is tired.

The fix is not to ban it. It is to decide the rule at home, out loud, before the bags are packed, so the conversation is finished before the road is. The screen comes out after the first two hours, or at the halfway stop, or when it gets dark. A rule agreed in the kitchen on Thursday is easier to hold to at a service station on Saturday than one you invent on the hard shoulder.

## The buckled-in line

This is where the stakes are highest and the advice is clearest, so it is worth saying plainly.

The CDC's stages run like this. Rear-facing until age two to four, or until the child outgrows the seat's height or weight limit, and never in the front seat. Forward-facing after that and until at least age five, in the back seat, with the top tether attached. A booster after that, until the seat belt fits properly on its own, which usually happens when children are nine to twelve. A seat belt fits properly when the lap belt sits across the upper thighs rather than the stomach, and the shoulder belt crosses the centre of the shoulder and chest rather than the neck, the face or off the shoulder.

And the line that does not bend: keep children properly buckled in the back seat until age thirteen. In 2023, 1,019 children aged fourteen and under died in motor vehicle crashes, and 43 per cent of them were not wearing a seat belt. That is the argument for a boring rule, applied every time, including the short trip to the shop.

## What to skip

- Anything marketed as a car tablet or a back-seat DVD player. It is the wrong tool for a moving car.
- Travel toy sets with dozens of loose pieces. They end up under the seat within the hour.
- Anything that needs a flat surface. Tray tables get folded away at the worst possible moment.
- Toys with a speaker and no volume control.
- Screens offered as a reward for good behaviour, which turns the whole journey into a negotiation.
- Novelty bought at the airport in the last twenty minutes before boarding.

## The bottom line

Packing for a six-to-twelve-year-old is not really a toy problem. Take one screen-free car activity that keeps their eyes up, one small thing for their hands in a queue, and one real job that belongs to them. Decide the screen rule before you leave the house, and keep everyone in the back seat until thirteen. Get those few things right and the bag of toys almost stops mattering.
