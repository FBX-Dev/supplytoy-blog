There is a wooden toy in most playrooms that nobody plays with. It is usually a beautifully finished object: smooth, heavy, the sort of thing an adult picks up and turns over before putting back on the shelf. It was also, very often, bought because it was wooden. That is the whole problem with this argument.

This is not a case against wood. It is a case against treating wood as a verdict.

## Why the material debate is usually the wrong debate

Most wooden-versus-plastic conversations compare two toys that were never designed for the same job. A hand-finished wooden puzzle for a two-year-old and a box of interlocking bricks for a six-year-old are not competitors. The comparison that matters is between two toys of the same type, for the same child, used in the same room.

Compared that way, material explains maybe a third of the difference. Design explains the rest: how much each piece weighs, how big the grip is, whether the paint is part of the surface or a layer sitting on top of it, and whether the toy does one thing well or six things badly.

So the useful question is not "wood or plastic." It is "what is this specific toy going to be asked to do, and which version of it does that better."

## Where wood genuinely wins

Wood earns its reputation in a few specific places, and they are worth naming precisely.

It has mass. A wooden block stays where you put it. It stacks without skidding, which is exactly what a two-year-old needs when the tower keeps toppling. Light plastic blocks often move three others when one is knocked.

It gives more information to a small hand. Weight, grain, and a slightly different surface temperature all tell a child something about what they are holding. That is not a mystical claim about natural materials; it is simply more sensory variety per toy.

It wears in rather than out. The corners of a well-made wooden toy round off with use. They do not split along a seam the way moulded plastic can, and they do not become brittle in a cold room.

It has no battery compartment. For a toy aimed at a child under three, that is not a small advantage. Anything containing a coin cell adds a hazard class that wood simply does not have.

## Where plastic genuinely wins

Plastic wins more often than wood-friendly marketing tends to admit, particularly in four situations.

Water. Wood swells, warps, and grows things. Anything that lives in the bath, the water table, or the sandpit should be plastic, silicone, or rubber.

Interlocking and precision. Plastic holds a tolerance of a fraction of a millimetre across millions of pieces, which is why nearly every construction system a child plays with past age five is plastic.

Light weight and large shapes. A three-year-old who wants to build something big cannot lift sixty heavy wooden blocks, but can drag a box of hollow plastic ones across the floor. Light is not always cheap. Sometimes light is the entire point.

Cleaning. A plastic toy can be washed in hot soapy water, and many can go through a dishwasher. Most wooden toys survive neither, and the ones that do are the exception rather than the rule.

## The part nobody mentions: cheap wood is worse than decent plastic

A wooden toy's quality depends on the same things a plastic toy's does: the joinery, the finish, the size of the parts, and whether anyone thought about how a child would actually use it.

There is a large category of inexpensive wooden toys made from softwood, coated in a thin decorative layer of paint, and assembled with glue that lets go in a hot car. Those toys chip, splinter at the edges, and shed paint into a mouth. A plain, well-moulded plastic toy at a similar price is frequently the better and safer object. If the choice is between bad wood and good plastic, take the plastic without guilt.

The reverse holds too. Expensive plastic is not automatically well designed. Pick up a "developmental" toy and ask how much of it is actually a light, a speaker, and one button.

## How to judge a toy regardless of material

Run the same tests on both.

Ask what the child's hands do. If the answer is "press a button and watch," the toy is doing the work and the child is watching it happen.

Pick it up yourself. Is it heavier or lighter than you expected? Weight is a reasonable proxy for whether it will stay put and for how much material is really in the object.

Look at edges, corners, and seams. On wood, anything sharp is unacceptable. On plastic, look for moulding flash, the thin ridge where two halves of a mould met, and for seams that could separate with chewing.

Test anything removable. A small-parts cylinder is the reference tool. If a piece fits entirely inside, it is a choking hazard for a child under three, whatever it is made of. A toilet roll tube is a rough substitute at home, but it is more generous than the real gauge.

Check anything that takes a battery. Screw-secured compartments are the standard to insist on; a taped or press-fit cover is not good enough. The reason is not hypothetical: official warnings state that a swallowed coin battery can cause internal chemical burns in as little as two hours.

## Safety rules apply to both materials

The US toy safety standard, ASTM F963-23, is a material-agnostic standard. It sets a lead limit of 90 ppm in paint, restricts eight soluble heavy metals, and limits phthalates. It applies to toys intended for children under 14 whether they are wood, plastic, fabric, or metal.

That means a wooden toy is not exempt from anything. If it is sold as a children's toy in the United States, it is supposed to be tested by a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory, carry a tracking label, and be covered by a Children's Product Certificate. That paperwork is what a safety claim should rest on, not the word "natural" on a product page.

## What to buy in wood

- Blocks, in a set large enough that a child can build something real rather than a two-piece tower.
- Stacking and nesting sets where the pieces are meant to be gripped and compared, not clicked.
- Chunky peg puzzles for children under three, where the weight helps the piece drop into place.
- Play food and play kitchen pieces that will mostly be held, arranged, and carried rather than washed.
- Simple figures and small-world pieces that will be handled for years.

## What to buy in plastic

- Anything that gets wet, sandy, or left outside.
- Interlocking construction systems.
- Ride-ons, push-alongs, and anything that has to take a child's weight and flex.
- Bath toys, provided they are the kind that can genuinely be drained and dried.
- Replacements for the wooden thing that broke.

## What we would not buy

The following are worth walking past in either material.

- Painted wooden toys where the paint layer is lifting at the edges, or that smell strongly of solvent out of the box.
- Novelties that hide small magnets inside a plastic shell. Loose or separable magnets that fit in a small-parts cylinder are precisely the hazard the magnetic toy rules were written about, and a swallowed pair can attract through body tissue and cause serious internal injury.
- Any bottle, cup, or rattle with liquid sealed inside and no way to drain it.
- Large multi-activity plastic units where most of the surface area is frame, sticker, or speaker rather than something a child can operate.
- Anything sold mainly on the strength of an adjective. "Eco," "natural," and "Montessori" are marketing words, not test results.

## A practical way to decide

Buy plastic for water, sand, construction, and anything that has to survive being stood on. Buy wood for stacking, gripping, arranging, and the toys you intend to hand down to a second child. When the toy is for a child under three, prefer the version with fewer parts and no battery.

And when two toys share the same shape, the same size parts, and a similar price, stop deliberating. Take the one your child can actually pick up unaided.

## The bottom line

Wood is often pleasant, occasionally excellent, and not inherently superior. Plastic is often cheap, occasionally excellent, and not inherently bad. Material determines how a toy feels in the hand and what it can endure. Design determines whether the child plays with it twice or for two years.
